Recall Watch

A consumer app that tracks USDA food recalls and tells people what to buy instead. This document turns the approved prototype into something a team can estimate and build.

1. What the product is

Recall Watch answers one question quickly: is the food in my kitchen safe, and if not, what do I buy instead? It is a browse-and-search app first — the user can read the week's recalls without ever creating an account — with an optional watched-pantry layer that turns the feed into a personal alert.

Primary users are home cooks and parents of children with food allergies. Secondary users are restaurant QA staff and institutional buyers, who need the same data with different filters; they are explicitly out of scope for v1.

The hard part, stated plainly

The UI is the easy half. The product's value depends on two data problems that are not solved by design: matching a physical package in someone's freezer to a recall notice, and knowing which substitute product is actually on a nearby shelf. Estimate those first — everything else is scaffolding around them.

2. Scope

In scope for v1

  • Recall feed, ranked by risk class and pantry relevance.
  • Recall detail: reason, affected lots, what-to-do steps, agency reference number.
  • Search with filters (risk class, category, allergen, free text over brand/product/lot).
  • Ranked substitutes in three tiers: same product different brand, nutritionally similar alternative, recipe-level swap.
  • Watched pantry with per-item status: recalled, check lot, clear.
  • Barcode and lot-code scan producing a plain-language verdict.
  • Return locations with refund guidance.
  • Onboarding: what the app does, allergen preferences, notification opt-in.
  • Push notifications for Class I recalls and pantry or allergen matches only.

Out of scope for v1

  • Accounts across devices, sharing a pantry between household members.
  • Restaurant or institutional workflows, bulk lot lookup, compliance reporting.
  • Receipt OCR ingestion (the onboarding button should degrade to barcode-only).
  • Android and web clients.
  • Any nutrition claim beyond what a substitute's own label states.

3. Data sources

USDA FSIS covers meat, poultry, and processed egg products only. Everything else a shopper thinks of as food — produce, dairy, seafood, packaged goods, infant formula — falls to FDA. A user who scans a bag of spinach and gets "no recalls found" from a USDA-only app has been actively misled, so v1 should ingest both feeds even though the app is named for USDA.

Source Covers Notes for the build
FSIS recall API Meat, poultry, egg products Structured fields for class, establishment number, states. Lot identifiers often live in free-text or an attached PDF.
FDA enforcement reports Produce, dairy, packaged goods, formula Published on a lag after the public press release; reconcile both so the app is not last to know.
Product catalog / UPC Barcode to product identity Vendor decision required. Recall notices rarely publish UPCs, so this is a join, not a lookup.
Store inventory or ad data Substitute availability Hardest to source. Degrade honestly to "commonly stocked" when unavailable.

4. The matching problem

A recall names a brand, a product description, an establishment number, and a set of lot or use-by codes. A pantry item, as captured by a phone camera, is a UPC plus whatever the user typed. Joining those two is the core engineering work, and it fails in specific ways worth designing for:

  • Lot codes are unstructured. Formats vary per plant and appear as free text or scanned PDFs. Expect a parsing layer with per-brand rules and a manual review queue.
  • The barcode does not carry the lot. A UPC identifies the product line, not the pack. Scanning alone can only ever produce "this product line is recalled — check your lot code," which is why the scan verdict sheet must ask for a second look rather than declaring safety.
  • Absence is not safety. The app must never say "safe." The vocabulary throughout is no recall found, with the date of last data refresh visible.

Recommended v1 posture: three states only — recalled (confident match on product and lot), check lot (product line matches, lot unknown or unparsed), no recall found. The prototype's third state is labelled "clear," which overpromises; rename it.

5. Substitute ranking

Substitutes are presented in three tiers, each answering a different user need. The ranking within a tier should be explainable in one sentence in the UI — users distrust silent recommendations about food.

  1. Same product, different brand. Match on category and form factor. Exclude any brand with an open recall, and any brand sharing the implicated establishment number — co-packing means a "different brand" can come off the same line.
  2. Nutritionally similar alternative. Match on protein, sodium, and preparation time. State the tradeoff explicitly ("less sodium, no breading") rather than implying equivalence.
  3. Recipe-level swap. A different dinner that fits the same weeknight slot. Cheapest tier to build and the most likely to be useful when the shelf is empty.

Allergen preferences from onboarding are a hard filter on every tier, not a ranking signal. A substitute that violates a stated allergen must never appear, even ranked low.

6. Screens and states

The prototype covers the happy path for eight screens. Each needs the states below before it can ship; none of them are designed yet.

7. Notifications

Onboarding promises alerts for Class I recalls and pantry or allergen matches, and nothing else. That promise is a product constraint: no digests, no engagement pushes, no marketing. Breaking it costs the permission permanently. Alerts must be idempotent — a recall that is later expanded should update rather than re-fire, and a terminated recall should send one clear correction.

8. Trust, accuracy, and liability

Telling someone their food is safe is a legal position, not a UX copy decision. Before launch, counsel should review the verdict language, the disclaimer placement, and the data-freshness commitment. Design implications that follow regardless:

  • Last-refreshed timestamp visible wherever a verdict is given.
  • Never the word "safe"; always "no recall found."
  • Every recall links out to the originating agency notice as the authority.
  • An in-app path to report a mismatch, feeding the manual review queue.

Accessibility is also a safety matter here: risk must never be conveyed by color alone — every class badge carries a text label, and the interface should be verified at largest Dynamic Type with VoiceOver.

9. Open decisions

  1. Do we ingest FDA recalls in v1, or ship USDA-only and accept the coverage gap?
  2. Which product catalog vendor, and what does UPC coverage actually look like for store brands?
  3. Can we get store-level availability at launch, or do substitutes ship without it?
  4. Is the pantry local-only in v1, or does it require accounts and sync?
  5. What refresh interval do we commit to publicly, and what is our alerting SLA against it?
  6. Who staffs the manual lot-parsing review queue, and what is the turnaround target?

10. Recommended sequence

Build the risky half first. A polished client over an unreliable match is worse than no product.

  1. Spike the ingestion and lot parser against six months of historical notices. Measure what fraction of recalls yield machine-readable lot codes. This number decides the product.
  2. Ship browse and search only. No pantry, no scan, no accounts. Read-only, useful, low liability.
  3. Add substitutes starting with tiers one and three, which need no availability data.
  4. Add pantry and scan once matching confidence is measured and the three-state vocabulary holds up in testing.
  5. Add notifications last — they are only trustworthy once matching is.

User-test the current prototype against step two's scope before writing production code: the fastest way to invalidate this plan is to watch five people try to find their own groceries in it.
