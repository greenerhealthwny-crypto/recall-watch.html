# The Four Types of Preventive Controls (Ch 9–12, App 5–6)

*(manual)* marks content confirmed in the OCR'd FSPCA manual; *(std)* marks standard knowledge, to check against current FDA guidance.

## Contents
- §1 Process preventive controls
- §2 Food allergen preventive controls
- §3 Sanitation preventive controls and environmental monitoring
- §4 Supply-chain preventive controls

**Management components for every preventive control** (as appropriate to its nature and role): **monitoring**, **corrective actions or corrections**, **verification** (validation where required) and **records** *(manual)*.

---

## §1 Process preventive controls (Ch 9)

- These are procedures, practices and processes at specific steps, such as cooking, pasteurization, acidification, refrigeration, metal detection and cooling *(manual)*. They are **frequently CCPs** *(manual)*.
- **Parameters and values / critical limits:** maximum or minimum values of a parameter that must be met to control the hazard, e.g. time, temperature, pH, a_w, flow rate, or presence of a functioning metal detector and reject *(manual)*.
  - They must be **science-based and validated**.
  - Set the limit at or beyond a value ("≥ 160 °F"), not as an exact value, because equipment can't hold an exact value *(manual)*.
  - **Operating limits** are tighter targets, e.g. 167 °F against a 165 °F critical limit. Adjusting the process when an operating limit is missed avoids a critical-limit deviation and the corrective action it triggers *(manual)*.
  - **Omelet example:** cook CCP critical limit = **omelet surface temperature ≥ 158 °F (70 °C) instantaneous before transfer to the assembly table**, determined through validation *(manual)*.
- **Monitoring** answers four questions *(manual)*:

| Question | Example (omelet cook step) |
|---|---|
| **What** is monitored? | Omelet surface temperature |
| **How**? | Calibrated thermometer |
| **Frequency**? | Each batch or a defined interval; continuous where possible |
| **Who**? | A trained line employee, recorded on the cook log |

  - Monitoring should give fast results, ideally immediately, sometimes seconds to minutes, or longer only if product is held *(manual)*.
  - Visual monitoring must make clear whether a limit was met. For example, the metal detector is observed as "on, and the reject works", checked at the start, middle and end of each shift *(manual)*.
  - Monitoring employees can be trained to investigate rejects, run calibration checks and act immediately *(manual)*.
- **Corrective actions** (the procedures must be written) must *(manual)*:
  1. Identify and correct the problem with implementing the control.
  2. Reduce the likelihood of recurrence (root cause).
  3. Evaluate all affected food for safety.
  4. Prevent affected food from entering commerce if its safety can't be ensured.
  5. Document it in a corrective action record.
  - Product may be segregated and evaluated, with testing as appropriate.
  - Recurring deviations point to a need for reanalysis.
- **Corrections** are prompt actions for minor, isolated problems, e.g. "employee is instructed to gown properly", or re-cleaning before production. They need less documentation (often none for sanitation corrections) *(manual)*.
- **Verification** *(manual)*:
  - Validation (see `verification-records-recall.md` §1).
  - Verification that monitoring is done, e.g. a supervisor's check measurement.
  - Calibration: the omelet thermometer gets a **daily accuracy check and annual calibration**.
  - **Records review within 7 working days** (cook log, corrective action and verification records).
- **Records:** monitoring logs, corrective action reports, verification records *(manual)*.

## §2 Food allergen preventive controls (Ch 10)

- **Why:** undeclared allergens cause at least a third of FDA food safety recalls, usually from labeling errors *(manual)*. Severe reactions include anaphylaxis *(manual)*.
- **Major food allergens** (US): milk, egg, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, wheat, peanuts, soybeans, plus ingredients containing protein from them *(manual)*. **Add sesame (from 2023)** *(std)*.
- **Allergen cross-contact** is the unintentional incorporation of a food allergen into a food *(manual)*. Note this is distinct from "cross-contamination", which is used for microbes.
- **Allergen controls cover** *(manual/std)*:
  1. **Protecting from cross-contact** during storage, handling and processing:
     - Receiving and identifying allergen-containing ingredients.
     - Segregated or designated storage (e.g. no open containers returned to the cooler; separate designated areas *(manual, omelet example)*).
     - Dedicated or color-coded utensils.
     - **Production scheduling and sequencing** (non-allergen → allergen).
     - Rework controls.
     - Changeover cleaning (validated where possible) and line inspection.
  2. **Labeling controls:** the right label on the right product, with an ingredient statement declaring all allergens.
     - Check incoming label batches against the formulation (by a knowledgeable person).
     - Verify the correct label at labeling or packing. In the omelet example, the **fill-line operator matches the label to the product number** at the start of the run and at each label change.
     - Manage label inventory and destroy obsolete labels.
     - "May contain" precautionary statements are **not** a substitute for controlling cross-contact *(std)*.
- **Monitoring** uses the same what, how, when, who structure, e.g. the label check at each run and changeover. **Corrective action** covers what to do with the product (relabel or hold) plus root cause *(manual)*.
- **Verification:** records review; label audits; allergen swabs or ELISA after cleaning, where appropriate. **Validation is not required** for allergen controls *(manual)*.

## §3 Sanitation preventive controls (Ch 11, App 5–6)

- These are required **only for hazards identified as requiring them** in the hazard analysis, typically:
  - (a) **environmental pathogens** in **ready-to-eat food exposed to the environment** after a kill step and before packaging; and
  - (b) **allergen cross-contact** through shared equipment *(manual)*.
  - Routine GMP sanitation stays a prerequisite program. All food-contact surfaces still need adequate cleaning procedures and frequencies *(manual)*.
- **Scope:** cleanliness of food-contact surfaces, including utensils and equipment; prevention of allergen cross-contact and cross-contamination from insanitary objects and from personnel to food, packaging and food-contact surfaces, and from raw to processed product *(std, per 117.135)*.
- **Written procedures** say what is cleaned, how (chemicals, concentrations, steps), when (frequency) and who *(manual)*. The **basic cleaning sequence** (Appendix 5) is *(std/manual)*:
  1. Dry clean and remove debris.
  2. Pre-rinse.
  3. Wash with detergent.
  4. Rinse.
  5. Inspect.
  6. Sanitize with an approved sanitizer at the label concentration.
  7. Air dry.
- **Monitoring:**
  - Pre-operational inspection.
  - Sanitizer concentration checks.
  - Visual cleanliness.
  - ATP checks are verification tools *(std)*.
  - **Corrections** suffice for issues found before production that don't affect product (re-clean); a **corrective action** is needed if product was affected *(manual)*.
- **Verification: environmental monitoring (EM)** is **required** when the environmental pathogen hazard requires a sanitation control for ready-to-eat food exposed after processing *(manual)*:
  - Many facilities test for ***Listeria* spp.** as an indicator *(manual)*.
  - **Hygienic zoning** (Appendix 6) *(manual/std)*:
    - Zone 1: food-contact surfaces.
    - Zone 2: non-food-contact surfaces close to Zone 1.
    - Zone 3: more remote areas in the processing room (floors, drains).
    - Zone 4: outside the processing room.
  - The program must define locations, number of sites, timing and frequency, the test organism, the lab method, and **corrective actions for positives** *(std)*.
  - Escalating responses run from intensified cleaning and vector swabbing to product hold and testing. **A pathogen detected in Zone 1** usually triggers product evaluation (hold, test or divert) and root-cause investigation *(manual/std)*.
  - Hygienic zoning, traffic control, and separating raw from ready-to-eat areas are prerequisites that may become preventive controls *(manual)*.
- **Validation is not required** for sanitation controls *(manual)*.

## §4 Supply-chain preventive controls (Ch 12)

- **When required:** the **receiving facility's** hazard analysis finds a hazard in a raw material or ingredient that requires a preventive control, and the **supplier** applies that control (a "supply-chain-applied control"), because the receiving facility has no step to control it *(manual)*.
  - Example: E.G. Food Company relies on its supplier's pasteurization (vegetative pathogens) and formulation (*C. botulinum*) for an ingredient, and manages this with a third-party audit *(manual)*.
  - **Not required** if the receiving facility controls the hazard itself, or if a customer downstream controls it (with disclosure and written assurances) *(std)*.
- **Program elements (Subpart G)** *(manual/std)*:
  1. Use **approved suppliers**, approved by the receiving facility before receiving, and documented.
  2. **Written procedures for receiving** raw materials from those suppliers.
     - Temporary use of unapproved suppliers is allowed only with verification before use *(std)*.
  3. Determine **appropriate supplier verification activities**, considering the hazard, where it's controlled, and the supplier's performance (procedures, processes, practices, FDA compliance, warning letters).
  4. Conduct and document the activities:
     - **onsite audits**,
     - **sampling and testing** of the raw material,
     - **review of the supplier's food safety records**,
     - other appropriate procedures.
  5. Take **corrective action** when a supplier fails.
  6. Keep records.
- **Onsite audit rule:** when the hazard could cause **serious adverse health consequences or death (SAHCODHA)**, conduct an **onsite audit before first use and at least annually** thereafter. The exception is a documented written justification that other activities or less frequent audits give adequate assurance *(manual)*.
  - Audits must be done by a **qualified auditor** using the applicable FDA requirements. They can be third-party *(manual)*.
  - A **supplier's self-audit, or its own review of its records, is not acceptable** verification *(manual)*.
- **Qualified facilities and certain farms as suppliers:** modified requirements apply. For a qualified facility, confirm its status before approval and **annually by 31 December**, and obtain written assurance of compliance at approval and **at least every 2 years** (21 CFR 117.430) *(manual)*.
- **Certificates of analysis (COAs)** can support verification, with their limits understood *(manual)*.
