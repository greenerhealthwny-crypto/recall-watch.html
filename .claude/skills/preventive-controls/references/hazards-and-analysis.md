## §1 Biological hazards (Ch 4, App 4)

- **Types:** bacteria, viruses, parasites (and their toxins).
  - Viruses and parasites can't grow in food.
  - Preventing bacterial growth matters because many illnesses need large numbers *(manual)*.
  - **Infection** = live organisms; **intoxication** = preformed toxin, and some toxins aren't destroyed by heat *(manual)*.
- **Grouping used for hazard analysis** *(manual/std)*:
  - **Vegetative pathogens** (*Salmonella*, pathogenic *E. coli*, *L. monocytogenes*, *Campylobacter*, *Shigella*, *Vibrio*, *Yersinia*, *S. aureus*). Killed by cooking or pasteurization; controlled by kill steps, temperature and formulation.
  - **Spore-forming pathogens** (*C. botulinum*, *C. perfringens*, *B. cereus*). Spores survive cooking, so control relies on formulation (pH, a_w), refrigeration, cooling, or a botulinum process.
  - **Toxin formers:**
    - *S. aureus*: its toxin is very heat stable and survives canning, but the bacterium dies with mild heat. Toxin formation needs extensive growth and is favoured by oxygen. The limits for toxin are narrower than for growth, and it competes poorly *(manual)*.
    - *B. cereus* emetic toxin; *C. botulinum* neurotoxin.
  - **Environmental pathogens:** pathogens able to survive and persist in the manufacturing environment and contaminate food that gets no later lethal treatment. The rule's examples are ***L. monocytogenes* and *Salmonella* spp.**, and the definition **excludes spores of sporeformers** *(manual)*. These drive sanitation controls and environmental monitoring for exposed ready-to-eat foods.
  - **Viruses** (norovirus, hepatitis A) and **parasites**: controlled by worker health and hygiene, and supplier controls (water, produce, shellfish).
- **Growth factors:** the manual's FAT TOM framework of food, acidity (pH), time, temperature, oxygen and moisture (a_w) *(std)*. Formulation (pH, a_w, preservatives) can favour some pathogens and exclude others *(manual)*. Key limits *(std)*:
  - pH ≤ 4.6 stops *C. botulinum*.
  - a_w ≤ 0.85 stops most pathogens; ≤ 0.94 stops *C. botulinum* group I.
  - *L. monocytogenes* and nonproteolytic *C. botulinum* grow at refrigeration temperatures.
  - The danger zone is about 41–135 °F (5–57 °C), per the FDA Food Code.
- **Time/temperature example in the omelet plan:** hydrated batter must not exceed **50 °F (10 °C) for more than 12 h**, to prevent *S. aureus* growth and toxin *(manual)*.
- **Reduced oxygen packaging** can support *C. botulinum* or *L. monocytogenes* in some foods, so consider these hazards specifically *(manual)*.
- **Sensitive ingredients** have a history of association with a hazard *(manual)*:
  - Eggs and *Salmonella* (in the omelet example).
  - Flour and *E. coli*/*Salmonella* (ready-to-eat cookie dough).
  - Nuts, spices, dairy powders, produce, peanut products.

## §2 Chemical, physical and economically motivated hazards (Ch 5)

- **Chemical hazards** *(manual/std)*:
  - **Food allergens:** the most important chemical hazard by recalls. The **major food allergens ("Big 8")** in the manual are milk, egg, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, wheat, peanuts and soybeans *(manual)*. **Sesame** was added as the 9th in 2023 *(std)*. Severe reactions include anaphylaxis, treated with epinephrine *(manual)*.
  - Pesticide residues (legal but limited), drug residues, heavy metals.
  - Natural toxins: mycotoxins (aflatoxin, patulin, etc.), scombrotoxin/**histamine** in fish (from microbial conversion of histidine), shellfish toxins, ciguatera *(manual)*.
  - Decomposition.
  - Unapproved food or color additives.
  - Food intolerance substances (e.g. sulfites).
  - Process chemicals: cleaning and sanitizing agents, lubricants, boiler chemicals.
  - Packaging migrants, e.g. inks *(manual)*.
  - **Radiological hazards** are included in "chemical" *(manual)*.
- **Physical hazards:** hard or sharp foreign objects, such as metal, glass, hard plastic, stone or bone, that can cause choking, injury or other harm.
  - Sources are equipment wear, ingredients, packaging and the facility *(manual)*.
  - Guard glass against breakage *(manual)*.
  - FDA's health hazard evaluation guidance treats hard or sharp objects 7–25 mm long as a potential hazard *(std; not legible in the manual)*.
- **Economically motivated adulteration (EMA):** intentional substitution or addition for economic gain that creates a food safety hazard.
  - Examples: lead-containing dyes in spices to enhance colour (cited in the manual); melamine in protein ingredients *(std)*.
  - Usually controlled through the **supply-chain program** and supplier knowledge *(manual)*.
- **Not in scope:** intentional adulteration aimed at harming people is covered by the separate Food Defense / Intentional Adulteration rule *(std)*.

## §3 Preliminary steps and resources (Ch 6–7)

1. **Assemble the food safety team.** It needs a PCQI, plus production, sanitation, maintenance, QA and outside experts as needed *(manual)*.
2. **Describe the product and its distribution:**
   - Name, ingredients and formulation.
   - Characteristics affecting safety: pH, a_w, preservatives.
   - Packaging (including reduced oxygen packaging).
   - Storage and distribution (refrigerated, frozen, shelf stable).
   - Shelf life and labeling instructions.
   - Intended use and **foreseeable use**, e.g. people eating "cook before eating" dough raw *(manual)*.
   - Intended consumers, flagging vulnerable populations (infants, elderly, immunocompromised) *(manual)*.
3. **Draw the flow diagram.** Include receiving of every ingredient and packaging material, rework, and water or air used as ingredients *(manual)*.
4. **Write the process description** for each step *(manual)*.
5. **Verify the flow diagram onsite** by walking the line, e.g. to catch new equipment or rework routes *(manual)*.

**Resources (Ch 7)** *(manual)*:
- FDA's *Hazards and Controls Guides* (draft hazard analysis guidance), including tables of "potential process-related hazards". Use them but don't treat them as exhaustive; do an onsite assessment too.
- CDC outbreak data and FDA/USDA recall lists (Class I, II, III).
- Scientific literature (e.g. via Google Scholar).
- **Predictive models:** USDA Pathogen Modeling Program, ComBase. They may not exactly reflect your food.
- Codex codes of practice, trade associations, extension services, process authorities and testing labs.
- **FSPCA Technical Assistance Network** and model plans on the FSPCA website.
- Your own data: test results, environmental monitoring, complaints.

## §4 Hazard analysis (Ch 8)

**Why:** this is the hardest and most important step.
- Miss a hazard and the plan fails.
- Include too many and the system becomes unmanageable and dilutes resources *(manual)*.

**The FSPCA hazard analysis form**, one row per process step and per incoming ingredient (similar ingredients may be grouped) *(manual)*:

| Col | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 | Ingredient or processing step (from the flow diagram) |
| 2 | **Identify** potential food safety hazards introduced, controlled or enhanced at this step: B (biological), C (chemical, including radiological and allergens), P (physical) |
| 3 | Do any potential hazards **require a preventive control**? (Yes/No) |
| 4 | **Justify** the decision for column 3 (severity, probability) |
| 5 | What preventive control measure(s) can significantly minimize or prevent the hazard? (process, allergen, sanitation, supply-chain, other) |
| 6 | Is the preventive control applied **at this step**? (Yes/No) |

Other formats, such as narratives or separate severity and likelihood columns, are fine if they cover identification, evaluation, control selection and justification *(manual)*.

**Hazard identification (brainstorm)** considers *(manual)*:
- Formulation.
- The condition, function and design of facility and equipment.
- Ingredients and raw materials, including water, ice and compressed air in contact with food.
- Transportation (bulk vs packaged; refrigerated abuse).
- Packaging and labeling (allergen labels; reduced oxygen packaging).
- Storage and distribution.
- Intended and foreseeable use.
- Sanitary conditions: raw and cooked product handled by the same people, ready-to-eat lines near raw lines, cleaning frequency.
- Economically motivated hazards.
- Experience: testing, complaints, outbreaks, recalls.

**Hazard evaluation:**
- **Severity:** duration, how serious (hospitalization or death), long-term effects, vulnerable consumers. If a hazard, when present, is reasonably likely to make the food injurious, include it *(manual)*.
- **Probability without controls:** outbreaks and recalls, literature, regulatory and trade guidance, your own data. It also depends on how well prerequisite programs work *(manual)*.
  - Two facilities making the same product can reach different conclusions. For example, one with a strong preventive maintenance history may not need metal detection as a preventive control, while another does *(manual)*.

**Choosing preventive controls** *(manual)*:
- **Biological:** kill steps (cooking, pasteurization, irradiation, HPP); growth prevention (acidification, fermentation, drying, cooling, refrigeration, time/temperature limits); sanitation (environmental pathogens); supply-chain programs (sensitive ingredients without a kill step); hygiene training.
- **Allergens:** labeling, production scheduling, cleaning (cross-contact), segregation. Cross-contact prevention can be an allergen or a sanitation control, or both.
- **Physical:** screening and sieving, aspiration, magnets, **metal detection**, x-ray.
- **Economically motivated:** supply-chain program, testing.
- **Questions to ask:**
  - Where is the control in the system relative to other controls?
  - Is it specifically applied to eliminate or reduce the hazard?
  - How variable is the process?
  - How likely is failure, and how severe would it be?
  - Could a later step correct it?
- **One control can cover several hazards**, e.g. a cook step for *Salmonella* and *E. coli* O157:H7 *(manual)*.
- **Choice can be strategic.** E.G. Food Company could have used a supply-chain program for *Salmonella* in pasteurized eggs, but chose its own cook step as the process control because it was easier to manage *(manual)*.
- **Not all preventive controls are CCPs.** Generic HACCP plans and decision trees can help, but may not address allergen, sanitation and supply-chain controls to the extent the rule requires *(manual)*.

**Grouping:** one hazard analysis can cover similar products (e.g. three omelet varieties) if formulation differences such as pH, a_w and allergen profile are explicitly considered *(manual)*.
