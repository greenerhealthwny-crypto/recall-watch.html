# Consumer Reports Complete Guide to Managing Your Money (1989)

A working guide distilled from the Consumer Reports Books guide by Janet Bamford, Jeff Blyskal, Emily Card and Aileen Jacobson, edited by Consumer Reports Books with Jeff Blyskal (Consumers Union, Mount Vernon, NY) *(book)*. It is a consumer-advocate, no-sales-pitch reference in six parts and 26 chapters. Most chapters end with "Close-Up" boxes such as "What's in the Fine Print?", tips for buying, and checklists *(book)*.

## Durable principles from the book

1. **Budget in four steps** *(book)*:
   1. Cash-flow analysis: see where past money actually went.
   2. Set budget goals.
   3. Allocate resources.
   4. Year-end review: total each category, compare with the budget and the prior year, make sure annual income (not counting savings) exceeds spending, and set next year's goals.
   - Supporting tools: budget for the unexpected, keep good records, and make the budget work.
2. **Build an emergency fund before investing.** Its size is "a personal matter". Some experts in the book suggest $10,000–$15,000; others a number of months of expenses *(book)*. Today's common guide is 3–6 months of essential expenses *(std, verify)*.
3. **Keep debt in check:**
   - Monthly principal and interest on all debts **except the mortgage** should not exceed **15% of gross income** *(book)*.
   - Lenders cap housing payments at about **28% of gross monthly income**, using the **28%/36%** guideline (housing, then total debt) *(book)*.
   - Don't invest in stocks until debts are under control and you have adequate insurance *(book)*.
4. **Trade-offs in investing:** liquidity, safety and total return. "Liquidity and safety usually go hand-in-hand, but for a higher return you generally have to sacrifice some liquidity or safety, or both" *(book)*.
   - Diversify rather than concentrating in one company.
   - Until your surplus beyond the emergency and retirement funds reaches about $10,000–$20,000, consider short-term CDs, Treasury bills or a low-minimum mutual fund.
   - Dollar-cost averaging and dividend reinvestment help small investors.

   [*(book)*]
5. **Insure against catastrophe, and calculate need.** Life insurance need takes three steps *(book)*:
   1. Estimate the family's expenses if you died: immediate costs (funeral, estate taxes) and future costs (living expenses, education).
   2. Subtract assets and income sources.
   3. Buy coverage for the gap.

   Understand **term** (pure protection) versus **cash-value** policies *(book)*. Insure a home for its **replacement cost of the building, excluding land** *(book)*.
6. **Know your credit file.**
   - Many routine payments (phone, utilities, rent, medical bills) are never reported to credit bureaus *(book)*.
   - The Equal Credit Opportunity Act created a "user" designation so a married person, usually a woman, gets credit for accounts in the spouse's name. Check that your accounts are reported *(book)*.
   - Today you can get free reports at annualcreditreport.com and dispute errors under the FCRA *(std, verify)*.
7. **Be skeptical of financial planners.** "Few people need a financial planner." Setting goals, revising them and investing on sound principles yourself "is safer and more sensible" for most. Watch for commission-driven product sellers with "misleading titles" *(book)*. Today, ask whether a planner is a fiduciary and how they're paid *(std, verify)*.
8. **Read the fine print, and expect to be paid for your business.** "Recognize your value to the bank and expect adequate compensation in return" *(book)*.
9. **Plan for retirement early.** Planning stages run by age, starting with early planning from 20 to 50. Don't let the focus on career and family crowd it out *(book)*.
10. **Have a will.** Dying intestate (without a will) leaves the disposition of your assets to state law *(book)*.

**Workflow for a personal money check-up:**
1. Cash flow: what came in and went out last year.
2. Emergency fund.
3. Debt: the 15%-of-gross rule for non-mortgage debt; pay off high-interest cards.
4. Insurance gaps: health, disability, life if there are dependents, home or renters, auto liability.
5. Retirement savings and employer match.
6. Investing the surplus according to liquidity, safety and return.
7. Will and beneficiaries.
