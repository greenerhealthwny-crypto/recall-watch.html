# Insurance and Taxes (Parts III–IV, Ch 10–16)

*(book)* = in the 1989 text. *(std, verify)* = current context to confirm.

## §1 Medical insurance (Ch 12)

- **The book's framing:** "protecting your most valuable asset". About 82% of people under 65 had coverage, and everyone over 65 is eligible for Medicare. The US had no national health system *(book)*.
- **Coverage types** *(book)*:
  - Traditional indemnity: basic plus major medical, with **coinsurance** (often 80/20) up to an out-of-pocket ceiling, often about $2,000 or less, after which the insurer pays 100%.
  - Dental.
  - HMOs and health service contractors.
  - Medicare and Medicaid.
  - Close-Ups: tips for buying, and health insurance for the unemployed (continuation coverage).
- Current *(std, verify)*: the ACA marketplace, essential health benefits, no pre-existing-condition exclusions, HSAs and HDHPs, Medicare Parts A–D and Medigap, and COBRA.

## §2 Life insurance (Ch 13)

- **Who needs it:** people with dependents who rely on their income *(book)*.
- **How much: the three-step method** *(book)*:
  1. Estimate the expenses your family would face if you died: immediate costs (funeral, probate and administration, estate taxes) and future costs (living expenses, children's education).
  2. Total the assets and income sources available: savings, Social Security survivor benefits, a spouse's income, existing policies.
  3. Buy coverage for the gap.
- **Types** *(book)*:
  - **Term**: pure protection, simple.
  - **Cash-value**: whole life, and **universal life** (introduced 1979; it shows you the cash-value accumulation that whole life keeps "in the dark"; front-load or back-load charges).
  - The book discusses converting between the two.
- **Credit-life insurance** "is usually not a good buy". One large policy beats several small ones *(book)*.
- **Heuristic** *(std)*: most families are well served by low-cost **term** for the years dependents need support, while saving and investing separately.

## §3 Homeowner's and renter's insurance (Ch 14)

- **Insure the replacement cost of the building**, excluding land, because land survives a disaster. Agents use cost per square foot *(book)*.
- **Replacement-cost contents** coverage costs more: about 10–15% extra for owners and 20–40% for renters. Policies may cap it, often at 4× cash value *(book)*.
- **Separate policies** exist for homeowners, renters, condominium owners (who cover their own unit) and mobile homes *(book)*.
- **Liability** coverage protects you and household members, for example if your dog bites someone *(book)*.
- **Umbrella** policies give $1 million or more of liability, then about $100 a year, and cover libel, slander and similar claims *(book)*.
- **Close-Ups:** a household inventory, and tips for buying *(book)*.
- Current *(std, verify)*: flood and earthquake insurance are separate (flood through NFIP or private insurers). Keep photo or video inventories in the cloud.

## §4 Automobile insurance (Ch 15)

- **Coverages** *(book)*:
  - Liability (bodily injury and property damage).
  - Medical payments.
  - **No-fault / PIP** in no-fault states.
  - Uninsured motorist.
  - **Collision** (your car's damage regardless of fault).
  - Comprehensive.
- **Premiums** depend on driver, car, location and use. **Shop around** *(book)*.
- **Assigned-risk** plans exist for high-risk drivers *(book)*.
- **Close-Ups:** watch out for packaged policies, and what to do if you are in an accident *(book)*.
- **Heuristic** *(std)*: carry high liability limits, raise deductibles to cut premiums, and drop collision on low-value cars.

## §5 Other insurance (Ch 16)

- **Disability insurance:** tally the monthly benefits you already have (group plan, Social Security disability, workers' comp) before buying more *(book)*.
- **Dread-disease (cancer) policies:** poor value for the dollar, narrow coverage, often sold with "promotional fear tactics" *(book)*.
- **Travel, title and legal insurance:** buy only for special circumstances, and ask what existing coverage already does *(book)*.

## §6 Taxes (Ch 10–11) — heavily outdated

- **The book covered** the 1986 Tax Reform Act phase-ins *(book)*:
  - the end of the consumer-interest deduction (10% in 1990, then zero);
  - IRA deductions limited to $2,000 a year when covered by an employer plan and above income limits (for example $35,000 AGI single);
  - passive-loss rules killing tax shelters (limited partnerships);
  - the alternative minimum tax;
  - home-office deductions;
  - Keogh and SEP plans for the self-employed.
- **Its planning advice still useful today** *(book)*: keep tax records and a tax-planning calendar; check withholding; deduct legitimate business expenses; get help with your return; know how IRS scrutiny works.
- **Special situations** *(book)*: business deductions, investments, divorce, barter.
- Current *(std, verify)*: brackets, standard deduction (greatly enlarged by the 2017 TCJA), IRA, 401(k) and HSA limits, SALT caps and credits all change yearly. Use IRS.gov or a tax professional. Never quote 1989 figures as current.
