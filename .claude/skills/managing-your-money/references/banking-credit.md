# Banking, Credit, Budgeting and Bankruptcy (Parts I–II, Ch 1–9)

*(book)* = in the 1989 text (OCR-checked). *(std, verify)* = current general knowledge to confirm with official sources.

## §1 Banking (Ch 1–5)

**Checking accounts (Ch 1)** *(book)*:
- Free checking with no minimum balance largely disappeared from the mid-1970s.
- Fee structures include:
  - **minimum-balance** accounts (for example, no fee if the average daily balance is $500);
  - per-check fees;
  - interest-bearing accounts.
- Studies found the average person writes about 21 checks a month. People with modest balances ($100–$500) or few checks should choose accordingly.
- **Shop by your own pattern:** balance, checks written, ATM use. "Recognize your value to the bank and expect adequate compensation in return."
- Small banks and credit unions can be more people-oriented.
- Debit cards carry pages of fine print; read them.
- Avoid **check-cashing stores**, which take a high percentage of the check's value.

**Deposit insurance** *(book)*:
- In 1989 the FDIC (banks) and FSLIC (S&Ls) insured each person's deposits at a bank up to **$100,000**.
- Current *(std, verify)*: FSLIC is gone. FDIC and NCUA insure **$250,000** per depositor, per bank, per ownership category.

**Check holds** *(book)*: from September 1988, Treasury checks, same-state government checks and on-us checks had to be available by the next business day (Expedited Funds Availability Act). Current rules are under Regulation CC *(std, verify)*.

**Savings (Ch 2)** *(book)*:
- Decide how much **liquidity** you need.
- Options are passbook and statement savings, **money market deposit accounts**, and **certificates of deposit**.
- CDs pay more because you accept penalties for early withdrawal. Larger denominations can earn higher rates.
- Insured bank accounts pay less than uninsured money-market funds.
- Credit unions often pay better savings rates.
- Read promotional mailings and statement inserts critically.

**Loans (Ch 3)** *(book)*:
- The book weighs the pros and cons of borrowing. It covers mortgages, auto loans, personal loans and home equity loans (then "a new form of mortgage").
- **Mortgages:** choose fixed or adjustable (rates reset every 1, 3 or 5 years). Compare the **APR including points**, and value a prepayment clause.
- **Down payment:** lenders traditionally lent **80%** of appraised value, wanting the buyer to put in 20% for motivation. Many stretched to **90–95% with mortgage insurance**.
- Make as large a down payment as possible and pay cash where you can.
- Home-equity loans had tax-deductible interest but "other major disadvantages", chiefly that your home is the collateral.

**Charge cards (Ch 4)** *(book)*:
- A credit card offers a line of credit with monthly payments, while a T&E (travel and entertainment) card must be paid in full.
- Watch annual fees, interest and grace periods.
- After the 1986 tax law, consumer-interest deductions were phased out (10% deductible in 1990, then zero), so pay cards in full.
- There are legal protections for cardholders on loss and fraud.
- Current *(std, verify)*: federal law caps liability for unauthorized credit-card charges at **$50**, and most issuers offer $0. Debit-card liability depends on how quickly you report (EFTA).

**Banking alternatives (Ch 5)** *(book)*: credit unions, money market mutual funds, **asset management accounts** at brokers, "nonbank banks", and loan alternatives. The book calls this "shopping in the financial supermarket".

## §2 Money management (Ch 6–9)

**Budgeting (Ch 6)** *(book)*:
1. **Cash-flow analysis:** how past income and spending actually flowed. This isn't yet a budget.
2. **Set budget goals.**
3. **Allocate resources** to categories.
4. **Year-end review:** total each category, compare it with the budget and the previous year, make sure income (excluding savings) exceeds spending, graph progress such as debt repayment, and set new goals.
- **Close-ups:** budget for the unexpected; keep your documents; make your budget work.
- **Emergency fund:** "a personal matter". Some experts suggest $10,000–$15,000, others a set number of months of expenses. Common current guidance is 3–6 months of essential expenses *(std)*.

**Financial planning (Ch 7)** *(book)*:
- Set and achieve goals.
- "Few people need a financial planner." Using a "professional" planner is often a choice between "a luxurious service for millionaires and financial products marketers with misleading titles".
- The book's Close-Up covers regulating financial planners.
- Current *(std, verify)*: ask whether a planner is a **fiduciary** and fee-only, and check them on FINRA BrokerCheck or the SEC's IAPD.

**Credit rating (Ch 8)** *(book)*:
- **How it works:**
  - Many payments (phone, utilities, rent, medical) are **not reported**.
  - The ECOA **"user" designation** gives a married person (usually a woman) credit for joint or spouse accounts. Check your report shows them.
  - Lenders must respond to a credit application within 30 days.
- **If you are denied credit:**
  - You can see your bureau file. The fee is waived if you were denied within the previous 30 days.
  - You can review it in person and bring one other person.
  - Dispute errors.
- **The book's skepticism:** it is wary of paid "credit credential" and monitoring services ("Are credit credentials worth their cost?").
- **Close-Ups:** a sample consumer credit report, and a credit report checklist.
- Current *(std, verify)*: get free weekly reports from all three bureaus at **annualcreditreport.com**. You have FCRA dispute rights, and you can place free **credit freezes**. Most negative items drop off after 7 years, Chapter 7 bankruptcy after 10.

**Bankruptcy (Ch 9)** *(book)*:
- **Chapter 7** ("straight bankruptcy") liquidates non-exempt assets through a court-appointed trustee to pay creditors.
- **Chapter 13** has the debtor repay part of the debt over about **three years** under a court plan.
- Ch 7 could be filed only once every seven years. Bankruptcy stays on your credit file for **10 years**.
- **Exemptions** protect some property.
- **Filings rose** from 172,414 in 1978 to 298,045 in 1985 after the 1978 Code. In 1984 Congress tightened the rules ("What Is Too Much Debt?").
- Current *(std, verify)*: the **2005 BAPCPA** added a means test and mandatory credit counseling, and changed waiting periods (8 years between Ch 7 discharges). Consult a bankruptcy attorney.
