# Investing, Real Estate, Retirement and Estates (Parts V–VI, Ch 17–26)

*(book)* = in the 1989 text. *(std, verify)* = current context to confirm.

## §1 Investing (Ch 17–20)

**Principles (Ch 17)** *(book)*:
- **Three potential advantages:** liquidity, safety and total return (income plus growth of capital).
  - "Liquidity and safety usually go hand-in-hand, but for a higher return you generally have to sacrifice some liquidity or safety, or both."
  - The more liquid an investment, the lower its likely return. That is why money-market funds yield less than corporate bonds.
- **Preconditions before stocks:**
  - Debts under control: non-mortgage debt payments at or below 15% of gross income.
  - Adequate insurance.
  - An emergency fund.
- **A staged strategy:** until your surplus beyond emergency and retirement funds reaches about $10,000–$20,000, use short-term (6–12 month) CDs, Treasury bills, or a low-minimum stock or bond fund. Rates are unpredictable, so stagger maturities.
- **Diversify:** a single company is less desirable than spreading your money.
- **Is sound advice available?** Consider a do-it-yourself approach, the range of investment alternatives, and timing and diversification.
- **Speculative assets** carry high risk: junk bonds, fledgling companies, precious metals, commodity options, collectibles, oil ventures.

**Stocks (Ch 18)** *(book)*:
- Stock market basics; investing in the stock market; "can anyone beat the stock market?"
- **Close-Ups:** a short course in living dangerously (speculation); technical analysis; how to handle problems with your broker.
- **Dollar-cost averaging** and **dividend reinvestment plans** (buying direct from companies without commissions) suit small investors.
- Use discount brokers for trades.

**Bonds and funds (Ch 19–20)** *(book)*:
- **Bonds:** Treasuries, municipals (tax-free) and corporates.
  - Diversification is "a must" with municipal and corporate bonds because default is possible.
  - Small-lot trading costs can be prohibitive, which is an argument for funds.
  - Compare after-tax returns.
- **Mutual funds:**
  - More diversification than most individuals can afford.
  - **Loads** (sales commissions) erode returns; no-load funds are sold directly.
  - **12b-1 fees** pay for marketing and "do nothing" for existing shareholders.
  - Check performance and minimums. The Investment Company Institute publishes a fund guide.
- **Current notes** *(std, verify)*:
  - Low-cost **index funds and ETFs** now dominate.
  - Expense ratios are far lower than in 1989.
  - Commission-free trading is common.
  - Target-date funds exist.
  - Check advisers on FINRA BrokerCheck.

## §2 Your home and other real estate (Ch 21–22)

- **Home** *(book)*:
  - Estimate how much you can afford, choose the right kind of home and location, make an offer, finance it, close, then manage it as an investment.
  - Lenders limit principal and interest to about **28% of gross monthly income** (the 28%/36% guideline).
  - **Private mortgage insurance** is required below 20% down, and costs about 0.25% extra interest until your equity reaches the required level.
- **Second homes** *(book)*: rates about 0.5–1% higher because defaults are more likely. Vacation-house rentals in resort areas "are not dependable" income.
- **Other real estate** *(book)*: rental property, REITs, real estate limited partnerships (RELPs), REMICs, unit trusts, real-estate CDs and raw land. The 1986 Act ended most real-estate tax-shelter benefits.
- **Current notes** *(std, verify)*: PMI can be cancelled at 20% equity and ends automatically at 22% (Homeowners Protection Act). Lender debt-to-income limits now vary by loan type. Mortgage-interest and property-tax deductions changed under the TCJA.

## §3 Retirement and estate planning (Ch 23–26)

**How to plan (Ch 23)** *(book)*:
- Plan in stages by age, starting with **early planning (ages 20 to 50)**: retirement saving should begin with your first job, even though the focus is "largely on career and family".
- Weigh pension benefits when changing jobs.
- Later stages cover intensifying the plan as retirement nears, and early-retirement choices.

**IRAs and Keoghs (Ch 24)** *(book)*:
- In 1989: up to **$2,000 a year** to an IRA, with spousal provisions. Deductibility was limited if you were covered by an employer plan and your AGI exceeded $35,000 (single).
- **Keogh and SEP** plans let the self-employed save more, based on net self-employment income. They are especially valuable if IRA contributions aren't deductible.
- Current *(std, verify)*: Roth IRAs (from 1998), much higher IRA, 401(k), SEP and Solo 401(k) limits, catch-up contributions, and RMD ages have all changed. Check IRS.gov.

**Social Security and pensions (Ch 25)** *(book)*: how benefits are earned and estimated; early benefits from age 62; Medicare at 65; and how pensions work (vesting, payout options). Current *(std, verify)*: full retirement age is 67 for people born in 1960 or later. Use your SSA account (ssa.gov/myaccount) for estimates.

**Wills, trusts and taxes (Ch 26)** *(book)*:
- **Many people die intestate** because they procrastinate, think they own too little, or assume the law will handle it. Then state law decides who inherits.
- **Probate and executor fees** vary by state and lawyer. Some states cap them as a percentage of the estate.
- **The revocable "living" trust** (inter vivos) avoids **probate** but gives **no income-tax benefit**, since you pay tax on trust income as trustee. Trust assets are exposed to creditors during your life but not after death.
- Current *(std, verify)*:
  - The federal estate-tax exemption is now in the millions and indexed.
  - Use beneficiary designations and transfer-on-death accounts.
  - Add a durable power of attorney and a health-care proxy or advance directive to any estate plan.
  - Consult an estate attorney.
