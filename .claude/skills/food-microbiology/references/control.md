## §1 Chemical antimicrobials (Ch 25)

- **The safety logic:** pathogens can grow without visible spoilage, so a preservation system that lets pathogens grow while it inhibits spoilage is inherently dangerous *(book)*.
- Most preservatives are **bacteriostatic**, not bactericidal. They are combined for synergy as part of hurdle technology. Their targets are the cell wall, membrane, enzymes and genes *(book)*.
- **"Clean label"** has no official definition. Removing preservatives without a replacement shortens shelf life, adds to food waste and can raise risk. For example, Kraft replaced sorbic acid in its Singles with natamycin *(book)*.
- **Organic acids** (acetic, lactic, propionic, sorbic, benzoic, citric):
  - Only the **undissociated (protonated)** form crosses the membrane. Inside the neutral cytoplasm it dissociates and acidifies the cell, which then burns ATP pumping protons out. So they work best in acidic foods at or below their pKa *(book)*.
  - Henderson–Hasselbalch gives the undissociated fraction: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]) *(std)*.
  - Sorbates and benzoates target yeasts and molds. Propionates are used against bread molds and rope.
  - Lactic and acetic acids are carcass washes *(book)*.
- **Nitrite** (cured meats):
  - Inhibits *C. botulinum* and gives cured colour and flavour.
  - More effective at lower pH and without air *(book)*.
  - "Natural" cures use nitrite from cultured celery powder. The book raises the question of whether the source matters for health *(book)*.
  - Concerns about processed meat and cancer (IARC) are discussed *(book)*.
- **Parabens** (esters of p-hydroxybenzoic acid) act like organic acids but stay active at higher pH *(book)*.
- **Sulfites:** mainly fruits, vegetables and wine; against yeasts, molds and browning *(book)*.
- **Sodium chloride** lowers a_w and causes plasmolysis *(book)*.
- **Chelators:** phosphates, EDTA, lactoferrin (milk's iron-binding protein) remove metals microbes need *(book)*.
- **Lysozyme:** from egg white; heat stable at low pH; lyses Gram-positive cell walls. Used against *Clostridium* blowing in European cheeses; less effective on Gram-negatives *(book)*.
- **Essential oils and spice compounds** have activity but often need flavour-limiting doses *(book/std)*.
- **Water activity as a hurdle:** pathogens don't grow below **a_w 0.85**. *S. aureus* grows to about 0.86 but needs about 0.93 for toxin. Most sporeformers stop at 0.93 *(book)*.
- **Disinfectants and sanitizers** are regulated by the **EPA** as indirect or incidental additives on food-contact surfaces *(book)*.
  - **Hydrogen peroxide** and **peroxyacetic acid** are the most common for some applications *(book)*.
  - **Chlorine compounds** are the most widely used in plants *(book)*.
  - Organic soil, water impurities (iron, manganese, nitrites, sulfides), pH and temperature reduce their efficacy. **Clean before you sanitize** *(book)*.
  - USDA increasingly requires microbial reduction steps (antimicrobial washes) for raw meat and poultry *(book)*.

## §2 Biological preservation and probiotics (Ch 26)

- **Refrigeration alone is not enough:** if temperatures are abused, LAB can produce acid "in place" and stop pathogens. The USDA approved a **Wisconsin process** for bacon in 1986: LAB plus sugar, as a backup to nitrite *(book)*.
- **Bacteriocins** are small antimicrobial proteins or peptides made by bacteria. They kill sensitive (mainly Gram-positive) bacteria by forming pores in their membranes *(book)*.
  - **Lantibiotics** (e.g. **nisin**, from *Lactococcus lactis*) contain unusual amino acids (lanthionine) and ring structures.
    - Nisin is GRAS in the US and used worldwide in processed cheese, dairy and canned foods. It sensitizes spores to heat and inhibits *C. botulinum* toxin formation *(book)*.
    - Using nisin to cut thermal processes isn't approved in the US, because it might mask poor process control *(book)*.
    - Combinations work better: nisin plus modified atmosphere, e.g. against *L. monocytogenes* in pork, or type E botulinal toxin in CO₂-packed fish *(book)*.
  - **Pediocin-like** (class IIa) bacteriocins (pediocin PA-1/AcH, sakacins, leucocin A, curvacin A) share a conserved N-terminal motif and are strongly **anti-*Listeria***. They don't affect clostridial spores *(book)*.
  - Bacteriocins are used by adding a **bacteriocin-producing starter** in the food, or as an approved additive *(book)*.
  - Bacteriocin genes, immunity genes and processing and export genes are grouped in operons, often on plasmids *(book)*.
- **ε-Polylysine** (from *Streptomyces albulus*, used in Japan): a lysine polymer active below 100 µg/ml *(book)*.
- **Bacteriophages** can be applied to kill *Listeria* and other pathogens *(book, Ch 24)*.
- **Probiotics** are "live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host" *(std; FAO/WHO)*.
  - Possible benefits: lower cholesterol, better lactose digestion, immune effects, fewer diarrhea symptoms, anticancer activity *(book)*.
  - The gut ecosystem is complex, which makes claims hard to prove *(book)*.
  - **Prebiotics** are non-digestible substrates that feed beneficial microbes *(std)*.

## §3 Physical methods (Ch 27)

- **Drying:**
  - Prevents growth but doesn't sterilize. Microbes die mostly during storage, as injured cells can't recover at low a_w *(book)*.
  - Condensation from humidity or temperature swings can allow surface growth *(book)*.
  - Jerky needs a validated lethality step before drying, per USDA-FSIS guidance *(book/std)*.
  - Freeze-drying (sublimation) keeps solutes in place *(book)*.
- **Refrigeration:**
  - Slows reactions and is below the minimum growth temperature of many pathogens. But psychrotrophs grow: *Listeria*, *Yersinia*, nonproteolytic *C. botulinum*, *B. cereus* (some strains), *Aeromonas* *(book/std)*.
  - Keep cold foods ≤ 41 °F (5 °C) *(std, Food Code)*.
  - Some produce suffers chilling injury below about 4–12 °C *(book)*.
- **Freezing** stops growth but is not reliably lethal. Damage comes from ice crystals and solute concentration; salt depresses the freezing point and prolongs solute stress. Thawing releases nutrient-rich drip *(book)*. Freezing kills parasites (e.g. in fish for raw consumption) *(std)*.
- **Controlled and modified atmospheres** (CO₂, N₂, low O₂, vacuum) extend the shelf life of meats and produce. But they can favour anaerobes and psychrotrophic pathogens, especially nonproteolytic *C. botulinum*, so they need a second barrier and time or temperature limits *(book)*.
- **Heat:**
  - **Pasteurization** kills all vegetative pathogens (not spoilage organisms or spores) and inactivates enzymes, so pasteurized foods still need refrigeration *(book)*.
    - Milk is classically pasteurized at 63 °C for 30 min (LTLT) or 72 °C for 15 s (HTST) *(std)*.
    - Juice HACCP requires a **5-log reduction** of the pertinent pathogen *(book, Ch 28)*.
  - **Commercial sterilization** kills all microbes that could grow in the product under normal storage *(book)*.
  - Lethality comes from damage to membranes, ribosomes, enzymes and DNA. **Moist heat** kills faster than dry heat, and spores are far more resistant than vegetative cells *(book)*.
  - **Kinetics:** death is negative exponential (log-linear).
    - **D value** = time at temperature T for a 1-log (90%) kill.
    - **z value** = degrees per 10-fold change in D.
    - **Lethal rate** = 10^((T − T_ref)/z); the **F value** integrates lethality over the process.
    - **Log reduction = time ÷ D** *(book)*.
  - **Botulinum cook:** 12D for *C. botulinum* types A and B spores (D₁₂₁ ≈ 0.20 min, z ≈ 10 °C/18 °F) gives **2.4 min at 250 °F**. One survivor per 10¹² spores is a huge safety margin *(book)*.
    - *Worked example:* a process giving 2.16 min at 250 °F delivers 10.8 logs. A process at the right time but about 10% lower temperature (225 °F) delivers 2.4 / (0.2 × 10^(25/18)) ≈ 0.5 log, far worse. **Temperature deviations matter much more than time deviations** *(book problem)*.
  - Heat processes must be determined in the actual food and container (heat-penetration studies, cold spot), and validated *(book)*. Heat shock and acid adaptation can raise microbial resistance *(book)*.
  - Peanut butter and other low-a_w foods can protect *Salmonella* from heat *(book problem/std)*.
- Validation of novel processes uses the **most resistant microorganism of public health significance** as target *(book, NACMCF definition)*.

## §4 Nonthermal processing (Ch 28)

The aim is to inactivate microbes while keeping fresh-like quality *(book)*.

- **High pressure processing (HPP):**
  - Typically about 400–600 MPa for a few minutes *(std)*.
  - Kills vegetative cells; spores need pressure plus heat. Pressure-assisted thermal sterilization is FDA-accepted *(book, Ch 10)*.
  - Uses: guacamole (it also inactivates polyphenol oxidase, preventing browning), juices, deli meats (as a post-packaging *Listeria* step and lower-nitrite option), oysters (shucking plus *Vibrio* reduction) *(book)*.
  - Needs flexible packaging; metal cans don't work *(book problem)*.
- **Pulsed electric fields (PEF):** short high-voltage pulses electroporate membranes, so cells "explode". About 5–9 log reductions in pumpable liquids such as juices, with fresh-like flavour *(book)*.
- **Ozone:**
  - A strong oxidant effective against bacteria, spores, fungi, protozoa and viruses (it damages the capsid). Dose is expressed as **Ct** (concentration × time) *(book)*.
  - Used in produce and meat wash water: one plant saved more than $100,000 a year replacing organic-acid washes *(book)*. Also poultry drinking water, and apple cider (which kept its "farm-fresh" character) *(book)*.
  - It is best for surfaces. Organic load consumes it *(book)*.
  - Spores that survive ozone become more sensitive to mild heat and salt *(book)*.
- **Irradiation (ionizing):**
  - Doses below about 5 kGy pasteurize the surfaces of meats and poultry and extend shelf life.
  - D₁₀ values run from low (*Campylobacter*, *Aeromonas*) to higher (*Salmonella*). *C. botulinum* spores are highly resistant (D about 2–4.5 kGy) *(book)*.
  - Consumer hesitancy limits use *(book)*.
- **UV light:** surface and clear-liquid treatment (e.g. apple cider) *(book)*.
- **Cold plasma:** ionized gas (the "fourth state of matter") generated at low temperature. It sanitizes heat-sensitive surfaces such as fresh-cut produce via reactive species, UV and charged particles. It is still early-stage *(book)*.
- **Others:** oscillating magnetic fields and ultrasound are future possibilities *(book)*.
- **Legal note:** "pasteurization" can now be defined by outcome rather than by heat alone. Any process must be validated for the pertinent pathogen (e.g. a 5-log reduction for juice HACCP) *(book)*.

## §5 Sanitation, GMPs, HACCP, FSMA (Ch 29)

- **Sanitation** is a science-based program to control microbes in the plant. It prevents pathogen contamination, limits spoilage organisms, extends shelf life and protects the brand *(book)*.
  - Cleaning removes soil; sanitizing reduces microbes. **Clean first.**
  - The cycle is: dry clean → pre-rinse → detergent → rinse → sanitize (→ rinse if required) *(std)*.
  - Cooling systems have spread microbes and caused outbreaks *(book)*.
- **Choosing a sanitizer:** its antimicrobial spectrum, the effect of organic matter and water hardness, temperature and pH, residues and toxicity, corrosiveness, and cost *(book)*.
  - **Chlorine** is cheap and broad-spectrum but inactivated by organic matter and corrosive.
  - **Iodophors.**
  - **Quaternary ammonium compounds:** residual activity, weaker against Gram-negatives.
  - **Peracetic acid and H₂O₂.**
  - **Ozone** *(std)*.
- **GMPs** (current Good Manufacturing Practices; FDA, 1969, revised 1986, modernized under FSMA) cover:
  - Personnel: hygiene, illness exclusion, training.
  - Plant and grounds.
  - Sanitary operations.
  - Sanitary facilities (water, plumbing, toilets, hand washing).
  - Equipment and utensils (designed for cleaning).
  - Processes and controls, warehousing.
  - **Defect action levels** for natural, unavoidable defects that pose no health hazard *(book)*.
- **SSOPs** are written sanitation procedures, required in USDA plants *(book)*.
- **HACCP** is a preventive, proactive system. It needs management support and a multidisciplinary team, possibly with an outside expert *(book)*.
  - **Preliminary steps:** describe the product and intended use; draw a **flow diagram** and verify it by walking the line, e.g. to catch new metal detectors or rework routes *(book)*.
  - **The seven principles** *(book)*:
    1. Conduct a hazard analysis (biological, chemical, physical).
    2. Determine **critical control points** (CCPs): steps where control is essential to prevent, eliminate or reduce a hazard to acceptable levels. Use a decision tree.
    3. Establish **critical limits**: measurable bounds such as time, temperature, pH, a_w.
    4. Establish **monitoring** procedures.
    5. Establish **corrective actions**.
    6. Establish **verification** procedures: validation, calibration, record review, testing.
    7. Establish **record-keeping and documentation**.
  - **Mandatory HACCP:** USDA's 1996 Pathogen Reduction/HACCP rule for meat and poultry (with *Salmonella* performance standards, SSOPs and *E. coli* testing), FDA seafood HACCP, and juice HACCP (5-log reduction) *(book)*.
  - It applies equally to **food service** (flow of food, cooking, cooling, hot and cold holding, avoiding cross-contamination) and even to the home *(book)*.
  - Out-of-spec ingredients can't simply be blended down to compliance *(book problem)*.
- **FSMA Preventive Controls (HARPC)** extends hazard analysis to process, allergen, sanitation and supply-chain controls, with a written food safety plan (see `pathogens.md` §1). GAPs cover the farm level *(book)*.
- **FSOs / POs and risk assessment** link public health goals to plant controls (see `fundamentals.md` §6) *(book)*.
- **Critical thinking with numbers:**
  - Check magnitudes against reference values; there are about 30 g per ounce (28.3 exactly).
  - Cross-check statistics across sources. The book notes that an estimate of foodborne deaths was repeated until it became "accepted" before being revised *(book)*.
