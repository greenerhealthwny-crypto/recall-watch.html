## §1 Food ecosystems

- **Intrinsic factors** (inherent to the food): pH, water activity (a_w), nutrients, redox, natural antimicrobials, structure. **Extrinsic factors** (external): temperature, gaseous atmosphere, the presence of other microbes *(book)*.
- **Metabolism and oxygen:**
  - Aerobes respire (the TCA cycle, with oxygen as terminal electron acceptor) and get far more ATP than anaerobes.
  - Anaerobes use substrate-level phosphorylation (1–2 ATP per glucose), and strict anaerobes are killed by air.
  - Facultative anaerobes do both. For example, *S. aureus* tolerates salt better when grown aerobically *(book)*.
  - Cooking drives oxygen out of foods, leaving much of a product anaerobic. This favours clostridia *(book)*.
- **pH** = −log[H⁺]; each unit is a 10-fold change.
  - Acid foods need milder processing.
  - Spoilage organisms (lactic acid bacteria, fungi) can grow in acid products, though pathogens rarely do *(book)*.
  - Cells keep internal pH (pHi) near neutral by proton pumping and buffering, by the **acid tolerance response (ATR)** and by acid shock proteins (triggered at pHi about 3–5) *(book)*.
  - The ATR **cross-protects**: acid-adapted *E. coli* O157:H7 gains heat and freeze–thaw resistance; acid-adapted *Listeria* survives nisin, heat and osmotic stress better *(book)*.
- **Water activity** a_w = P/P₀ (vapour pressure of the food ÷ that of pure water at the same temperature). It equals the equilibrium relative humidity ÷ 100.
  - Foods with the same moisture content can have different a_w.
  - Drying, freeze-drying, salting and sugaring lower a_w *(book)*.
  - Lower a_w lengthens the lag phase, slows growth and lowers the stationary population.
  - Gram-negatives need the highest a_w: *Pseudomonas* about 0.96 *(std)*, many Enterobacteriaceae about 0.93 *(std)*.
  - Many lactobacilli need about 0.94, and some micrococci grow below 0.90 *(book)*.
  - *S. aureus* is unique among pathogens: it grows to about **0.86** but makes no enterotoxin below about **0.93** *(book)*.
  - Most sporeformers don't grow below **0.93** *(book)*.
  - **a_w 0.85 is the lowest at which pathogens grow**, a cardinal value *(book)*.
  - Xerophilic molds and osmophilic yeasts can grow lower still.
  - Microbes cope with low a_w by accumulating compatible solutes: K⁺ and proline in bacteria; glycerol, erythritol and arabitol in fungi *(book)*.
- **Temperature** is the dominant extrinsic factor. It changes gene expression (motility, virulence), not just growth rate *(book)*.
  - Psychrophiles have optima below about 15 °C *(std)*. Psychrotrophs grow at refrigeration temperatures but have optima of about 20–30 °C *(std)*. Mesophiles have optima of about 30–40 °C, and thermophiles about 55–65 °C *(std)*.
  - Growth rate roughly doubles per 10 °C, but only over a limited range. It falls steeply above the optimum and gradually below it *(book)*.
  - **Danger zone:** 40–140 °F, about 4–60 °C *(book)*. Food at about 35 °C (a hot picnic) becomes dangerous in a few hours *(book)*. Above about 160 °F (about 71 °C), vegetative cells die within seconds *(book, risk chart)*.

## §2 Growth, injury, VBNC, biofilms, communication

- **Growth curve:** lag → exponential → stationary → death. Inhibitors can lengthen lag, cut growth rate, lower the stationary population or speed death *(book)*.
- **Injury:** cells hit by sublethal heat, acid, cold, sanitizer or radiation can't grow on selective media but still grow on nonselective media. Injured cells = count on nonselective medium − count on selective medium *(book)*. Injury matters because:
  1. Injured cells counted as dead make a heat process look stronger than it is.
  2. Cells missed at sampling can repair before the food is eaten.
  3. Food ingredients (salt, acids, low temperature) act as "selective agents" and hide injured cells.
- **Repair** needs RNA and protein synthesis and lengthens the lag phase. *L. monocytogenes* injured at 55 °C for 20 min fully recovers in about 9 h under favourable conditions, but at 4 °C repair is slow and full recovery takes 16–19 days *(book)*. Use repair or enrichment media.
- **VBNC (viable but nonculturable):** cells that won't grow on any medium yet can resuscitate and cause illness. Perhaps 99% of bacteria in the biosphere are nonculturable *(book)*. *Campylobacter* enters this state in water *(book)*.
- **Quorum sensing:** signal molecules accumulate as populations rise and switch on genes when a threshold is reached. Its role in spoilage is suspected but data are sparse *(book)*.
- **Biofilms:** structured communities on surfaces. They are hard to remove, resist sanitizers (mainly because cells grow slowly, not because of a diffusion barrier) and harbour *Listeria* and other pathogens in plants *(book)*.

## §3 Growth math and hurdles

- **N = N₀ · 2ⁿ**, where n = generations = t / doubling time; equivalently N = N₀·e^(μt), and doubling time t_d = 0.693/μ *(book)*.
- **Two levers:** lowering the initial count shifts the curve but keeps the slope. Lowering the temperature cuts μ: if μ halves per 10 °C, going from 37 to 7 °C cuts μ about 8-fold *(book)*. For a fixed shelf life, a 10-fold lower starting count buys roughly log₂10 ≈ 3.3 extra doublings.
- **Hurdle technology:** combine several sub-inhibitory barriers (pH, a_w, temperature, preservatives, competitive flora, atmosphere) so that together they inhibit growth. For example, a pH of 4.6 alone, or a milder pH (about 5.2) plus reduced a_w, can each prevent pathogen growth *(book)*. Claims of synergy should be backed by quantitative analysis *(book)*.
- **Risk zone thinking:** time × temperature determines hazard. Rapid cooling is critical, because slow cooling of large batches of cooked food causes more outbreaks (e.g. *C. perfringens*) than almost anything else *(book)*.

## §4 Spores and their significance (Ch 3)

- **Why they matter:** extreme resistance to heat, radiation, chemicals and drying. **Low-acid canned foods** (pH > 4.6, a_w > 0.85) must be commercially sterile *(book)*. FDA or USDA registration of the process is required before production *(book)*.
- **Commercial sterility:** no microbes that can grow under normal storage. It can be reached by heat alone, or by heat combined with acidification or reduced a_w *(book)*.
- **D value:** minutes at a given temperature to kill 90% (one log). **z value:** degrees needed to change D 10-fold *(book)*.
- **12D botulinum cook:** a 12-log reduction of *C. botulinum* spores. D₁₂₁·₁°C (D₂₅₀°F) ≈ 0.2 min, so 12D ≈ 2.4 min at 250 °F (F₀ ≈ 2.4–3) *(book)*. Any time–temperature combination equivalent to this is acceptable. Heavy spore loads (mushrooms, potatoes, spices) or spoilage concerns may need more *(book)*.
- **Acid and acidified foods** (equilibrium pH < 4.6) needn't kill all spores, because sporeformers can't grow there. Cured meats that aren't heated enough to kill spores rely on curing plus refrigeration *(book)*.
- **Key sporeformers:**
  - *C. botulinum* is the most heat-resistant pathogen. Its group I strains are proteolytic and mesophilic with very heat-resistant spores. Group II strains are nonproteolytic, have less heat-resistant spores but grow in the cold, which is a concern for minimally processed refrigerated foods *(book)*. Spores resist irradiation (D about 2.0–4.5 kGy) and ethylene oxide but are inactivated by halogens and H₂O₂ (used in aseptic packaging) *(book)*.
  - *C. perfringens* grows extremely fast in warm high-protein foods. Its heat-resistant and heat-sensitive spore classes may both survive cooking *(book)*.
  - *B. cereus* spore germination occurs at about 8–30 °C; food-poisoning strains tend to be more heat resistant *(book)*.
  - **Spoilage sporeformers:** thermophilic flat-sour bacteria (e.g. *Geobacillus stearothermophilus*, more heat resistant than botulinum spores), thermophilic anaerobes, butyric clostridia, *Alicyclobacillus acidoterrestris* (a disinfectant-like taint in juices) and psychrotrophic clostridia (blown packs of chilled meat) *(book)*.
  - **Heat-resistant molds** (*Byssochlamys*, *Neosartorya*, *Talaromyces*, *Eupenicillium*) make ascospores that survive more than 85 °C for 5 min and spoil fruit products *(book)*.
  - Emerging: psychrotrophic *B. weihenstephanensis* in milk and infant formula *(book)*.
- **Spore biology:**
  - The core holds only about 0.4–1 g water per g dry weight, vs about 4 g in vegetative cells. This dehydration, maintained by the cortex (a special peptidoglycan), drives dormancy and heat resistance *(book)*.
  - Dipicolinic acid (DPA) with Ca²⁺ and **SASP** (small acid-soluble proteins that saturate and protect DNA) add resistance *(book)*.
  - Sporulation temperature and mineral content affect resistance.
  - The life cycle runs sporulation → dormancy → activation → **germination** → **outgrowth**.
  - **Superdormant** spores germinate very slowly, if ever, which defeats "germinate-then-kill" hurdle strategies *(book)*.

## §5 Detection, enumeration, rapid methods (Ch 4–5)

- **Aerobic plate count (APC/SPC):**
  - Serial 10-fold dilutions; plate 0.1 ml (spread) or 1 ml (pour); incubate 24–48 h; count plates with **25–250 colonies**; multiply by the dilution factor and report as CFU/g or CFU/ml *(book)*.
  - It assumes one cell → one colony, but chains and clumps make it an underestimate. Strict anaerobes aren't counted *(book)*.
- **MPN:** for low numbers (below about 30 CFU/ml). Inoculate triplicate tubes at three or more dilutions and read the pattern of positive tubes against an MPN table. The three-tube detection limit is about 3.6/g (about 0.96/g with 10 tubes). It is less precise than plating *(book)*.
- **Other methods:**
  - Membrane filtration for liquids and low counts.
  - Direct microscopic count: fast, but counts dead cells too and needs high numbers.
  - Spiral plating, Petrifilm-type films.
  - Dye-reduction (reductase) tests for raw milk.
  - ATP bioluminescence and contact plates or swabs for surfaces *(book)*.
- **Sampling** must be statistically valid and validated for the food matrix. Selective enrichment lets you find a few pathogens among many competitors *(book)*.
- **Rapid methods:**
  - Count the whole time to result, including enrichment *(book)*.
  - Most detect about 10³–10⁵ CFU/ml, so samples usually need **enrichment (often about 12 h)** first *(book)*.
  - Types:
    - Miniaturized biochemical strips (e.g. API).
    - Specialty chromogenic or fluorogenic media.
    - Immunoassays: latex agglutination (needs above about 10⁵ CFU), ELISA, lateral flow, immunomagnetic separation.
    - Nucleic-acid methods: PCR and **real-time qPCR**, which needs an internal amplification control to catch false negatives caused by food inhibitors *(book)*. Also microarrays and sequencing.
  - Trends: biosensors and nanotechnology, which are hampered by fats and proteins fouling the sensors. Portable qPCR is used for biothreat agents *(book)*.
  - Validation by **AOAC International** (plus ISO, IDF) is the main third-party benchmark *(book)*.
  - "Test and hold" programs keep product until results are back *(book)*.

## §6 Indicators and microbiological criteria (Ch 6)

- **Indicators** signal possible contamination, poor sanitation or process failure when testing for every pathogen is impractical.
  - Ideal fecal indicators are found only in the intestine, are abundant in feces, survive as long as the pathogens, are easy to detect and are absent from pathogen-free foods *(book)*.
  - ***E. coli*** is the most widely used indicator of fecal contamination *(book)*.
  - **Fecal coliforms** are used for shellfish and shellfish-growing waters, though they can establish themselves in plants *(book)*.
  - **Coliforms** in heat-processed or ready-to-eat products indicate post-process contamination or process failure. They are preferred over *E. coli* or APC there, because they're more numerous and don't increase during proper storage *(book)*.
  - **APC** reflects quality and handling. Interpret it against the expected flora; some products (e.g. ground meat) carry high counts even under GMPs *(book)*.
  - Metabolic products such as diacetyl, ethanol, histamine and TVB-N can also indicate quality *(book)*.
- **Microbiological criteria** are set only when needed, effective and practical *(book)*. A criterion states:
  - The food.
  - The microbe or toxin.
  - The analytical method.
  - The sampling plan.
  - The limits.
  - The number of sample units that must conform.
  - Criteria are **standards** (mandatory), **guidelines** (advisory) or **specifications** (purchase).
- **Sampling plans (ICMSF attributes plans):**
  - **Two-class plan:** n units tested, c = the maximum allowed above m. Used for pathogens (presence/absence, usually c = 0).
  - **Three-class plan:** adds M, the unacceptable limit. Units between m and M are tolerated up to c.
  - Raising n at fixed c makes the plan more stringent *(book)*.
  - **ICMSF "cases":** stringency rises with the severity of the hazard and whether conditions of use could increase or decrease it. Hazards range from severe for the general population (e.g. *S.* Typhi) through severe for restricted populations, serious and moderate *(book)*.
- **FSOs (food safety objectives):** the maximum frequency or concentration of a hazard *at consumption* that is acceptable. **POs (performance objectives)** apply at earlier steps.
  - Hypothetical examples from the book: *L. monocytogenes* in ready-to-eat foods ≤ 100 CFU/g; *S.* Enteritidis ≤ 1 egg in 100,000; aflatoxin limits in peanuts. Very low-risk foods (carbonated drinks, most breads, sugar) need none *(book)*.
  - FSOs are not microbiological criteria. HACCP gives the evidence they are met *(book)*.
- **Microbial risk assessment (Codex):**
  - Hazard identification → exposure assessment → hazard characterization (dose–response) → risk characterization.
  - It is part of risk analysis, together with risk management and risk communication *(book)*.
  - Examples: *E. coli* O157:H7 in ground beef; *V. parahaemolyticus* in raw oysters *(book)*.
