## §1 Regulation, surveillance, outbreaks (Ch 7)

- **Burden:** the book cites 48 million Americans with foodborne illness each year *(book)*. The causes of about half of cases are unknown, and many are probably viral *(book)*.
- **Infection vs intoxication:** an infection comes from ingesting live microbes that grow in the host; an intoxication comes from a preformed toxin *(book)*.
- **FDA** (HHS) regulates most foods: seafood, produce, dairy, shell eggs and processed foods. Its food centre is CFSAN; its Center for Veterinary Medicine covers animal drugs and antimicrobial resistance *(book)*.
- **USDA-FSIS** regulates meat, poultry and processed egg products under continuous inspection *(book)*. Before FSMA, USDA couldn't force a recall, so recalls were "voluntary" under pressure *(book)*. USDA-AMS handles organic food; ARS does research *(book)*.
- **EPA** regulates sanitizers and pesticides *(book)*.
- **CDC** is nonregulatory. It does epidemiology and outbreak investigation:
  - **FoodNet** is active surveillance of laboratory-confirmed infections, including *Campylobacter*, *Salmonella*, *E. coli* O157 and non-O157 STEC, *Shigella*, *Vibrio* and *Yersinia* *(book)*. It also covers *Listeria*, *Cryptosporidium* and *Cyclospora* *(std)*.
  - **PulseNet** compares DNA fingerprints of isolates, by PFGE and increasingly whole-genome sequencing, to link cases *(book)*.
  - *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter* are the most frequent laboratory-confirmed infections *(book)*.
- **Key laws:**
  - The Pure Food and Drug Act and Meat Inspection Act (1906), after Upton Sinclair's *The Jungle*.
  - The Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (1938).
  - The Poultry Products Inspection Act (1957); the Wholesome Meat and Poultry Acts (1967–68); the Egg Products Inspection Act (1970) *(book)*.
  - **FSMA (2011)** shifts to prevention *(book)*:
    - The FDA can order mandatory recalls.
    - Risk-based inspection frequency.
    - Stronger oversight of imports (about 15% of US food is imported, including about 50% of fresh fruit, 20% of fresh vegetables and 80% of seafood).
    - A **Produce Safety Rule**: agricultural water tested for generic *E. coli*; 120-day interval after raw manure for crops in contact with soil (90 days if not); rules for sprouts, animal intrusion and worker hygiene.
    - The **Preventive Controls for Human Food Rule**: a written **food safety plan** with hazard analysis and risk-based preventive controls (process, allergen, sanitation and supply-chain controls), monitoring, corrective actions, verification (validation; environmental monitoring for ready-to-eat foods exposed after processing), recall plan, records, and reanalysis at least every 3 years. A "preventive controls qualified individual" oversees it.
- **Outbreak investigation** *(book)*:
  1. Detect an increase in cases, and confirm it is real rather than an artefact of more testing.
  2. Write a case definition.
  3. Find cases (descriptive epidemiology: time, place, person).
  4. Form hypotheses.
  5. Test them (case–control or cohort studies, food histories).
  6. Do lab work (PulseNet matching) and traceback.
  7. Apply control measures (recall, closure).
  8. Communicate.
  - Modern outbreaks are often diffuse and multistate, from low-level contamination of widely distributed commercial products.
- **Food defense/bioterrorism:** layers of prevention, detection and response *(book)*.
- **Consumer rules:** clean, separate, cook, chill. Cook to safe internal temperatures: 145 °F for whole cuts with a rest, 160 °F for ground meats, 165 °F for poultry and leftovers (USDA guidance, as cited in the book) *(book/std)*.
- **International:** the Codex Alimentarius sets standards to protect health and fair trade; global initiatives harmonize approaches *(book)*.
- **Infectious dose figures** are extrapolations from outbreaks or volunteer studies on healthy adults. Treat them cautiously, as worst-case estimates that vary with host, food and strain *(book)*.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *S. aureus* | Intoxication: heat-stable enterotoxin preformed in food | ~2–6 h; the book's example averages 4.4 h | Sudden vomiting, cramps; little fever; short | Handled cooked foods: ham, salads, cream pastries | Hand hygiene, chilling; reheating won't destroy the toxin |
| *B. cereus*, emetic | Intoxication: cereulide, heat stable | ~0.5–6 h *(book: rapid)* | Vomiting | Fried or held rice, pasta, starchy foods | Cool rice fast; don't hold warm |
| *B. cereus*, diarrheal | Toxicoinfection: heat-labile enterotoxin | 6–14 h *(book)* | Diarrhea, cramps; < 24 h | Meats, gravies, sauces, dairy, vegetables | Rapid cooling, hot holding; ~10⁵–10⁶ cells/g needed |
| *C. perfringens* type A | Toxicoinfection: enterotoxin released when it sporulates in the gut | ~8–16 h *(std)* | Diarrhea, cramps; usually no vomiting or fever | Large batches of cooked meat, poultry, gravy, stews cooled slowly | Rapid cooling, hot holding ≥ 140 °F, reheat to 165 °F; needs ~10⁶+ cells/g |
| *C. botulinum* | Intoxication: neurotoxin types A–G (A, B, E, F in humans) | 12–36 h, range hours to days *(book)* | Double vision, trouble swallowing, descending flaccid paralysis; can be fatal | Home-canned low-acid foods, improperly held potatoes (foil-baked), garlic in oil, fermented fish; honey in infants | 12D canning, pH ≤ 4.6, salt or nitrite, refrigeration for group II; no honey under 1 year |

| EHEC / STEC (*E. coli* O157:H7 and non-O157) | Infection, Shiga toxins | ~3–4 days, range 1–8 *(std)* | Bloody diarrhea (hemorrhagic colitis); HUS (kidney failure), especially in children | Undercooked ground beef, raw milk, unpasteurized juice, leafy greens, sprouts, flour or cookie dough, water, animal contact | Cook ground beef to 160 °F, pasteurize, GAPs; < 100 cells *(book)* |
| *L. monocytogenes* | Infection, intracellular | Days to weeks (up to ~70 days for invasive disease) *(std)* | Mild flu-like illness in healthy people; septicemia, meningitis, miscarriage or stillbirth in pregnancy | Ready-to-eat deli meats, hot dogs, soft cheeses (especially from raw milk), smoked seafood, cut melon, sprouts | Grows in the fridge; environmental monitoring, post-lethality treatments, avoid recontamination, advice for high-risk groups |
| *Salmonella* (non-typhoidal) | Infection | ~6–72 h *(std)* | Diarrhea, fever, cramps; occasionally invasive | Poultry, eggs (*S.* Enteritidis inside shells), meats, produce, peanut butter, low-moisture foods, pet and reptile contact | Cook, chill, separate; on-farm control; low dose possible |
| *Shigella* | Infection | ~1–3 days *(std)* | Dysentery (bloody, mucus diarrhea), fever | Foods handled by infected workers (salads), water | Hand hygiene, exclude ill workers; as few as 100 cells *(book)* |
| *V. parahaemolyticus* | Infection | ~4–24 h *(std)* | Watery diarrhea, cramps | Raw or undercooked seafood, especially oysters | Cook seafood; chill quickly; warm-water months are riskiest *(book)* |

## §3 Bacterial pathogens in detail

**Bacillus cereus (Ch 8).**
- Gram-positive, spore-forming, from soil and widespread.
- It belongs to the *B. cereus* group with *B. anthracis* (plasmid toxin, anthrax), *B. thuringiensis* (insecticidal toxin crystals), *B. mycoides* and *B. weihenstephanensis* (psychrotrophic) *(book)*.
- Two illnesses *(book)*:
  - **Emetic:** cereulide, a heat-stable peptide made by nonribosomal synthesis in the food, linked to starchy foods.
  - **Diarrheal:** heat-labile enterotoxins made in the gut, linked to protein foods.
- Dose about 10⁵–10⁸ organisms *(std; the book's figure is illegible)*.
- Illness is under-reported because it is short (under 24 h) and resembles other illnesses *(book)*.
- Spores survive pasteurization; psychrotrophic strains can grow in refrigerated milk near the end of shelf life *(book)*.
- Some *Bacillus* strains are used as probiotics (e.g. *B. coagulans*) *(book)*.

**Campylobacter (Ch 9).**
- Gram-negative, curved or spiral, **microaerophilic**, with an optimum of **42 °C**. It doesn't grow at room temperature or in air, yet survives in cold, moist foods better at 4 °C than at 25 °C *(book)*.
- Very sensitive to acid, drying and freezing (freezing cuts numbers on poultry) *(book)*.
- Main reservoir is poultry. The book estimates about 845,000 US infections a year *(book)*.
- Outbreaks come from raw milk (more than half of outbreaks from 1998 to 2012), poultry and chicken liver, and water. Sporadic cases peak in summer *(book)*.
- Complications: reactive arthritis, Guillain–Barré syndrome *(book)*.
- Fluoroquinolone resistance led the FDA to ban enrofloxacin in poultry in 2005 *(book)*.
- VBNC in water *(book)*.

**Clostridium botulinum (Ch 10).**
- Anaerobic sporeformer making the most potent known toxin (lethal dose about ng/kg) *(book)*.
- **Forms of botulism:**
  - Foodborne: home canning (e.g. a 2015 Ohio church potluck outbreak from home-canned potatoes) *(book)*.
  - Infant: intestinal colonization. Avoid honey and other raw agricultural products for infants, as the American Academy of Pediatrics advises *(book)*.
  - Wound.
  - Adult intestinal.
- **Groups:**
  - Group I (proteolytic): mesophilic, heat-resistant spores, minimum pH 4.6, minimum a_w about 0.94, minimum temperature about 10 °C *(std)*.
  - Group II (nonproteolytic, including type E in fish and aquatic environments): grows down to about 3.3 °C *(std)*, has less heat-resistant spores, minimum a_w about 0.97 *(std)*.
- **Controls:**
  - 12D thermal processing.
  - pH < 4.6.
  - Brine concentration, i.e. salt in the water phase (salt acts mainly by lowering a_w) *(book)*.
  - Nitrite.
  - Refrigeration plus a second barrier for refrigerated low-acid foods.
  - High pressure combined with heat is now FDA-approved for commercial sterilization *(book)*.
- **Treatment:** antitoxin and ventilation have cut the fatality rate to about 10% or less *(book)*.
- The organism is a select agent and is handled at BSL-3 *(book)*.

**Clostridium perfringens (Ch 11).**
- Anaerobic sporeformer, widespread in soil and intestines.
- Grows extremely fast in warm cooked protein foods: generation times as short as about 7–10 min near 43–45 °C *(std)*.
- **Type A food poisoning:**
  - Cooking kills competitors, activates spores and drives off oxygen. Slow cooling or warm holding then lets spores germinate and multiply.
  - Large numbers eaten (about 10⁶–10⁷ or more per g) sporulate in the gut and release enterotoxin (CPE, from the *cpe* gene; it is a paracrystalline protein released when cells lyse after sporulating) *(book)*.
  - Symptoms are mostly diarrhea *(book)*.
- **Prevention:** cool cooked foods quickly; hold hot; reheat thoroughly *(book)*.
- Toxin typing (A–E) is based on the major lethal toxins *(book)*.

**Enterohemorrhagic E. coli (Ch 12).**
- **Cattle** are the major reservoir; **undercooked ground beef** is the single most frequent vehicle *(book)*.
- Other vehicles: raw milk, juice, produce, sprouts, water, petting zoos, raw cookie dough or flour.
- **Low dose (< 100 cells)** allows person-to-person spread *(book)*.
- **Acid tolerance** is a key trait *(book)*.
- **Virulence:** Shiga toxins (Stx1, Stx2); attaching-and-effacing lesions from the LEE pathogenicity island (intimin); the pO157 plasmid *(book)*.
- **HUS** mainly affects children and the elderly.
- O157:H7 is distinguished by **not fermenting sorbitol** and **lacking β-glucuronidase** *(book)*.
- Non-O157 STEC are increasingly linked to HUS *(book)*.
- The other diarrheagenic *E. coli* categories are ETEC, EPEC, EIEC, EAEC and DAEC *(book)*.

**Listeria monocytogenes (Ch 13).**
- Gram-positive, facultatively intracellular; **psychrotrophic** (grows at refrigeration temperatures); salt tolerant; forms biofilms in processing plants *(book)*.
- Carried asymptomatically by many healthy people and animals *(book)*.
- **Listeriosis:**
  - Rare but severe.
  - At-risk groups: pregnant women (miscarriage, stillbirth, neonatal infection), newborns, elderly and immunocompromised people.
  - Mild flu-like illness in others *(book)*.
- Ready-to-eat foods are the concern (hot dogs, deli meats, soft cheeses, smoked fish), especially products contaminated after processing. It grows faster in pasteurized milk contaminated afterwards, where there are fewer competitors *(book)*.
- **US regulatory status:** zero tolerance in ready-to-eat foods (vs a 100 CFU/g approach in some countries) *(book/std)*. The CDC advises pregnant women and other at-risk groups to avoid certain foods (unpasteurized soft cheeses; deli meats and hot dogs unless steaming hot) *(book)*.
- **Virulence:** internalins for entry; listeriolysin O to escape the vacuole; ActA for actin-based cell-to-cell spread, controlled by PrfA *(std)*.

**Salmonella (Ch 14).**
- Gram-negative rods; two species, with many serovars (e.g. *S. enterica* serovar Enteritidis, Typhimurium) *(book)*.
- Typhoid and paratyphoid (Typhi, Paratyphi) are human-adapted *(book)*.
- **Reservoirs:** animal intestines. Poultry and eggs are the principal vehicles; *S.* Enteritidis can be inside intact eggs (transovarian) and is hard to control in flocks *(book)*. Other vehicles are produce, nuts, peanut butter and low-moisture foods (high survival when dry), and pork (e.g. a 2015 *S.* Infantis outbreak traced by PulseNet) *(book)*.
- Adapts to salt and low pH (acid tolerance response); antibiotic-resistant strains are linked to agricultural antibiotic use *(book)*.
- Epidemiological evidence suggests a **low infectious dose** *(book)*.
- **Virulence:** invasion genes on pathogenicity islands (SPI-1, SPI-2), virulence plasmids, invasion and survival in host cells *(book)*.

**Shigella (Ch 15).**
- Human-only reservoir, so transmission is fecal–oral via infected food handlers, water and person-to-person.
- **About 100 cells** can cause illness *(book)*.
- Invades colonic cells and spreads cell to cell (bacillary dysentery). *S. dysenteriae* type 1 makes Shiga toxin.
- **Control:** hygiene; exclude ill workers until stools are negative *(book)*.

**Staphylococcus aureus (Ch 16).**
- Carried in the nose and on the skin of many people.
- Food poisoning is an **intoxication** with heat-stable **enterotoxins** (SEA–SEE and more) preformed in food. Onset is rapid (the book's case: 6–10 h; average incubation 4.4 h) *(book)*.
- Tolerates salt and low a_w (grows to about 0.86, but toxin needs about 0.93 or more) *(book)*. Competes poorly with other flora, so cooked foods recontaminated by handlers are typical vehicles.
- Detection is by the coagulase test and enterotoxin immunoassays *(std)*.
- **Control:** hygiene; keep foods out of the danger zone; starter cultures acidify fast in cheese and fermented sausage *(book)*.

**Vibrio (Ch 17).**
- Marine, halophilic Gram-negatives. Counts in water peak in warm weather *(book)*.
- *V. parahaemolyticus* causes gastroenteritis from raw shellfish.
- *V. vulnificus* causes primary septicemia: capsule essential, needs high serum iron; it is the cause of **95% of seafood-related deaths in the US** *(book)*.
- *V. cholerae* O1/O139 causes cholera and is uncommon in the US *(book)*.
- Other species: *V. fluvialis*, *V. furnissii*, *V. hollisae*, *V. alginolyticus* *(book)*.
- **Control:** proper thermal processing kills vibrios *(book)*. Post-harvest processing of oysters (HPP, freezing, irradiation) and rapid chilling.

**Yersinia enterocolitica (Ch 18).**
- **Psychrotrophic** Gram-negative; swine are the main reservoir (pork, chitterlings), plus raw milk and water.
- Symptoms can mimic appendicitis; reactive arthritis can follow *(std)*.
- Grows in refrigerated foods; heat sensitive.

## §4 Reasoning from an outbreak

1. **Onset within about 1–6 h, mainly vomiting:** a preformed toxin (*S. aureus*, emetic *B. cereus*). Ask about handled foods held warm, and rice.
2. **About 8–16 h, diarrhea without vomiting or fever:** *C. perfringens* or diarrheal *B. cereus*. Ask about large batches of cooked meat or gravy cooled slowly.
3. **About 1–3 days with fever and diarrhea:** *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, *Shigella*, *Vibrio*, norovirus (12–48 h, much vomiting).
4. **Bloody diarrhea plus HUS risk:** STEC (O157:H7). Ask about ground beef, produce, raw milk, flour.
5. **Neurological signs (double vision, slurred speech, descending paralysis):** botulism, a medical emergency.
6. **Pregnancy, older adults or immunocompromised people, with fever, sepsis or meningitis after days to weeks:** *Listeria*.
7. **Weeks, with jaundice:** hepatitis A (15–50 days *(book)*). **Prolonged watery diarrhea:** *Cyclospora* or *Cryptosporidium*.

Always: preserve leftover food (unopened product helps prove the source *(book)*), report to the health department and see a clinician.

## §5 Parasites, viruses, prions (Ch 23–24)

**Parasites** *(book unless noted)*:
- **Protozoa** have complex life cycles and are spread as (oo)cysts.
  - *Cryptosporidium* and *Giardia* are infectious when shed. One protozoan in the book (most likely *Giardia*; the OCR is unclear) infects with about 10–25 cysts, incubates 3–25 days (median 7–10) and causes illness that can last weeks.
  - *Cyclospora cayetanensis* and *Toxoplasma* oocysts must sporulate in the environment first. Fresh produce (raspberries, basil, snow peas, leafy greens) has carried *Cyclospora*.
  - *Toxoplasma gondii*: undercooked meat (tissue cysts), cat feces (oocysts) and congenital transmission.
- **Helminths:**
  - *Trichinella* (pork and wild game), *Taenia* tapeworms, *Anisakis* (raw fish) and *Fasciola* (watercress). Clinical signs can be vague.
  - Killed by heat and by freezing (freeze fish intended to be eaten raw; *std*).
- Preharvest contamination comes from livestock, soil and water. Use USDA cooking temperatures and good hygiene.

**Viruses** *(book)*:
- Obligate intracellular and host-specific (ligand–receptor binding). They **cannot grow in food** but persist on it, and cause a large share of foodborne illness.
- Dilution in water doesn't make them safe, because the infectious dose is tiny. Norovirus may need only about 18 particles.
- **Norovirus** is the leading cause of foodborne illness: vomiting and diarrhea for 1–3 days, spread by infected food handlers, shellfish and produce. It binds histo-blood-group carbohydrates.
- **Hepatitis A** incubates 15–50 days; people shed it about 10–14 days *before* symptoms. Vehicles are produce, shellfish and handlers. A vaccine exists.
- **Rotavirus** incubates 3–4 days, with diarrhea lasting 2–3 days, mainly in children.
- Detection relies on molecular methods (RT-PCR). Classic culture is impractical for norovirus.
- **Bacteriophages** are used as biocontrol (e.g. anti-*Listeria* phage preparations) and for microbial source tracking.

**Prions** *(book)*:
- Misfolded proteins cause transmissible spongiform encephalopathies: BSE ("mad cow"; the book's timeline notes the first US report; 2003 *(std)*) and variant CJD in humans after eating BSE-contaminated tissue. They have long incubation periods.
- They resist normal cooking and sterilization. Control relies on feed bans and removal of specified risk materials.
