# The Sixteen Other Entrepreneurs (Ch 5–20)

Chapter subtitles come from the book's contents page *(book)*. Details are from the OCR. Some left-hand pages were blurred, so a few stories are summarized more thinly. Each lesson is Levitt's point or a close paraphrase of it.

| Ch | Founder | Business and angle | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | **Arthur Imperatore** | "Obsessive drive converts an Army truck into a transportation empire." The son of immigrants started with two under-used Army-surplus trucks owned by his brothers. A-P-A Transport became the third most profitable trucking company in the US *(book)*. | **He had no angle,** so this doesn't follow Levitt's formula. His all-consuming drive alone carried the business: "without this all-consuming drive, few new business ideas, even brilliant ones, would get off the ground" *(book)*. |
| 6 | **Joseph (Joe) Kohler** | "A 'Burma Shave' advertising twist worth $2.5 million." He remembered Burma Shave roadside signs. He put tasteful, changeable ad signs on ski-lift towers, each with a **ski-safety slogan** ("Picture Yourself Skiing Safely—Kodak"; "Leave the Hotdogging to Us—French's Mustard"). Ski areas got cash and a safety message *(book)*. | **A win for everyone:** the ski area got revenue and safety, the advertiser got a captive audience. Earlier, at a ski club, he had been given "responsibility without the necessary authority; an impossible situation" *(book)*. |
| 7 | **Julian Brodie** | "Expert counseling for the new retiree market, at wholesale." He saw his own parents unprepared for retirement. His idea was pre-retirement counseling **sold to corporate employers** for their staff *(book)*. | **A need personally observed.** Sell to the payer who reaches many customers at once (wholesale), not to individuals one at a time. |
| 8 | **Kenneth Jay Lane** | "An eye for fashion trends finds a gold mine in rhinestones." He trained in taste, not business. Fake diamonds, emeralds and pearls for "the beautiful people" brought about $20 million a year *(book)*. | **Aptitude and taste can be the angle.** He stumbled onto a need. |
| 9 | **Stew Leonard** | "Selling milk and groceries in a 'Magic Kingdom' setting." The son of a milkman built one store in Norwalk, Connecticut. It has a single one-way aisle past every product and only **600 items** (against about 14,000 in a supermarket), with **$80 million a year in sales** *(book)*. | **Turn a failing family business into something of value.** A focused range plus a showmanship experience. A drive "to be somebody" *(book)*. |
| 10 | **Dean Sloane** | "Delivering sophisticated health care in a profitable new way." A CPA who left accounting. He built an innovative post-cardiac health-care facility with doctors as participating partners *(book)*. | **Financial skill plus a partnership structure** that aligns professionals' interests. |
| 11 | **Glenn Bernbaum** | "Snob appeal made Mortimer's the playpen for the rich and famous." Formerly Custom Shop's chief operating officer, 1959–79. He opened a restaurant because "Why should I drink at someone else's bar when I can drink at my own?" *(book)* | **No classic angle,** but an exclusive, snob-appeal atmosphere. Levitt notes he ignored Levitt's advice in places *(book)*. |
| 12 | **Steve Israel** | Great American Salvage Company: architectural artifacts rescued from demolition. A Vermont warehouse plus stores in New York and elsewhere *(book)*. | **See value in what others throw away.** Levitt calls him "not at all a businessman", a romantic, yet he did extraordinarily well *(book)*. |
| 13 | **Mike Weatherly** | "Carving out a fortune with the Swiss Army knife." A Princeton and Harvard MBA with a "bumpy" career. He bought the Forschner Company, sales $4M and profit $300k, for about $2M (about 7× earnings), largely with borrowed money. Swiss Army knife sales grew **from $1M (1974) to $20M (1986)**, and cutlery from $3M to $10M *(book)*. | **Buy a small business with an under-exploited brand and grow it.** Contacts and persistence count. Ask the subliminal reason for a sale: the seller was aging, and the dollar had fallen about 27%, raising import costs *(book)*. |
| 14 | **Gene Ballin** | "A true entrepreneur invents his own…" An inventor who built houses for a flat fee and held several patents, including a plastics container business (Tyz-All Plastics). Happenstance, such as a strike, damaged several ventures *(book)*. | **Resilience:** "picking himself up, shaking himself off, and starting all over again. It's impossible to overrate such a quality" *(book)*. |
| 15 | **James McManus** | Marketing Corporation of America (MCA), founded 1971: "a money-making smorgasbord of marketing, advertising, and management". Twelve companies, each under its own president, with over $400M in revenue (1986). A generous local philanthropist *(book)*. | Separate companies, each with a president. **The Ten Commandments** are listed below *(book)*. |
| 16 | **William (Bill) Raveis** | "Scratch an entrepreneur and you will find an ego." Westinghouse never credited him for a computer program he created. Hurt, he vowed never to work for anyone again and went into real estate with no experience. He built a computerized brokerage with an executive relocation service *(book)*. | **Ego as fuel,** plus brains, imagination and energy. Levitt asks whether he should have kept his job and explored real estate part-time first, or worked for a broker for experience *(book)*. |
| 17 | **Al (musician)** | "A musician's virtuoso venture into radio programming." A TV-theme composer revived the big-band sound for older listeners, using **electronically cleaned-up** original recordings *(book)*. | **Serve an audience the market has abandoned.** Older listeners were ignored by stations chasing 18–49-year-olds *(book)*. |
| 18 | **Ed Mitchell** | "A retail clothier brings in the crowds with a warm family touch." Mitchells of Westport, Connecticut, run by Ed and his sons *(book)*. | **The owner's personal presence and warmth.** Levitt admits he couldn't recreate this across 81 stores *(book)*. |
| 19 | **George Lindemann** | "Filling an old prescription in a lucrative new way." He bought three pharmaceutical companies *first*, then searched for a marketable product. Levitt calls it "an incredible pharmaceutical marketing ploy" *(book)*. | **The angle can come after the purchase,** but that is the riskier path. |
| 20 | **Julee Rosso and Sheila Lukins** | The Silver Palate: "gourmet takeout, palatable at any price" *(book)*. | See the steps below. |

**How The Silver Palate grew** *(book)*:
- They started from their own need to entertain stylishly without cooking.
- Saks and Crate & Barrel put their preserves in catalogues. The decorative European jars could be reused as vases.
- With no advertising budget they used publicity, holding luncheons for food editors, and gave away recipes to build word of mouth.
- A *Vogue* editor suggested a cookbook, which sold "a couple of million" copies. It was the first American cookbook translated into French.
- They sold without a distributor and used telemarketing with their 2,000 retailers. They opened in Tokyo in 1986.
- Lessons: publicity over paid advertising, generosity (give away recipes), and products that are easy for the customer to use successfully.

## McManus's "Ten Commandments" (Ch 15)

[all *(book)*]
1. With whom is more important than where.
2. The more you give, the more you get.
3. Make a commitment to the development of new [ventures/ideas]. *(The last word is cut off in the OCR.)*
4. Put product before profits.
5. You have permission to fail.
6. Spend money to make money.
7. Insist on individual accountability and authority.
8. Everybody profits.
9. Be the best.
10. Have fun.

## Patterns across the cases

| Pattern | Founders |
|---|---|
| Angle came from a personal need | Levitt, Brodie, Leonard, Rosso and Lukins |
| Angle found after starting | Imperatore, Bernbaum, Raveis, Lindemann, Lane |
| Bought an existing business and grew it | Weatherly (Forschner), Lindemann (drug companies) |
| Selling to the payer or wholesale | Brodie (employers), Kohler (advertisers and ski areas) |
| Publicity instead of advertising | Silver Palate; Levitt's own ads never paid |
| Persistence through happenstance | Ballin, Weatherly, Raveis |
| Owner's personal touch versus scale | Ed Mitchell against The Custom Shop's turnover problem |
| Responsibility without authority fails | Kohler at the ski club; Commandment 7 |
