## §1 The one important question (Ch 1)

- **The question:** *Why should customers come to me for their goods/service/whatever, instead of continuing to go where they have been going?* Don't open a new business unless you have studied that question seriously and come up with a serious answer *(book)*.
- **What he means by a business:** not "the typical mom-and-pop store", such as a dry cleaner, stationery store or hardware store. That is just "an alternative way of making a living". You are the boss, but you work much harder for an income no better than middle management *(book)*.
  - He is interested in "sleepers": small idea businesses that produce considerable income for the owner, and ego satisfaction as a bonus. Clearing "a million or two, pretax, in a business of modest size, is not unusual" *(book)*.
- **Examples of answers:**

  | Founder | Answer |
  |---|---|
  | Levitt | A custom shirt fitted to the neck and shoulders for the price of a ready-made one |
  | Brodie | Pre-retirement counseling, sold to employers |
  | Stew Leonard | A "Magic Kingdom" food store |
  | Rosso and Lukins | Gourmet takeout for busy women who entertain |

  [all *(book)*]
- **Founders with no answer** also appear, to show the range *(book)*:
  - Bernbaum: "Why should I drink at someone else's bar when I can drink at my own?"
  - Imperatore: had two under-used Army trucks.
  - Ballin: built a house for a flat fee, saving the customer the usual 15% overhead and 10% profit.
  - Lane: stumbled onto a need.
  - Raveis: refused ever to work for anyone again.
  - Lindemann: bought three drug companies, then searched for a product.

  Each "latched onto an angle well after he started". "There is no single right or wrong scenario."
- **Small ideas can be enough.** Gallup suspected the *Literary Digest* poll, which relied on telephones that only upper-income homes had in 1936. He polled a representative sample and correctly predicted Roosevelt's re-election *(book)*.

## §2 Who will succeed (Ch 1)

- **The credo:** "Success in business is not for the greedy—on the contrary; success results from giving more without charging more, and the possibilities are infinite" *(book)*.
- **Common causes of failure:**
  - undercapitalization (the most popular);
  - an inclination to gamble;
  - inability to delegate authority;
  - unwillingness to listen to others;
  - failure to attract or nurture managerial talent;
  - failure to monitor changing trends.

  In his opinion, though, most fail because "they have no substantive answer" to the one question *(book)*.
- **An answer is only the beginning.** It must be backed by obsessive motivation, and you need the **complete package**: presentation, service, product, finance, timing and execution. Then there is the **X factor** *(book)*.
- **Aptitude matters.** McEnroe, Olivier and Iacocca are "extraordinarily gifted" in their areas, not just trained *(book)*.
- **Credibility:**
  - Iacocca turned Chrysler around by promoting its engineering heritage, backing it with a 5-year/50,000-mile guarantee and his personal credibility *(book)*.
  - DeLorean's presentation didn't persuade Levitt: "His shirt collar was too flamboyant! As it turned out, so was his lifestyle" *(book)*.
- **Energy:**
  - The common element of all seventeen founders is "unrelenting motivation… a will to shine, not merely survive". "Talent, like the heart muscle, diminishes without steady exercise." "There is no such thing as a nine-to-five entrepreneur." Be prepared to put your idea ahead of sports, culture "and even family" *(book)*.
  - Levitt admits his own "seemingly languid example": he retired from day-to-day operations in 1941, four years after starting *(book)*.
  - The Samuel LeFrak *Who's Who* entry shows the energy of the competitors you will face *(book)*.

## §3 The Custom Shop (Ch 2–3)

**Background** *(book)*:
- He grew up poor in Brooklyn, dropped out of high school after failing languages, and became an office boy at 16.
- He then sold fabric (Everfast). He lost his motivation and was fired from four jobs out of four, "obviously not an ideal background".
- He was a customer of Mermelstein, a small Lower East Side shirtmaker, because ready-made shirts didn't fit his neck and shoulders.

**The April 1937 calculation, done on his fingers** *(book)*:

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Mermelstein's charge for "cut, make and trim" | $1.25 (estimated real cost ≈ 75¢) |
| Fabric per shirt | 3 yards at 18¢ a yard = 54¢ |
| Levitt's cost per shirt | ≈ $1.30 |
| Retail price | ~$2.00, set at **$2.15 for three or more** |
| Comparison prices | Arrow ready-made $1.85; upstairs custom $6–$10 |

There was **no inventory risk**: "Wow!"

**How the idea worked** *(book)*:
1. **Bought direct from the mills** in full pieces, not from jobbers. It saved 40% on fabric. Two mills agreed after he explained his plan for 35 stores.
2. **Three-shirt minimum.** It cut cutting and administrative costs and raised the size of each sale.
3. **Cash and carry,** so no credit losses.
4. **Volume:** he needed about 2,000 shirts a week for an efficient factory, so he planned a chain.
5. **Prime street locations in tiny spaces.** Rent was low because there was no inventory. Rival shirtmakers worked upstairs.
6. **His own factory from the start,** saving the contractor's profit. Shirts were cut individually, then sewn on an **assembly line of 31 operations**, like Ford's. Rivals used one skilled operator per shirt.
7. **Customers' deposits** funded the work, since nothing was cut without one.

**The start** *(book)*:
- His savings fell from $10,000 to $1,000 in the August 1937 market crash. He borrowed against his life insurance.
- He opened a tiny shop, 7 feet wide, with this window copy: "We have devised a method whereby we can afford to custom-make shirts… at $2.15, provided three or more shirts are ordered."
- His first customer gave a $10 bill and he had no change. Early problems included late shirts, poor fit and mixed-up collar orders.

**What made it last** *(book)*:
- **The angle:** the only extra cost of custom work is cutting individually and about 30 minutes of measuring. The three-shirt price covers the measuring, and bypassing the manufacturer's profit covers the cutting.
- **A service business, not a shirt business:** "a 'make the customer look better' service". That means fit that compensates for the body, collars designed for each face and neck, and teaching image and color coordination.
- **The package:**
  - The striped-dome awning logo.
  - Elegant stores with walnut walls and red velvet.
  - Five months of training before staff may take measurements.
  - Collars in 5 back heights × 4 front heights.
  - Customers try collars on and get a fitting before the shirt is made, "like a Polaroid camera".
- **Stayed within what they did best.** No socks, underwear or pajamas. Women's blouses were added in 1947, and custom suits in 1977 as a defensive move when men stopped wearing ties. Suits raised volume 25% and used the same mill-direct advantage.
- **No partner:**
  - Andrew Seligson worked for two months, declared Levitt "bankrupt" and demanded half the whole business for $5,000.
  - Levitt refused: "It's my idea, and it's my business."
  - The partner later made him two offers he called "not equitable". By turning one down, he says, he "saved $50 million".
- **Competitors failed** for these reasons *(book)*:
  - The Shirt Gallery (International Industries) opened 31 stores, many franchised, and folded in four years. Its costs were higher because the factory had to profit from franchisees. Staff had only two weeks of training, there was "no taste", and it was undercapitalized ($75k per store against his $150k).
  - Genesco: its prices were 30% higher because its factory also had to make a profit.
  - W. R. Grace: lost about $6 million on mail order and closed after four years.

## §4 Lessons in failure (Ch 4)

**1. Advertising didn't pay** *(book)*:
- A *New York Times* ad cost **$84 to bring in one new customer**. The average sale was $200 with $20 pretax profit.
- He tested 8 approaches in 50 cities in 1985–86 with the same result. He opened 50 out-of-town stores without an opening ad.
- "How could it be that the whole world advertised, most of it pretending a competitive advantage that did not truly exist?"

**2. Direct mail, fixed by a simple test** *(book)*:
- The 12-page color catalogue cost about $80,000 per mailing for $100,000 in sales. He had kept doing it for years without checking.
- He cut it to custom shirtings only, halving the cost to $40k, and used the savings for a **10% discount on mail reorders**.
- Mail sales jumped **from $100,000 to $700,000**.
- Lesson: test, measure, and pass savings on to the customer.

**3. Naming** *(book)*:
- "The Custom Shop" is impersonal. People recall it wrongly as "The Custom Shirt Shop", and it sounds like an "English Tea Shoppe".
- A personal name such as "David London, Shirtmaker" would have been better.
- The awning trademark rescued recognition.

**4. The window ad's "devised a method"** invited competitors to spread rumors that the shirts weren't really custom-made. Even after investigators from the *Times*, the Better Business Bureau and the FTC confirmed 13 individual measurements, the myth persisted *(book)*.
   - Lesson: don't hint at a secret your rivals can twist.

**5. Succession and the chief operating officer** *(book)*:
- From 1941 to 1959 he trusted the business to three men who lacked merchandising experience.
- It took 18 years to find a pro (Glenn Bernbaum), then three more years to find Anthony Bergamo.
- He still had no understudy for Bergamo, and admits the business is "not safe" without one.
- Lesson: develop a second-in-command early.

**6. People turnover** *(book)*:
- He needed about 12 new managers and 12 assistants a year. To get 24 he had to hire 72, and only 6 lasted five years.
- Customers often poached his well-trained staff, and competitors poached plant managers with 50% raises.
- With 81 stores he couldn't recreate the family warmth of an owner-run store (Ed Mitchell's, the original Neiman-Marcus).
- He found union dynamics adversarial (his opinion). He admired McDonald's systems and the "family" culture of the Friendly chain.

**7. Customers undo the service.** They ruin shirts with cheap laundering and resist advice on color coordination. Reorders were lower than he expected *(book)*.

**8. His "biggest and most disappointing failure":**
- He offered companies a 2½-hour image consultation for each executive, a full wardrobe plan, and a 20% discount. The company would pay $100, and Levitt took a loss to build "missionaries".
- After almost ten years it was "a complete and total failure". Executives avoid the subject of dress *(book)*.
- Lesson: a good idea fails if customers don't want the behavior change it needs.

**His summary:** "We are in the image business, a service business. If we don't improve the image of our customer, he goes elsewhere" *(book)*.

## §5 Conclusions (Ch 22)

**Generalizations** *(book)*:
1. **The basic concept must be sound.** Do "a fair amount of research" to make sure your angle is unique.
2. **There will always be a lot of competition.** The more successful you are, the more people will copy you, "perhaps do it even better".
3. **Ego, self-confidence and drive** let a founder push on relentlessly. Examples are Levitt refusing to sell out to Seligson, Imperatore's drive, and Weatherly, Ballin, Raveis and Kohler persisting.
4. **Happenstance will play a part,** good or bad.

**His happenstance examples** *(book)*:

| Investment | What happened |
|---|---|
| 550 acres in San Mateo County, 1962, $1.3M | Rezoned from 5-acre to 100-acre lots, and the planned road was never built; dead money for 25 years |
| Armonk airport land, $5,000 an acre | IBM built next door, then the state condemned 14 acres at $125,000 an acre |
| Fifth Avenue building, 1953, $325,000 | Sold 30 years later for $9M: "No brains, just happenstance" |
| DePinna Building, bought for $5.5M | Zoning delays; sold to the Shah of Iran for $8.6M. Later the land alone was worth about $25M and the building about $60M |

Rising New York real-estate values, driven by foreign capital and corporate moves to the suburbs, helped Raveis, Ed Mitchell and Stew Leonard *(book)*.

**Closing thoughts** *(book)*:
- "Endless trouble is the price we all must pay for the gift of life." The best way to handle trouble is "to relax and face the situation and do what needs to be done".
- To readers who go ahead: good luck, "hopefully, you will be the one entrepreneur who makes it, while the other four cry themselves to sleep". To those who keep their shirt by not going ahead: "congratulations".

**Stock market aside** *(book)*:
- "Stay out of the market. But if you must gamble, confine it to 20 percent, not more, of your capital."
- He was wiped out in 1929, 1932 and 1937, and sold everything in June 1986.
- Present this as his personal opinion, not investment advice.
