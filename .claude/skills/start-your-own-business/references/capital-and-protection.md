# Raising Capital, Protecting Your Idea, Structure, and the E.Q. Quiz (Ch 21)

*(book)* = as stated in the 1988 text. *(std, verify)* = current general knowledge, given only as a pointer. Check current SBA, USPTO, Copyright Office and IRS sources or a professional before relying on it. Never present the 1988 figures as current.

## §1 Sources of start-up capital

| Source | What Levitt says *(book)* | Current note *(std, verify)* |
|---|---|---|
| **Wealthy individuals ("angels")** | **His preference.** Approach them directly, by referral from your banker, lawyer or accountant. As an investor he wants: the founder risking **his own money** (a "determining factor"), an angle he likes, a serious person, and active management by the founder. | Angel groups and networks are now common. |
| **Venture capitalists** | Found through VC association directories. They prefer companies likely to go public or be acquired, and seek **40–60% annualized returns over 5–7 years**. | Return expectations and fund structures have changed. VC suits only high-growth businesses. |
| **SBA** | Most SBA loans are made by private lenders and guaranteed by the SBA: up to **90%, maximum guarantee $500,000**, with interest capped at 2¼–2¾ points over prime. You must first be turned down for a conventional loan. A small number of direct loans exist. | SBA 7(a), 504 and microloan programs now have different guarantee percentages, limits (7(a) up to $5M) and rate rules. Check sba.gov. |
| **SBICs and MESBICs** | Privately owned companies licensed by the SBA. Investments run from under $100k to over $1M, with management help. | SBICs still exist. MESBICs became "SSBICs" and were later folded in. |
| **Commercial banks** | Need security and guarantees. Expect prime plus 1–4 points. | Collateral and a personal guarantee are still typical. |
| **Business development corporations** | State-chartered private lenders. Flexible, creative packages, often SBA-guaranteed. | Varies by state. |
| **State venture-capital funds** | More than 20 states had programs for start-ups. Contact your state economic development office. | Many states have innovation funds. Also check federal SBIR and STTR grants. |
| **Public and private stock offerings** | Need experienced securities lawyers for federal law and state "blue-sky" laws. Some brokerages specialize in small offerings. | Crowdfunding (Reg CF) and Reg A+/D exemptions now exist. Get legal advice. |

**Business plan** *(book)*:
- You need one to borrow, and it must be custom-made. Write it **yourself**, because investors spot plans done by outsiders, and canned computer projections are a turnoff.
- Investors spend about **five minutes** screening a plan (Joseph Mancuso). Grab them with the **executive summary**.
- Sources he cites: Mancuso's *How to Write a Winning Business Plan*; Rich and Gumpert's *Business Plans That Win $$$*; free SBA Management Aids for retailers and service firms.

## §2 Testing and protecting your idea

**Test the market** *(book)*:
- Research what is already out there.
- Have the product rated at an **evaluation center**, for example the Wisconsin Innovation Service Center at UW–Whitewater, which cost about $100 for a detailed report.

**Confidentiality** *(book)*:
- There is no guarantee of confidentiality. Keep a **dated log** of your creative process and consider talking with a lawyer.
- Be wary of "invention brokers" who work on contingency.

| Protection | 1988 text *(book)* | Current note *(std, verify)* |
|---|---|---|
| **Utility patent** | For a new and useful process, machine, article or composition, or an improvement. Refused if obvious to a person skilled in the field. The hardest and most costly to get. Filing fees under $300 for individuals and small businesses, plus maintenance fees totaling $1,400. Search about $300+. Legal fees $2,500–3,500 or more. | Term about 20 years from filing. Fees are much higher now, with small and micro-entity discounts. **Provisional applications** are an option. Since 2013 the US is **first-inventor-to-file**, so filing early matters more than notebooks. Check uspto.gov. |
| **Design patent** | Covers appearance. Easier and cheaper, but may be of little value because a slight change avoids infringement. | Term 15 years from grant (for applications since 2015). |
| **Getting help** | Hire a patent lawyer. The PTO lists registered attorneys and agents. A free half-hour consultation was available through AIPLA's Inventor Consultation Service. Drafting the application yourself, then having a lawyer refine it, cuts fees. Book: MacCracken's *Handbook for Inventors*. | USPTO pro bono and Patent and Trademark Resource Centers. |
| **Before filing** | Keep daily work notes, witnessed by someone trustworthy who understands the invention but isn't involved. Mailing yourself a registered letter "does not help". Have two people sign an invention disclosure. The PTO **Disclosure Document Program** kept disclosures for 2 years, but it is not a substitute for filing. "Patent Pending" after filing deters copiers but gives no legal protection. | The Disclosure Document Program was **discontinued in 2007**. Use a provisional application instead. |
| **Copyright** | Protects original writings and similar works: life of the author plus 50 years (joint works: last survivor plus 50). Works for hire: 75 years from publication or 100 from creation, whichever is shorter. Registration through the Copyright Office, Library of Congress. | Now life **plus 70** years. Works for hire: **95** years from publication or 120 from creation. |
| **Trademark** | Protects names, symbols and distinctive packaging (for example a soft-drink bottle). Do a clearance search before use. PTO filing fee $175. Protection for 20 years, renewable every 20. | Registration is now renewable every **10 years**, and fees differ. Check uspto.gov (TEAS). |

## §3 Legal structure *(book)*

- **C corporation:** it protects your home, car and personal accounts from company creditors. But the corporation pays tax on profits. In 1985 a New York corporation might pay **58%** (48% federal plus about 10% city and state).
- **S corporation:** the same liability protection with no corporate tax; profits are taxed as personal income. "Decided advantage" in 1988's tax structure. Discuss with an attorney or accountant.
- **Current note** *(std, verify)*: the federal corporate rate is a flat **21%** since 2018. **LLCs** are now the most common small-business structure. The choice depends on taxes, payroll, investors and the state. Get professional advice.

## §4 The Entrepreneurial Quotient (E.Q.) quiz

The quiz was prepared by Northwestern Mutual Life. Levitt includes it "not suggesting that you take this test too seriously… interesting or, at the very least, amusing". It "can't predict your success"; entrepreneurial skills can be learned *(book)*.

**Example items**, scored plus or minus *(book)*:
- Child of immigrants: +1.
- Top student: −4 (entrepreneurs usually weren't).
- Enjoyed group activities: −1.
- Preferred to be alone as a youngster: +1.
- Started childhood enterprises (lemonade stands, newspaper routes): +2, otherwise −2.
- Stubborn child: plus.
- Cautious youngster: minus.
- The opinions of others matter a lot to you: −1.
- Tired of routine as a motive: +2.
- Enjoy work and will work overtime; "as long as it takes" with little sleep: +4 more.
- Start the next project immediately after finishing one: +2.
- Willing to spend your savings: +2. Willing to borrow from others too: +2.
- If the business fails, start another immediately: +4. Look for a well-paying job instead: minus.
- Believe entrepreneurs are "risky": scored (the value is cut off in the OCR).
- Write down long- and short-term goals: +1.
- Know more about cash flow than most people: +2.
- Easily bored: +2.
- Optimist: +2.

**Score bands, top to bottom** *(book)*:
- "Everything going for you": the highest scores.
- "Excellent chances".
- "A head start".
- "Might make a go of it if you work extra hard".
- Lowest scores: "talents probably lie elsewhere". Another kind of career, such as working for a company or developing a technical speciality, may suit you better.

Use the quiz as a conversation starter about motivation, risk tolerance and resilience, never as a verdict.
