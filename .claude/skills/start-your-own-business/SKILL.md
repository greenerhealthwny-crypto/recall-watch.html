# How to Start Your Own Business Without Losing Your Shirt (Mortimer Levitt, 1988)

A working guide distilled from Mortimer Levitt's 1988 book. Levitt was a high-school dropout who had been fired from four jobs. In 1937 he opened The Custom Shop with about $1,000 *(book)*. Fifty years later he owned 81 stores outright, with no partners, stockholders or franchises *(book)*.

The book has three parts:
- **Chapters 1–4:** his own story, his "elements of success" and his "lessons in failure".
- **Chapters 5–20:** sixteen other entrepreneurs, each with a different angle.
- **Chapters 21–22:** raising capital, protecting your idea, and his conclusions.

He wrote it for three reasons *(book)*:
1. To warn would-be founders. Four out of five new businesses fail, so the book may talk some readers out of it, and those readers keep their shirts.
2. Because the *Excellence* best-sellers by consultants offered "little practical value". He read 750 pages and learned nothing.
3. To tell the inside story of The Custom Shop, "warts and all".

## Levitt's core framework

1. **The one important question.** "Why should customers come to me for their goods/service/whatever, instead of continuing to go where they have been going?" In his view, most new businesses fail because they have no substantive answer. Don't open until you have a serious one *(book)*.
2. **Give more without charging more.** "Success in business is not for the greedy—on the contrary; success results from giving more without charging more, and the possibilities are infinite" *(book)*. His own angle was a custom-made shirt for about the price of a ready-made one: $2.15 against Arrow's $1.85 ready-made, when upstairs custom shirtmakers charged $6–10 *(book)*.
3. **The angle often comes from a need you personally observed.** Levitt had a skinny neck and broad shoulders. Brodie saw his aging parents struggle. Stew Leonard had his father's failing milk routes. Rosso and Lukins wanted to entertain without cooking *(book)*. Some founders found their angle only after starting, such as Imperatore, Bernbaum, Raveis and Lindemann.
4. **Obsessive drive.** "Motivation that is above and beyond the norm. It should be obsessive." Relentless motivation is the one element common to all seventeen founders. "There is no such thing as a nine-to-five entrepreneur" *(book)*.
5. **The complete package:** presentation, service, product, finance, timing and execution *(book)*.
6. **The X factor.** This is "the single, totally unexpected situation that no research can uncover". Examples are new legislation, a depression, confiscation by a foreign government, overproduction, or a new competitor with a better idea *(book)*. Happenstance cuts both ways; see his real-estate stories in `principles.md` §5.
7. **Competition is fierce, and capitalism is "strictly for players."** Success attracts imitators. More than 25 tried to copy The Custom Shop and failed, including three billion-dollar conglomerates *(book)*.
8. **Execution, credibility and constant watching.** "A good idea deserves constant watching" *(book)*. Iacocca's credibility made his guarantee believable, while DeLorean's flamboyance made him untrustworthy to Levitt *(book)*.

**Common reasons for failure** he lists *(book)*:
- undercapitalization (the most common);
- an inclination to gamble;
- an inability to delegate authority;
- an unwillingness to listen to the opinions of others;
- failure to attract and nurture managerial talent;
- failure to monitor changing trends;
- above all, no answer to the one important question.

**Workflow for evaluating someone's business idea:**
1. Make them answer the one question in a sentence. Push back if the answer is "better service" or "quality" with nothing concrete behind it.
2. Check whether the angle gives more without charging more, and the mechanism that makes it possible. Levitt bought from the mills, sold direct, had a three-shirt minimum, took cash only and used assembly-line sewing.
3. Walk through the complete package: presentation, service, product, finance, timing, execution.
4. Name likely competitors, imitators and X factors.
5. Check capital (undercapitalization) and the founder's drive and aptitude. The E.Q. quiz is a light-hearted prompt, not a predictor.
6. Find the case study closest to their situation and draw out its lesson.
7. Be candid. Levitt's own advice is that some people keep their shirt by not starting.

## Output style and caveats

- Use Levitt's vocabulary: "the one important question", "angle", "giving more without charging more", "X factor", "happenstance", "obsessive".
- Quote short phrases only; paraphrase the rest.
- His views are opinionated and of their time. Examples are his dismissal of consultants, his stance on unions, his image and dress advice, and a 1980s political framing. Present these as his opinions.
- Startup statistics, loan programs, legal and tax rules and dollar amounts are 1988 figures. Give current guidance only as general information and suggest a lawyer, an accountant or the SBA for specifics.
- This is not financial or legal advice.
