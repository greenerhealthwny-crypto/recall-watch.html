## §1 Never Outshine the Master
**Judgment.** Make those above you feel comfortably superior. Displaying too much talent inspires fear and insecurity; make your masters look more brilliant than they are.
**Story.** Nicolas Fouquet threw a party more magnificent than anything Louis XIV could stage and was arrested within weeks (transgression). Galileo dedicated his discovery of Jupiter's moons to the Medici as cosmic symbols of their family and won a lasting position (observance).
**Keys.** Insecure superiors are most dangerous; flatter subtly; attribute your ideas to them; don't presume on their affection.
**Reversal.** If the master is weak or falling, outshining may be the way up; don't be too subservient for too long.
**Defense.** If you lead, notice when a subordinate's deference is strategic; reward talent openly so it doesn't go underground.

## §2 Never Put Too Much Trust in Friends, Learn How to Use Enemies
**Judgment.** Friends betray more quickly because they envy; they become spoiled. A former enemy has more to prove and is more loyal.
**Story.** Byzantine emperor Michael III raised his friend Basilius to power and was murdered by him. Chinese emperors who co-opted enemies (Sung dynasty founder) kept their thrones.
**Keys.** Keep work and friendship distinct; hire for competence; turn rivals into allies; an enemy gives you focus.
**Reversal.** A friend can do dirty work loyally, but don't let your guard down; watch for envy and ingratitude.
**Defense.** Be wary when a friend's new dependence on you turns to resentment; keep favors balanced.

## §3 Conceal Your Intentions
**Judgment.** Keep people off balance by not revealing the purpose behind your actions. Use red herrings and smoke screens.
**Story.** Ninon de Lenclos coached the Marquis de Sévigné to stop revealing his feelings; Bismarck argued *for* peace with Austria to gain the ministry he needed to wage war later; Haile Selassie's mild manner concealed relentless ambition.
**Keys.** Use decoy desires, false sincerity, noble gestures, a bland exterior; people believe what they see.
**Reversal.** Once you have a reputation for deception, it stops working; then be the frank "honest rogue."
**Defense.** Judge people by results and patterns, not stated aims; ask who benefits.

## §4 Always Say Less Than Necessary
**Judgment.** The more you say, the more common and less in control you appear. Powerful people impress by saying less.
**Story.** Coriolanus, a war hero, talked too much in his campaign and was banished. Louis XIV's "I shall see" kept courtiers anxious and attentive.
**Keys.** Silence unsettles others and makes them fill the gap; vague, open answers seem deep; words, once out, can't be recalled.
**Reversal.** Silence can arouse suspicion; sometimes talk is a smoke screen or a way to play the jester.
**Defense.** When someone stays silent in negotiation, resist filling the void with concessions.

## §5 So Much Depends on Reputation — Guard It with Your Life
**Judgment.** Reputation is the cornerstone of power; make it unassailable, thwart attacks early.
**Story.** P. T. Barnum built a reputation for spectacle and ruined a rival museum's reputation through mockery. Zhuge Liang's reputation for cunning alone made an enemy army retreat.
**Keys.** Establish one outstanding quality (generosity, honesty, cunning); defend it immediately; humor and satire disarm attacks better than anger.
**Reversal.** If your reputation is already gone, there is little to lose; attack a powerful figure's reputation only when you can't be hurt.
**Defense.** Track your reputation; respond to smears calmly and quickly; never answer gossip with outrage.

## §6 Court Attention at All Cost
**Judgment.** What is unseen counts for nothing. Stand out; appear larger, more colorful and mysterious than the masses.
**Story.** Barnum's hoaxes and attractions; the mystique of Mata Hari.
**Keys.** Create scandal or spectacle early; then cultivate mystery; never let the audience get bored.
**Reversal.** Attention that seems desperate or overshadows a superior is dangerous (see Law 1). Notoriety can turn to contempt.
**Defense.** Separate the substance from the show; don't let hype set the agenda.

## §7 Get Others to Do the Work for You, but Always Take the Credit
**Judgment.** Use others' wisdom and labor; your helpers will be forgotten and you remembered.
**Story.** Nikola Tesla's work enriched Edison and Westinghouse while he died poor.
**Keys.** Find people with skills you lack; package, finish and present; protect your own credit and ideas.
**Reversal.** Taking credit too visibly breeds resentment; sometimes share credit to win loyalty.
**Defense.** Document and publicly attach your name to your work; get agreements in writing before collaborating.

## §8 Make Other People Come to You — Use Bait If Necessary
**Judgment.** When you force others to act, you control the game. Lure them with gains and make them abandon their plans.
**Story.** Talleyrand let Napoleon escape Elba and walk into a trap.
**Keys.** Make others play on your ground; patience; use bait (opportunity, flattery) to draw them in.
**Reversal.** Sometimes speed and surprise are better; don't wait so long that you lose the initiative.
**Defense.** When an offer seems to pull you onto unfamiliar ground, ask why and on whose terms.

## §9 Win Through Your Actions, Never Through Argument
**Judgment.** Triumph in argument breeds resentment. Demonstrate; don't explicate.
**Story.** Michelangelo "fixed" Soderini's complaint about a statue's nose by pretending to chisel while dropping marble dust; the official was satisfied.
**Keys.** Show, prototype, let results speak; arguments make people defensive.
**Reversal.** Verbal argument is useful as a distraction or when you need time; with peers it can be healthy.
**Defense.** Ask for evidence of results, not eloquence.

## §10 Infection: Avoid the Unhappy and Unlucky
**Judgment.** Emotional states are infectious. The chronically unfortunate draw misfortune on themselves and you.
**Story.** Lola Montez left ruin in every court she entered.
**Keys.** Judge people by their effect on the world; associate with the fortunate and energetic.
**Reversal.** None for chronic infectors; but people in genuine temporary hardship deserve help — Greene's target is the habitual drama-maker.
**Defense.** Notice whose presence drains you and sets off conflict; limit exposure without cruelty.

*Ethics note:* Don't use this to justify abandoning people who are sick, grieving or poor.

## §11 Learn to Keep People Dependent on You
**Judgment.** Be needed. The more you are relied upon, the more freedom you have.
**Keys.** Develop a unique, hard-to-replace skill; don't teach others so much they no longer need you.
**Reversal.** Dependence ties you too; being needed by a weak master can sink you with them.
**Defense.** Avoid single points of failure in your team or business; cross-train.

## §12 Use Selective Honesty and Generosity to Disarm Your Victim
**Judgment.** One honest, generous move covers many dishonest ones.
**Story.** Count Lustig returned money to Al Capone to win his trust and a reward.
**Keys.** A timely gift or confession lowers defenses.
**Reversal.** If you already have a record of deceit, honesty will seem calculated.
**Defense.** Be careful when unusual generosity comes just before a request; separate the gift from the decision.

## §13 When Asking for Help, Appeal to People's Self-Interest, Never to Their Mercy or Gratitude
**Judgment.** Show what's in it for them, out of proportion.
**Story.** Corcyra won Athens' alliance by stressing Athenian interest, not past favors.
**Keys.** Know what each party values; frame the ask as their opportunity.
**Reversal.** Some people prefer to be seen as charitable; then appeal to their generosity.
**Defense.** When pitched, separate the benefit claimed from the benefit real.

## §14 Pose as a Friend, Work as a Spy
**Judgment.** Gather information on rivals; in social settings, probe indirectly.
**Story.** Talleyrand drew out guests at his dinners.
**Keys.** Ask indirect questions; feign confidences to elicit theirs.
**Reversal.** Information can be fed to you; spies can be turned; be careful about being caught.
**Defense.** Assume social chat with rivals is intelligence gathering; say less (Law 4).
*Ethics note:* Information-gathering means research and listening, not surveillance, hacking or deception that breaks laws or privacy.

## §15 Crush Your Enemy Totally
**Judgment.** A feared enemy left half-defeated will recover and seek revenge.
**Story.** Xiang Yu spared Liu Bang, who later destroyed him; Wu Chao eliminated rivals completely.
**Keys.** Finish conflicts decisively; leave no faction to regroup.
**Reversal.** Crushing can create martyrs and lasting hatred; sometimes let enemies destroy themselves or win them over.
**Business translation:** decisively win the deal, position or market, and close the conflict cleanly (contracts, settlements); never personal harm or harassment.

## §16 Use Absence to Increase Respect and Honor
**Judgment.** Too much presence lowers your value. Create value through scarcity.
**Story.** Deioces withdrew from judging disputes until the Medes made him king; Guillaume de Balaun's lover withdrew to rekindle his love.
**Keys.** Once established, withdraw temporarily; scarcity raises value.
**Reversal.** Before you are established, absence means being forgotten.
**Defense.** Notice when someone's unavailability is a tactic to raise their price.

## §17 Keep Others in Suspended Terror: Cultivate an Air of Unpredictability
**Judgment.** Predictability gives others control; be deliberately unpredictable.
**Story.** Bobby Fischer's erratic demands unsettled Spassky; Picasso's unpredictable moods kept dealers off balance.
**Keys.** Vary your patterns; surprise.
**Reversal.** Constant unpredictability looks unstable; in subordinates it is resented.
**Defense.** Don't let erratic behavior rattle you; stay with your own plan and timeline.
*Ethics note:* Don't apply "terror" to employees, partners or family; it's abusive.

## §18 Do Not Build Fortresses to Protect Yourself — Isolation Is Dangerous
**Judgment.** Isolation cuts you off from information and makes you a target. Circulate, find allies.
**Story.** Qin Shi Huang hid in his palaces, lost touch, and his dynasty collapsed; Louis XIV kept everyone close at Versailles.
**Keys.** Stay in the flow of people and information.
**Reversal.** Brief solitude is useful for thinking; only extended isolation is fatal.
**Defense.** Leaders: keep honest channels open; don't let gatekeepers wall you in.

## §19 Know Who You're Dealing With — Do Not Offend the Wrong Person
**Judgment.** People react differently; some will seek revenge for life. Choose opponents carefully.
**Types to beware:** the arrogant and proud, the hopelessly insecure, the suspicious, the serpent with a long memory, the plain unassuming person who is easily underestimated.
**Reversal.** Know-who-you're-dealing-with has none: always read people before acting.

## §20 Do Not Commit to Anyone
**Judgment.** Don't rush to take sides; stay independent and let others pursue you.
**Story.** Elizabeth I kept suitors hoping and never married; Isabella d'Este kept Mantua independent among warring powers.
**Keys.** Hover above disputes; offer support without total commitment.
**Reversal.** Eventually neutrality makes everyone distrust you; sometimes you must pick the winning side decisively.

## §21 Play a Sucker to Catch a Sucker — Seem Dumber Than Your Mark
**Judgment.** Make others feel smarter than you; they won't suspect your motives.
**Story.** The great diamond hoax that fleeced San Francisco financiers who were sure they were the smart ones.
**Reversal.** Sometimes you must show intelligence to rise (e.g., early in a career).
**Defense.** Be careful when someone flatters your expertise while steering the decision.

## §22 Use the Surrender Tactic: Transform Weakness into Power
**Judgment.** When weaker, don't fight for honor; surrender, recover, and wait for the stronger party to weaken.
**Story.** The Athenians vs. the Melians (Melos refused to yield and was destroyed); Bertolt Brecht's "surrender" to the House Un-American Activities Committee.
**Reversal.** Surrender only as a tactic; if your opponent intends to destroy you, fighting may be necessary.

## §23 Concentrate Your Forces
**Judgment.** Intensity beats extensity. Find the one deep source (a key patron, market, skill) and work it.
**Story.** The Rothschilds concentrated wealth and trust within the family.
**Reversal.** Concentration can make you a target; sometimes dispersing (guerrilla) works.

## §24 Play the Perfect Courtier
**Judgment.** Master indirection, flatter, yield to superiors, assert power gracefully.
**Laws of court politics:** avoid ostentation; practice nonchalance; be frugal with flattery; arrange to be noticed; alter your style for each person; never be the bearer of bad news; never affect friendliness with your master; never criticize those above you directly; be frugal in asking favors; never joke about appearances or taste; don't be the court cynic; be self-observant; master your emotions; fit the spirit of the times; be a source of pleasure.
**Story.** Versailles under Louis XIV; Jean-Baptiste Isabey's and other courtiers' handling of Napoleon.
**Reversal.** None: you are always in a court; but be careful not to be a fawning courtier.

## §25 Re-Create Yourself
**Judgment.** Don't accept the role society assigns; forge a new, dramatic identity you control.
**Story.** Julius Caesar's theatrical public life; George Sand's re-creation of herself.
**Keys.** Master your emotions; create a memorable character; use dramatic timing.
**Reversal.** None per Greene, but keep re-creation grounded in real skills so it isn't seen as fraud.

## §26 Keep Your Hands Clean
**Judgment.** Seem a paragon of civility; use others as scapegoats and cat's-paws for dirty work.
**Story.** Cesare Borgia had Remirro de Orco pacify the Romagna brutally, then executed him to win public gratitude.
**Reversal.** Scapegoating can be obvious and backfire; sometimes taking blame openly earns respect.
**Defense.** When something goes wrong, ask who ordered it, not just who executed it. Leaders: own mistakes; scapegoating destroys trust.

## §27 Play on People's Need to Believe to Create a Cultlike Following
**Judgment.** People want to believe; offer a cause, vague promises, ritual and sacrifice.
**Five steps (as Greene lays them out):** keep it vague and simple; emphasize the visual and sensual over the intellectual; borrow the forms of organized religion; disguise your source of income; set up an us-vs-them dynamic.
**Story.** Mesmer and animal magnetism; Francesco Borri.
**Reversal.** Disillusioned followers turn violent.
**Use defensively only.** Use this to recognize manipulative groups, sales schemes, and influencers. For community-building, take only the honest parts: a clear mission, shared ritual and belonging, without deception, isolation or exploitation.

## §28 Enter Action with Boldness
**Judgment.** Timidity is dangerous; doubt infects execution. Mistakes made boldly can be fixed with more boldness.
**Story.** Count Lustig "sold" the Eiffel Tower; Pietro Aretino's audacity.
**Reversal.** Boldness should be a strategy, not a personality; constant boldness is reckless.

## §29 Plan All the Way to the End
**Judgment.** The ending is everything. Plan for all consequences, obstacles and twists so you know when to stop.
**Story.** Vasco Núñez de Balboa didn't plan beyond discovering the Pacific and lost everything; Bismarck planned to the end.
**Reversal.** Plans must stay flexible (see Law 48).

## §30 Make Your Accomplishments Seem Effortless
**Judgment.** Conceal the toil and tricks; act as if you could do much more.
**Story.** Houdini; Sen no Rikyu's effortless tea ceremony.
**Reversal.** Sometimes showing the effort earns sympathy or credit; but never reveal your tricks.

## §31 Control the Options: Get Others to Play with the Cards You Deal
**Judgment.** Give people choices that all favor you; they feel in control.
**Tactics:** color the choices; force the resister; alter the playing field; the shrinking options; the weak man on the precipice; brothers in crime; the horns of a dilemma.
**Story.** Ivan the Terrible; Kissinger presenting Nixon with options designed to lead to his preferred one.
**Reversal.** Others see through limited options; give real choices sometimes.
**Defense.** When offered only two options, ask what the third is; reframe the decision.

## §32 Play to People's Fantasies
**Judgment.** Truth is often unpleasant; people flock to those who supply romance and fantasy.
**Story.** The alchemist Bragadino in Venice; Victor Lustig.
**Reversal.** Fantasy that isn't tied to some reality collapses; disillusionment turns to anger.
**Defense.** Be suspicious of offers that promise transformation with no visible mechanism.

## §33 Discover Each Man's Thumbscrew
**Judgment.** Everyone has a weakness — an insecurity, uncontrolled emotion or secret pleasure.
**Keys:** watch gestures and unconscious signals; find the helpless child; look for contrasts (the overt trait hides its opposite); find the weak link; fill the void.
**Reversal.** Playing on weakness can provoke an emotional reaction you can't control.
**Use for:** understanding motivations and needs in negotiation and leadership; **not** blackmail, coercion or exploiting addiction or vulnerability.
**Defense.** Know your own thumbscrews (flattery, anger, a need for approval) so others can't use them.

## §34 Be Royal in Your Own Fashion: Act Like a King to Be Treated Like One
**Judgment.** How you carry yourself determines how you're treated. Self-respect inspires respect.
**Story.** Columbus demanded a noble title before his voyage; Louis-Philippe, the "bourgeois king," was disrespected.
**Keys.** Ask for more; carry yourself with dignity; deal with peers as equals.
**Reversal.** Regal bearing must be earned and shouldn't become arrogance.

## §35 Master the Art of Timing
**Judgment.** Never seem in a hurry. Wait patiently, read the spirit of the times, and strike when ripe.
**Story.** Joseph Fouché survived revolutions by timing his loyalties.
**Keys.** Long time (patience), forced time (upsetting others' timing), end time (strike decisively).
**Reversal.** None; timing is everything.

## §36 Disdain Things You Cannot Have: Ignoring Them Is the Best Revenge
**Judgment.** Paying attention to a petty problem or enemy gives it credibility; show contempt for what you can't have.
**Story.** Henry VIII's obsession with the Pope's refusal; Pancho Villa ignored Pershing's pursuit.
**Reversal.** Real threats shouldn't be ignored; small fires can grow.

## §37 Create Compelling Spectacles
**Judgment.** Striking imagery and symbolic gestures create an aura of power.
**Story.** Louis XIV as the Sun King; Mesmer's theatrics.
**Reversal.** Spectacle without substance is eventually seen through.

## §38 Think as You Like but Behave Like Others
**Judgment.** Flaunting unconventionality makes people feel inferior and they punish you. Blend in; share originality only with friends.
**Reversal.** Being different works only if you have power or the times reward it.

## §39 Stir Up Waters to Catch Fish
**Judgment.** Stay calm while making your enemies angry; rattled people make mistakes.
**Story.** Napoleon's rage at Talleyrand; Ninon de Lenclos's calm.
**Reversal.** Some people channel anger well; don't provoke those who can.
**Defense.** When someone is clearly baiting you, pause and refuse the bait; calm is power.

## §40 Despise the Free Lunch
**Judgment.** Free things carry tricks or obligations. Pay your way; be generous with money — generosity attracts power.
**Story.** J. Paul Getty's stinginess cost him loyalty and reputation.
**Reversal.** Taking a free lunch is wise when it costs you nothing and you're under no obligation.

## §41 Avoid Stepping into a Great Man's Shoes
**Judgment.** Successors and children of the famous must do double to outshine them. Change course; establish your own identity.
**Story.** Alexander the Great surpassed Philip by going further; Louis XIV and his successors.
**Reversal.** Use the predecessor's legacy where it still has value.

## §42 Strike the Shepherd and the Sheep Will Scatter
**Judgment.** Trouble often stems from one strong individual; isolate or remove their influence.
**Reversal.** Removing a figure can make a martyr.
**Workplace translation:** address the one disruptive person through clear accountability, boundaries, and fair process — not bullying or retaliation.

## §43 Work on the Hearts and Minds of Others
**Judgment.** Coercion creates backlash. Seduce people into wanting what you want by playing on emotions, values, and fears.
**Story.** Marie Antoinette's indifference to the people's hearts.
**Reversal.** None; but understanding people is not exploiting them.

## §44 Disarm and Infuriate with the Mirror Effect
**Judgment.** Mirror enemies' actions to confuse and teach them; mirror friends' values to seduce.
**Effects:** the Neutralizing Effect, the Narcissus Effect, the Moral Effect, the Hallucinatory Effect.
**Reversal.** Mirroring can backfire if the mirror reminds people of something unpleasant, or makes you seem unoriginal.

## §45 Preach the Need for Change, but Never Reform Too Much at Once
**Judgment.** People are creatures of habit. Change too fast and they revolt; make change feel like an improvement on the past.
**Story.** Thomas Cromwell's rapid reforms ended with his execution; Mao used familiar forms.
**Reversal.** In a crisis, sweeping change can be welcome.

## §46 Never Appear Too Perfect
**Judgment.** Appearing faultless breeds envy and silent enemies. Display harmless flaws.
**Reversal.** Some people want a flawless leader; but even then, a touch of humanity helps.

## §47 Do Not Go Past the Mark You Aimed For; In Victory, Learn When to Stop
**Judgment.** Victory is a moment of peril; arrogance pushes you past your goal. Set a goal; stop when you reach it.
**Story.** Cyrus the Great overreached and died; Madame de Pompadour knew her limits.
**Reversal.** None; restraint is a strength.

## §48 Assume Formlessness
**Judgment.** A visible, fixed plan invites attack. Be fluid and adaptable like water; nothing is certain.
**Story.** The Spartans' rigidity vs. Athenian adaptability; Mao's guerrilla tactics.
**Reversal.** Concentration and form are sometimes necessary (Law 23).

## §50 Defensive checklist: spotting the laws used against you

- **Unusual generosity or confession before an ask** → Law 12.
- **Only two choices, both bad for you** → Law 31.
- **Flattery about your intelligence while they steer** → Laws 21, 33.
- **Friendly chat that keeps probing your plans** → Law 14.
- **Someone becoming suddenly unavailable to raise their price** → Law 16.
- **A free gift with strings** → Law 40.
- **Your work presented under someone else's name** → Law 7.
- **Erratic behavior that keeps you off balance** → Law 17.
- **Being baited into anger in public** → Law 39.
- **A charismatic group with vague promises, rituals and an enemy** → Law 27.
- **A scapegoat for a leader's decision** → Law 26.
- **Promises of transformation with no mechanism** → Law 32.

The general counter: slow down, say less, judge by actions over time, and keep your own goals in view.
