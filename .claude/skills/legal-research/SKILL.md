## The research workflow

The book's method, in order:

1. **Learn the framework.** Know the sources of law, state versus federal law, and which courts bind which *(book, Ch 1)*.
2. **Frame the issue** in four steps *(book, Ch 3)*:
   1. Is it **civil or criminal** (or both)? Also: is it **substantive** (what the law is) or **procedural** (the court process), and is it **state or federal** law?
   2. Which **area of law** is it (contracts, torts, family, landlord-tenant…)?
   3. Which **resources** will help?
   4. **Write your research question.** Ask why you are researching. Filing a lawsuit needs depth; preparing to meet a lawyer needs only an overview. Then list the specific questions to answer.
3. **Start with secondary sources** to learn the vocabulary and get citations to primary law. Prefer a state-specific source for state-law issues *(book, Ch 2, 4)*.
4. **Find the primary law** (constitution, statute, regulation, ordinance, cases) and read it closely *(book, Ch 5–7)*.
5. **Validate**: confirm every case and statute is still **good law** by Shepardizing or KeyCiting, and check pocket parts and updates *(book, Ch 8)*.
6. **Organize and use it**: keep files by issue, write an IRAC memo, go to court, or brief a lawyer *(book, Ch 9)*.

**Key rules** *(book)*:
- **Courts follow primary sources, not secondary ones.** Never rely only on a secondary source, because the law changes faster than commentary does.
- **Just because a source is online doesn't mean it holds legal weight.** Lower-court decisions can be overruled, and a legislature can undo case law with a new statute (unless the case interprets the Constitution).
- Most failed research happens because "the researcher runs out of patience at the index". Keep trying new terms.
- Confine your arguments to existing law. Trial judges almost never disregard precedent (*stare decisis*). Change comes on appeal.

**When helping a user with a legal question:**
1. Ask for their **state** (and county or city, if local rules or ordinances may apply), the key facts, and their goal: understand the issue, sue, defend, or meet a lawyer.
2. Classify the issue: civil or criminal, substantive or procedural, state or federal, and the area of law.
3. Suggest 5–10 search terms (Cartwheel style: broader, narrower, synonyms, related terms) and a Boolean query.
4. Name the best starting secondary source (a Nolo title or article, the state encyclopedia, a practice guide) and the primary law to look for.
5. Explain how to verify that the law is current: Shepard's or KeyCite at a law library, the pocket part, the official code website.
6. Offer an IRAC outline or a list of questions for a lawyer.

## Caveats

- **This is legal information, not legal advice.** The book itself says self-help information is not a substitute for a lawyer in every case *(book)*. Recommend a lawyer for serious matters such as criminal charges, large sums, immigration, custody, or deadlines that are close *(std)*.
- **Websites and products change.** The book's URLs date from 2021 or earlier *(book)*. For example, GPO's FDsys has since been replaced by **govinfo.gov**, and eCFR is at **ecfr.gov** *(std, verify)*. Check that a link still works before you cite it.
- **Deadlines** (statutes of limitations, response deadlines such as the typical 30 days to answer a complaint) vary by state and by claim. Always verify them *(book: answer usually 30 days; std: verify)*.
- **Never invent citations.** Give the user a search strategy and a way to verify, rather than case names or citations you haven't confirmed *(std)*.
