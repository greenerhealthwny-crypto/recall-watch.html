## §2 Constitutions, statutes, regulations, ordinances (Ch 5)

- **The book's advice:** start with secondary sources before reading primary law.
- **Federal statutes:**
  - The **U.S.C.** gives the text only. The **U.S.C.A.** (West) and **U.S.C.S.** (LexisNexis) are *annotated*: they add case summaries, history and cross-references.
  - Find a statute by its citation, its **popular name** (Popular Names tables, or Shepard's Acts and Cases by Popular Names), the index, or by browsing its title.
  - Bills and legislative history are on congress.gov.
- **State statutes:** use the official state code site or an annotated code, for example West's or Deering's for California.
- **Always check the pocket part** (the paper supplement at the back of the volume), or the online update, for amendments and repeals. Ask the librarian about newer supplements.
- **Seven rules for reading a statute:**
  1. Read the statute **at least three times, then read it again.**
  2. Pay close attention to **"ands" and "ors"**.
  3. Assume **all words and punctuation have meaning**.
  4. Interpret it **consistently with related statutes** if possible.
  5. **Criminal statutes are read strictly**, with ambiguities resolved for the defendant. In the book's Vermont courthouse example, a man was acquitted of breaking and entering because intent at the moment of entry wasn't proven.
  6. Interpret **ambiguities to further the law's purpose**, especially in social welfare law. In the book's welfare example, "earns" meant earnings from employment, not a gift from a parent.
  7. Interpret the statute **so that it makes sense**, avoiding results the legislature could not have intended. Example: California's $100-a-day utility shutoff penalty was read as "up to $100" (*Hale v. Morgan*, 22 Cal.3d 388, 584 P.2d 512 (1978)); the legislature later amended the statute.
- **Track down every cross-reference.**
- **Court interpretations are part of the statute.** Read the cases that interpret it, using the annotations.
- **Other interpretation aids:**
  - **Attorney general opinions**: not binding, but persuasive. Find them through NAAG or FindLaw.
  - **Words and Phrases**: one-line judicial definitions of terms.
  - **Legislative history**: committee reports and similar materials. On congress.gov for federal law. Harder to find for older state laws.
- **Regulations:** federal regulations are in the **Code of Federal Regulations** (the book points to GPO; now govinfo.gov and ecfr.gov *(std, verify)*). New and proposed rules appear in the Federal Register *(std)*. State regulations are on agency and state sites.
- **Procedural statutes and rules:** court rules, the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, local court rules.
- **Ordinances:** on city and county websites, or through municipal code publishers *(std: Municode, American Legal Publishing)*.

## §3 Finding cases (Ch 6)

**Reading a citation.** Example: *Lukhard v. Reed*, 481 U.S. 368, 95 L.Ed.2d 328, 107 S.Ct. 1807 (1987).
- **Case name**:
  - Usually plaintiff v. defendant, but the appealing party may be listed first.
  - "In re" means "in the matter of".
  - Family cases are often named *Marriage of [Surname]*.
- **Volume** number of the reporter.
- **Reporter** abbreviation. A new series (2d, 3d…) starts after a set number of volumes.
- **Page** on which the case begins.
- **Court and year.** For example, (3d Cir. 1984), or F.Supp. (E.D. Pa.) for a district court.
- **Parallel citations** are the same case in different reporters. Supreme Court cases appear in U.S., L.Ed. and S.Ct.
- **Advance sheets** carry the same volume and page numbers as the bound reporter will.
- Citation style follows *The Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation* (21st edition).

**Ways to find cases:**
- **Free online:** Google Scholar, Justia, FindLaw. Search by name, citation or keyword.
- From **annotations** to a statute (the annotated codes).
- From the **table of statutes** in reporter volumes, and **Shepard's Citations for Statutes**.
- From **secondary sources** (encyclopedias, A.L.R., treatises).
- From **digests**:
  - Digests collect **headnotes** (one-sentence summaries) by topic and **key number** (West).
  - Kinds of digest: the U.S. Supreme Court Digest, the Federal Practice Digest, state digests, and four regional digests.
  - Use the **Descriptive Word Index**. The **table of cases** gives a citation from a case name.
  - **Always check the digest's pocket part.**
- **One good case leads to more:** use its headnote's key number, and the cases it cites.

## §4 Reading and using cases (Ch 7)

- **Parts of an opinion:**
  - Above the opinion: a synopsis and **headnotes** added by the publisher. These are **not law** and "tend to overly simplify issues", so read the opinion itself.
  - The opinion itself usually contains:
    1. the **facts**;
    2. the **issue(s)**;
    3. the **holding** (decision);
    4. the **reasoning**.
- **What counts as law:**
  - Only the decision of the **majority** (or a **plurality**) is the law.
  - **Concurring** and **dissenting** opinions are not binding, though they can be persuasive later.
  - **Dicta**, language not needed to decide the case, is not binding. The exception is the US Supreme Court, where every word is weighed.
- **Precedent versus persuasive authority:**
  - A published appellate decision binds the courts below it in that system.
  - Where a state has only one intermediate appellate ruling on an issue, it is generally followed statewide until others are issued *(book, partly legible)*.
  - Out-of-state cases are persuasive only. A Colorado court relied on cases from Hawaii, Texas and Virginia.
- **Distinguishing:** it is "much easier to get a court to 'distinguish' an old case than openly overrule it", on the basis of a factual or legal difference.
- **Analyzing whether a case helps you** (the book's approach; steps partly legible):
  1. Identify the precise issues the court had to decide, and compare them with yours. If none match, the case probably won't help.
  2. Check whether the case's decision on those issues logically applies to your facts.
  3. Check whether the deciding court's rulings are binding on the court that will hear your matter. Otherwise treat the case as persuasive.
  4. Check whether it is still good law (§5).
- **Prefer recent cases.** Check the newest ones first.

## §5 Validating: is it still good law? (Ch 8)

"Shepardizing is every bit as important as finding a case or statute." It has two jobs:
- it confirms the case or statute **hasn't been overruled, reversed or otherwise changed**;
- it **finds more cases** that cite it.

**In print (law library):**
1. Identify the citation.
2. Find the Shepard's volumes for that reporter.
3. Use the right section and the right time period.
4. Find the volume number, then the page number in bold.
5. Read the citing references and their codes.
6. Check every supplement.

**History of the case:** affirmed, reversed, vacated, modified, and Supreme Court action (for example, review denied, which leaves the decision standing as good law).

**Treatment by later cases** (codes used when the citing case is unrelated):

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| c, criticized | Another court disagreed. Shows the arguments an opponent may use |
| d, distinguished | A similar case was decided differently because of a difference in its facts or law |
| e, explained | The cited case is clarified |
| f, followed | The rule is applied. Helpful |
| j, dissenting opinion | Cited in a dissent |
| ~, concurring opinion | Cited in a concurrence |
| o, overruled | **No longer valid** |
| q, questioned | Its validity is doubted |
| su, superseded by statute | A statute has replaced the rule |

**Online signals:** Lexis Shepard's and Westlaw **KeyCite** update almost immediately and link to every citing case. Westlaw flags cases at risk of being overruled. Shepard's online signals (source: lexisnexis.com):
- **red**: strong negative treatment (overruled or reversed);
- **orange**: validity questioned;
- **yellow**: possible negative treatment (distinguished or criticized);
- **green**: positive (affirmed or followed);
- **blue A**: neutral analysis available;
- **blue I**: citation information only (for example, law reviews).

*(The book lists these meanings; the colors are std, verify.)*

**Free alternatives:** read cases on Justia, FindLaw or VersusLaw (which lists citing cases without treatment analysis). Then **Shepardize for free at the local law library**, which often gives public access to Lexis or Westlaw.

**Statutes** are Shepardized too, for amendments, repeals, and court decisions that interpreted or invalidated them. Also check the pocket part and the official code site.

**Using Shepard's for research:** each citing case is a lead to more law on your issue.
