# The Framework, Where to Search, and Framing the Issue (Ch 1–3)

All points are from the book *(book)* unless marked *(std)*.

## §1 Sources of law

| Source | What it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **US Constitution** | Structures the three branches; sets federal versus state power. The amendments guarantee rights | All other law must conform to it |
| **Federal statutes** | Acts of Congress, compiled ("codified") by subject in a code | United States Code |
| **Federal cases** | Interpret federal law. Also cover disputes between parties from different states | |
| **Federal regulations** | Issued by agencies (EEOC, IRS, EPA) under an **"enabling statute"** | Administrative law |
| **State constitutions, statutes and cases** | The same structure at state level. State law applies only within the state | Codes are grouped by subject, for example the California Family Code |
| **Local ordinances** | City and county rules: zoning, building permits | Disputes often go to the local body (a planning commission hearing) before any court |

- Tribal law is outside the book's scope.
- **Common law (case law)** is judges' written opinions interpreting statutes and the Constitution. Statutes are only "a small part" of the law, because courts interpret them. For example, judges look at prior cases to decide what "best interests of the child" means.
- **Stare decisis** ("let the decision stand"): judges follow precedent unless there is an excellent reason not to. Trial judges expect anyone seeking a change in the law to appeal.
- **Congress can override statutory case law.** After the Supreme Court ruled against Lilly Ledbetter on the 180-day filing deadline for pay discrimination, Congress passed the Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act. This isn't possible when the court was interpreting the Constitution.

## §2 State versus federal law

| Mainly state | Mainly federal | Both |
|---|---|---|
| Child custody, conservatorships, contracts, corporations, most crimes, divorce, powers of attorney, guardianships, landlord-tenant, licensing, living wills, motor vehicles, partnerships, paternity, personal injury, probate, property tax, real estate, trusts, wills, workers' compensation, zoning | Bankruptcy, copyright, customs, federal taxes, immigration, interstate commerce, maritime, patent, Social Security, trademark | Consumer protection, criminal law, employment, environment, health, labor, occupational safety, subsidized housing, taxes, transportation, unemployment insurance, veterans' benefits, welfare |

**Rule of thumb:** if federal funds are involved, at least one element of federal law is at play.

## §3 Courts and precedent

- **Structure:**
  - **Trial court**: may be called superior, municipal, or in New York the "supreme court". Trial courts generally don't issue binding opinions.
  - **Intermediate appellate court**: reviews the trial court for mistakes, without a new trial. Its opinions bind the trial courts below it.
  - **Supreme court**: usually chooses which cases to hear. If it declines, the appellate ruling stands.
- **Exceptions:** small claims courts; *de novo* review, where a trial court reviews a case afresh; bankruptcy court.
- **Federal courts:** US District Courts (at least one per state) → **13 circuits** (the First through Eleventh, plus the DC and Federal Circuits) → the US Supreme Court. The court finder is at uscourts.gov.
  - A circuit decision binds only the district courts in that circuit. For example, the Fifth Circuit (Texas, Louisiana, Mississippi) binds those districts. To the Eleventh Circuit it is only **persuasive**.
- **State and federal systems don't generally bind each other.** But a state court deciding a federal claim must follow federal law.
- **Binding versus persuasive:** the higher the court, the more persuasive its opinion. **"Every word (even dicta) of a U.S. Supreme Court opinion"** matters. An intermediate court in a small state carries little weight elsewhere.
- **Most cases don't go to trial**, and most settle *(book; details partly illegible in the scan)*.

## §4 Where to research

**Primary versus secondary sources:**
- **Primary sources** are the law itself.
- **Secondary sources** explain the law. Start with them when you know nothing about a topic. Then move to primary law, which is closer to your facts, more current, and carries legal weight.

**Places to research:**
- **Law libraries** (law schools, state and county): librarians, plus free access to paid databases. Call first to check public access. Most won't lend.
- **Court self-help centers and court websites**: forms, deadlines and procedures. The staff can't give legal advice.
- **PACER**: federal court filings, for a small fee. Useful to see whether a party has been sued before.
- **Legislative sites:** congress.gov for bills and legislative history. GPO for the US Code and CFR (the book points to gpo.gov/fdsys, now **govinfo.gov** *(std, verify)*).
- **Agency websites**: regulations and explanations.
- **Free legal sites:**
  - **Nolo.com**: plain-English articles, state-by-state charts, a lawyer directory.
  - **Justia** (law.justia.com): US Code, Supreme Court opinions since 1790, federal appellate cases since 1950, state materials, and a legal blog search.
  - **FindLaw** (caselaw.findlaw.com).
  - **Google Scholar**: choose "Case law" and use "Select courts". Use the advanced search.
  - **Cornell LII / Wex** (listed in the book's index).
- **Low-cost:**
  - **VersusLaw**: cases and a citing-case list, but no treatment analysis.
  - **FastCase**: cases, statutes, rules and regulations, with a most-cited sort.
- **Premium:** **Westlaw** and **LexisNexis** are comprehensive but costly. Use their tutorials before paying by the search.
- **Other tools:**
  - Google News and Google Alerts to follow a topic;
  - "define:" searches;
  - Nolo's online dictionary;
  - Google Books.

## §5 Boolean searching

| Operator | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **and** (Google: a space; Westlaw: **&**) | Both terms | slip & fall |
| **or** (Westlaw: a space) | Either term | box or container |
| **and not** / minus sign | Exclude a term. Careful: this can drop relevant results | criminal and not plaintiff |
| **near/n**, **n/3**, **w/3** | Terms within *n* words of each other | slip w/4 fall |
| **adj**, **adj/n** | Adjacent, in the order given | dangerous adj beauty |
| **"quotes"** | Exact phrase | "slip and fall" |
| **xor** | One term or the other, not both | box xor container |
| **\*** | Root extender | drive\* (matches driver, drivers', driving) |
| **? or !** | Single-letter wildcard | wom?n |
| **( )** | Group terms | (break or enter) and (house or car) |

Example: license and suspend\* and (driver or operator). Keep changing the query as new terms turn up.

## §6 Framing the issue (Ch 3)

- **Civil or criminal?** If the conduct is punishable by jail, and a government prosecutor brings the charges, it's criminal. A dispute with a person, a business or the government seeking **damages** (money) or an order to do or stop doing something is civil.
- **Substantive or procedural?** Both civil and criminal questions can be either:
  - *Substantive* is what the law is, for example burglary versus larceny, or tort law.
  - *Procedural* is the court process: which court, what to file, deadlines, service, evidence, appeals.
  - A warrantless search is a procedural issue. Whether the drugs found were yours is a substantive one.
- **Procedural rules:** each state has its own, and many resemble the **Federal Rules of Civil Procedure**.
- **Areas of law** (the book's list):
  - civil rights, commercial, constitutional, consumer, contracts, corporations, creditor/debtor;
  - cyberlaw, education, elder, employment, energy, environmental, estate planning, evidence;
  - family, health, housing, immigration, intellectual property, juvenile, military, municipal;
  - prison, property, public benefits, public utilities, tax, tort (personal injury and negligence);
  - unemployment insurance, workers' compensation.
- **Write the research question.** State your goal, then list specific questions. The book's examples:
  - "My goal is to file a lawsuit against the furniture store that damaged my hardwood floor…" Questions: what law did they break, where do I file, what paperwork do I need.
  - "My goal is to create a list of questions so I can discuss with my attorney the estate planning strategies… that will avoid probate." Here the questions guide the meeting rather than needing answers.

## §7 Index techniques

**The Cartwheel** (from William Statsky) has 18 steps. For each of the following categories, first identify the words, then look up each one in the index and the table of contents:
1. the major words from the facts;
2. broader categories;
3. narrower categories;
4. synonyms;
5. antonyms;
6. closely related words;
7. procedural terms;
8. agencies;
9. long shots.

In the book's "wedding" example, the long shots include dowry, bigamy and antenuptial. "You must be imaginative."

**A simpler method** (the book's own):
1. Choose plain-English key terms and alternatives (drunk driving, operating under the influence, driver's license or operator's permit).
2. Pick a probable legal category (motor vehicles).
3. Follow cross-references.
4. Search the subentries.
5. Go to another main entry if the first doesn't work (alcohol, traffic offenses).
6. Think of more detailed terms.

**Index jargon:**
- "Generally, this index": the term is a main entry elsewhere in the index.
- "See": the material is under the named term.
- "See also": related material.
- "*Infra*": further down the same entry. "*Supra*": further up.

**Online:** use top-down menus, keyword searches or keyword menus, applying the same persistence.
