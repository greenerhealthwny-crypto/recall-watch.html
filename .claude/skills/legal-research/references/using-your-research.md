# Organizing and Using Your Research (Ch 9)

All points are from the book *(book)* unless marked *(std)*.

## §1 Organize as you go

- **Keep separate folders** (or accordion files) **for each research question.** If a document covers several issues, file it under the first or most important one.
- **Annotate each case:** mark where the **issue, facts, decision and rationale** appear.
- **In the library,** copy the statutes and cases you need. You don't need to copy the Shepard's pages unless you'll research the listed cases.
- **Your plan depends on your goal:** filing a court document, meeting a lawyer, writing an academic paper, or reporting to your boss.

## §2 The legal memorandum

**A memo is not a brief.** An internal memo lays out the facts and the law neutrally, for yourself or your employer. A **brief** (or "memorandum of points and authorities") is filed in court and argues one side.

**Its parts** follow the elements of a court opinion:

1. **Statement of facts.** Only the **legally relevant** facts. Example: a new owner trying to evict a tenant over pets needs the lease terms, the date ownership changed, and what the prior landlord allowed.
2. **Question presented** (the issue). One succinct question, usually answerable "yes" or "no". Example: "Can a new owner of an apartment house in California evict a tenant for having pets even though a prior landlord allowed them?"
3. **Decision** (conclusion or answer). Your predicted holding. Example: "Probably not. The new owner takes ownership subject to the agreements… negotiated by the prior owner."
4. **Discussion** (analysis). "Really the most important part": apply the law to the facts using **IRAC**:
   - **Issue:** the problem the case will answer. Example: is a Louisiana employer liable when an employee, driving negligently while on a work call, hits a pedestrian?
   - **Rule:** what the case holds. In *Ellender v. Neff Rental, Inc.* (a Louisiana appellate case), an employee on a work-related call on an employer-provided phone was acting in the course and scope of employment, so the employer was **vicariously liable**.
   - **Analysis:** show how your facts match the case, or differ from it.
   - **Conclusion:** what the case leads you to conclude about your situation.
5. **Use proper citations** throughout. Citations are "the roadmap" for the reader.

**Writing a memo for someone else** (a supervising lawyer or an employer): follow their format, and be objective about weaknesses in the case *(book, paraphrased)*.

## §3 Going to court: the basics

**The usual sequence of a civil case:**
1. **Jurisdiction and venue.** Jurisdiction rules decide whether a court has the authority to hear the case. Venue rules decide *where* it is heard. You can't sue in Nebraska over an Oregon incident between Oregonians.
2. **Complaint.** The plaintiff files a complaint (some courts have fill-in **complaint forms**) and **serves** the defendant.
3. **Defendant's response.** The defendant must respond in writing within a set time, often 30 days. The response can be:
   - an **answer**, which denies the allegations and may raise defenses or counterclaims;
   - a **motion to dismiss or demurrer**, which argues that even if the facts are true there is no legal claim. If it is granted without leave to amend, the case ends.
4. **Discovery.** The sides exchange information through **interrogatories** (courts limit how many), document requests and **depositions**. Motions can enforce discovery, or limit abusive requests.
5. **Motions.** For example, **summary judgment** argues that there are no genuine issues of material fact, so the case can be decided without a trial. Like a demurrer, it is dispositive.
6. **Before trial.** A filing to **set the case for trial**. Some courts require **settlement conferences**, or nonbinding **arbitration** (either side can reject the award and go to trial).
7. **Trial.** Either side can demand a jury; otherwise a judge decides.
   - The trial runs: jury selection, then motions before trial (*in limine*), then opening statements, then the plaintiff's evidence, then the defendant's, then closing arguments.
   - Next come **jury instructions**. Find your state's online, for example CACI and CALCRIM in California. Federal instructions are in Federal Jury Practice and Instructions, or Modern Federal Jury Instructions.
   - After a bench trial the judge may issue "Findings of Fact and Conclusions of Law".
8. **Appeal.** Typically a notice of appeal within a strict deadline, often 30 days, followed by written briefs (from the book's glossary).

**Other points:**
- **Most lawsuits never go to trial;** they settle.
- **Small claims court** exists in all states, with simplified rules. Clerks are often required to help. In many states lawyers aren't allowed to represent the parties. It is not designed for complex cases. Nolo's guide is *Everybody's Guide to Small Claims Court*.
- **Court documents:** use your court's forms where they exist. Follow the local rules on format and filing. Use your research to cite the law.

## §4 Finding and working with a lawyer

- **Finding one:**
  - **Best:** a recommendation from someone who worked with that lawyer. Lawyers specialize **by subject and by place**, so you need someone local, in the right area of law.
  - **Next:** local professional or trade groups. For example, an apartment association for landlords.
  - **Then:** a referral service or directory. Nolo's Lawyer Directory lists education, experience, practice areas and fees. Check the lawyer's discipline history too.
- **Fees:**
  - **Contingency:** paid only if you win or settle. About **25% of a settlement** is typical, and **33% after a trial win** isn't uncommon.
  - **Flat fee.**
  - **Hourly:** set a **time limit** after which the lawyer must call you before doing more work.
- **Flexible arrangements:** use your own research to cut costs. Come prepared with the questions from your research. You may be able to agree that you do some of the work while the lawyer advises (the book's phrase is a "more flexible work arrangement").
  - *(std)* Many states allow **limited-scope ("unbundled") representation**, where a lawyer handles only part of a matter or coaches you. Check your state's rules.
- **Why research helps even with a lawyer:** you understand the issues, ask better questions, use billable time efficiently, and can check that your case is handled well.

**Research plan**

```
Goal: (sue / defend / meet a lawyer / paper)
Jurisdiction: state ___, county/city ___, federal? ___
Type: civil or criminal | substantive or procedural | area of law ___
Questions:
  1.
  2.
Search terms (Cartwheel): broader / narrower / synonyms / related / procedural / agencies / long shots
Start with (secondary): Nolo title or article / state encyclopedia / practice guide
Primary law to find: statute ___ | regulation ___ | cases ___ | court rules ___
Validate: Shepard's or KeyCite at the law library; pocket parts; official site date
```

**Memo**

```
TO / FROM / DATE / RE
Facts: (legally relevant only)
Question presented: (one yes/no question)
Brief answer: (probably yes/no + one-line reason)
Discussion (IRAC for each issue): Issue / Rule (cite) / Analysis (compare facts) / Conclusion
Next steps / open questions
```
