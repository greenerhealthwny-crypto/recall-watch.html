# Glute Lab (Bret Contreras)

A working guide distilled from *Glute Lab: The Art and Science of Strength and Physique Training* by Bret Contreras, PhD ("the Glute Guy") with Glen Cordoza (Victory Belt, 2019). Contreras popularized the barbell hip thrust and coaches mostly women with physique and strength goals. The book has five parts:
1. **Why train the glutes:** aesthetics, health (injury and pain reduction), strength, performance.
2. **The science:** anatomy, function, genetics, how muscle grows, how to gain strength, exercise categorization.
3. **The art:** fundamentals, the eight program design variables, advanced methods, troubleshooting.
4. **Periodization and programs:** periodization, training splits, sport-specific glute training, and three 12-week programs (novice, intermediate, advanced).
5. **Exercises:** glute-dominant, quad-dominant and hamstring-dominant exercise libraries with technique, faults and corrections.

**Source coverage:** Built from the full EPUB the user supplied. The three 12-week programs and the exercise-by-exercise instructions are summarized, not reproduced.

## Core principles

1. **Hip extension builds glutes best, but one exercise can't do everything.** Peak glute activation happens at end-range hip extension (top of a hip thrust). Squats and deadlifts load the glutes stretched and mainly hit the lower glute max. Abduction hits the upper glutes. Use a "shotgun approach": variety in exercises, loads, vectors and tempos.
2. **Mechanical tension first, metabolic stress second, a little muscle damage.** Get stronger over time (progressive overload) *and* feel the working muscle (mind-muscle connection). Neither alone is enough.
3. **The Rule of Thirds.** About a third of glute work each for horizontal (hip thrust/bridge), vertical (squat/deadlift/lunge) and lateral-rotary (abduction, external rotation). A third of sets heavy (1–5 reps), a third medium (6–12), a third light (13–50). A third to failure or 1 shy, a third 2–3 shy, a third nowhere near failure.
4. **Effort beats volume.** Most people (especially women Contreras coaches) do too much volume with too little effort. Pushing sets close to failure with good form matters more than more sets.
5. **Three glute days a week is the sweet spot for most;** two is far better than one. You can train glutes more often with low-damage exercises (bridges, bands, abduction).
6. **Form before load, and progress isn't linear.** Standardize technique, retest baselines yearly, train around pain rather than through it, and expect progress in waves.
7. **Individualize.** Genetics, anatomy and preference drive exercise choice, volume and frequency. Keep what feels right and grows you; drop what hurts.
8. **Have fun and stay consistent.** Adherence is the long game.

**Workflow for writing a program or workout:**
1. **Gather:** goal (glute size, strength, sport, fat loss), training age, days per week and session length, equipment (home/bands/commercial gym), injuries or pain, exercises they like or hate, and whether they're pregnant or postpartum.
2. **Choose frequency and split** (§8): usually 3 glute days; full body or one of the splits.
3. **Pick exercises with the Rule of Thirds:** roughly four glute exercises per session at 3x/week (one or two horizontal, one or two vertical, one or two lateral-rotary), big lifts first, burnout last.
4. **Set loads, reps and effort** with the Rule of Thirds; 3–4 sets per exercise; about 16 hard sets per session as an upper guide; 10–40 weekly glute sets depending on the person.
5. **Plan progression:** 4-week cycles (week 1 easy/deload → week 4 hardest), monthly focus rotation, and how to log and beat numbers.
6. **Add technique cues and swaps** for anything that hurts or isn't felt in the glutes.

## Output style and safety

- Be specific: exercises, sets × reps, effort targets (RIR), rest, and progression. Use tables for programs.
- Explain the *why* briefly with Contreras's reasoning (vector, tension, pump, stretch), and say when something is his coaching experience versus research.
- This is training education, not medical advice. For pain that persists or is sharp, injuries, pregnancy, postpartum, heart conditions or other medical issues, recommend clearance from a physician or physical therapist; never tell anyone to train through "bad" pain.
- For pregnancy, follow the book's cautious guidance (§7) and defer to the user's obstetric provider.
- On diet, keep to the book's general guidelines; for eating disorders, medical conditions or very low-calorie plans, refer to a registered dietitian. Avoid language that shames bodies; the book emphasizes praising effort and focusing on what you control.
- Don't reproduce the book's programs or long passages verbatim; build original programs from its principles.
