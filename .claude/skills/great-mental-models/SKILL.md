# The Great Mental Models, Volume 1: General Thinking Concepts (Farnam Street)

A working guide to *The Great Mental Models Vol. 1* by Shane Parrish and Rhiannon Beaubien (Farnam Street) *(book)*. The book argues that the quality of our thinking depends on the models in our heads, and that we need a **variety** of models from many disciplines, not just more of the ones we already use *(book)*.

**Source:** the user's PDF is a scan with a text layer (227 pages). Points marked *(book)* were checked in it.

## The nine models

| # | Model | Core idea | Key tools *(book)* |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The map is not the territory** | Models are reductions of reality; useful but imperfect and can go stale (Korzybski, 1931) | (1) **Reality is the ultimate update**: update maps from experience; (2) **Consider the cartographer**: maps reflect their makers' values and limits; (3) **Maps can influence territories**: Jane Jacobs on planners forcing cities to fit models. Also: Tragedy of the Commons sidebar |
| 2 | **Circle of competence** | Know what you know (the "Lifer") vs what you think you know (the "Stranger"); ego creates blind spots | Build and maintain it with **curiosity and a desire to learn, monitoring** (your track record), and **feedback**. Outside it: (1) learn at least the basics, (2) talk to someone with a strong circle there, (3) use broad mental models |
| 3 | **First principles thinking** | Separate the underlying facts from assumptions; rebuild from what is non-reducible in context | **Socratic questioning** (6 steps: clarify; challenge assumptions; look for evidence; consider alternatives; examine consequences; question the original question) and the **Five Whys** |
| 4 | **Thought experiment** | "Devices of the imagination used to investigate the nature of things" | Steps: ask a question → background research → hypothesis → test with thought experiments → analyze and conclude → compare and adjust. Example: LeBron James vs Woody Allen (easy) vs James vs Kevin Durant (hard). Trolley problem sidebar |
| 5 | **Second-order thinking** | Consider the effects of the effects ("law of unintended consequences") | Use for (1) prioritizing long-term interests over immediate gains, (2) constructing effective arguments. Example: the British cobra bounty in Delhi led people to breed cobras |
| 6 | **Probabilistic thinking** | Estimate likelihoods with math and logic, since the future is uncertain | (1) **Bayesian thinking**: update prior beliefs with new data; (2) **fat-tailed curves**: extremes are far larger than in a bell curve (wealth vs height); (3) **asymmetries**: "metaprobability", the probability that your estimates themselves are any good (investors routinely forecast 20–40% returns that are rarely achieved). Sidebars: conditional probability, orders of magnitude, antifragility |
| 7 | **Inversion** | Approach a problem from the opposite end; "avoiding stupidity is easier than seeking brilliance" | Two approaches: (1) assume what you want to prove is true or false and show what else would have to be true; (2) think about what you want to avoid. Jacobi: "invert, always invert". Examples: Hippasus and the square root of 2; Bernays and Lucky Strike; Marie Van Brittan Brown inventing CCTV. **Kurt Lewin's force field analysis** (5 steps) |
| 8 | **Occam's razor** | Simpler explanations are more likely true; prefer the one with the fewest moving parts | Caveat: some things are not simple; medical-field sidebar |
| 9 | **Hanlon's razor** | Don't attribute to malice what is more easily explained by stupidity; the likeliest explanation has the least intent | Avoids paranoia and ideology; road-rage example; "the Devil Fallacy" sidebar |

**Supporting ideas** *(book)*: falsifiability (Karl Popper), necessity and sufficiency, causation vs correlation.

## Force field analysis (Kurt Lewin) *(book)*
1. Identify the problem. 2. Define your objective. 3. Identify forces that support change toward the objective. 4. Identify forces that impede it. 5. Strategize: strengthen step 3 forces and reduce or remove step 4 forces.

## How to use this skill

**Decision workflow (combining the models):**
1. **Map check:** what model am I using, who made it, is it out of date?
2. **Circle check:** is this inside my competence? If not, learn basics, consult an expert, apply broad models.
3. **First principles:** list assumptions and challenge each with Socratic questions or Five Whys.
4. **Thought experiment:** simulate the options and their likely outcomes.
5. **Second order:** "and then what?" for each option, over 10 minutes, 10 months, 10 years *(std framing)*.
6. **Probabilities:** estimate base rates, update with evidence (Bayes), check for fat tails and asymmetric errors.
7. **Invert:** what would guarantee failure? Avoid it; run force field analysis.
8. **Razors:** prefer the simplest explanation; don't assume malice.

## Caveats
- Volume 1 covers only these general thinking concepts; later volumes cover physics, chemistry, biology, systems, economics and art *(std)*.
- Models are aids, not answers; the book itself warns that maps are imperfect *(book)*.
