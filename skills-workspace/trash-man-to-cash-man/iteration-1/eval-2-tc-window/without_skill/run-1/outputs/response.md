# Myron Golden: The "Window of Opportunity" and the Financial House

> **How sure I am:** This is written from memory, without the book in front of me. The overall framework below matches what Myron Golden teaches in *From the Trash Man to the Cash Man* and in his talks. The exact wording, diagrams and labels in his book may differ, so check the book before you quote it.

---

## 1. The core idea: every household is a "financial house"

Golden pictures each person's finances as a **house**. Money moves into it and out of it through openings:

- **The front door is income.** Money comes in through a paycheck, business revenue or other earnings.
- **The back door is expenses.** Money leaves to pay for living costs, consumption and debt payments.
- **The window is opportunity.** This third opening is where money goes out to buy or build **assets**. Those assets then send money back into the house.

His point is that most people only think about the doors: earn more at the front, spend less at the back. The wealthy put their attention on the **window**. They use income to acquire or create things that produce more income.

## 2. The "Window of Opportunity"

To Golden, the Window of Opportunity is the space between what comes in and what has to go out. It is your **margin**: money that is not already spoken for. You can use that margin to:

1. **Consume it.** Spend it on lifestyle, and the window "closes."
2. **Commit it to liabilities.** Take on payments for things that lose value, such as cars, gadgets or a bigger house financed with debt.
3. **Invest it through the window into assets.** Buy or build things that put money in your pocket: a business, offers and products, real estate, intellectual property, investments.

He usually adds that the window is about **seeing** opportunity as much as having money. A rich mindset looks for problems to solve and value to create, because that is how wealth is built. A poor mindset looks at price tags and limits.

Related teachings he links to this:
- **Assets put money in your pocket. Liabilities take money out.** This is a Kiyosaki-style definition that Golden uses heavily.
- **The marketplace pays you for the value you bring.** To widen your window, raise the value you deliver. Moving from selling time to selling skills, products and offers is how you climb.
- **Stewardship and multiplication.** He often draws on Bible principles, such as the parable of the talents: money is meant to be put to work, not buried.

## 3. The financial house of the poor, middle class and rich

| | **Poor** | **Middle class** | **Rich** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Where income comes from** | Mostly trading time for money (a job or hourly work) | Mostly a job or profession with higher pay, still time-for-money | Mostly assets: businesses, investments, IP, real estate |
| **Where money goes** | Straight out the back door to living expenses | To liabilities they *think* are assets: house, cars, toys, financed with debt | Through the window into assets first, then lifestyle is paid *from* asset income |
| **State of the window** | Effectively shut. There is no margin, and money goes out as fast as it comes in. | Shut by payments. There is margin in theory, but debt service uses it up. | Wide open. Margin is put to work on purpose. |
| **Typical mindset** | "I can't afford it." Survival and consumption. | "I deserve it." Status, security and looking rich. | "How can I afford it?" Ownership, value creation and multiplication. |
| **Result over time** | Stuck: income in, income out | The rat race: higher income, matching expenses, often more debt | Compounding: assets buy more assets, and income grows without more hours worked |

### The poor man's house
Money comes in the front door and goes right out the back door. Every dollar is already spent on necessities and consumption. There is no window, so nothing is left to send out that could come back. The way out is to **create margin** and **raise the value you bring to the marketplace**.

### The middle-class house
Income is higher, but so are expenses. The middle class fills the house with **liabilities that look like assets**, such as a bigger mortgage, new cars and consumer debt. The window exists, but monthly payments block it. They often look prosperous while having little real freedom. Golden's critique is that they are trying to *look* rich instead of *becoming* rich.

### The rich man's house
The rich route money **through the window first**. They buy or build income-producing assets, and those assets pay for the lifestyle. Income from the assets comes back in the front door, which makes the margin bigger, which means more can go through the window. That is the compounding loop that builds wealth over generations.

## 4. The practical takeaways Golden draws from it

1. **Open your window.** Create margin by spending less than you earn and avoiding consumer debt.
2. **Know what an asset is.** If it doesn't put money in your pocket, don't count it as one.
3. **Feed assets before lifestyle.** Let asset income pay for the luxuries.
4. **Raise your value in the marketplace.** Move from selling time to selling skills, then products and offers, then ownership. This is the "trash man to cash man" climb.
5. **Change how you see things first.** The window is opened by thinking about opportunity, not about lack.

---

*Summary:* The doors of the financial house (income and expenses) decide whether you get by. The window (opportunity, meaning margin sent into assets) decides whether you get rich. The poor have no window, the middle class block theirs with liabilities, and the rich keep theirs wide open.
