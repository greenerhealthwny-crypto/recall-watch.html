# What most likely made your guests sick: *Clostridium perfringens*

## Why this is the top suspect

Your outbreak matches the textbook pattern for *Clostridium perfringens* food poisoning very closely:

| Clue from your dinner | Why it points to *C. perfringens* |
|---|---|
| **Roast beef and gravy** | Meat, poultry and gravy are the classic foods. *C. perfringens* lives on raw meat and in the environment. |
| **Cooked the day before** | Cooking kills the growing bacteria but **not their spores**. Heat also "activates" the spores and drives oxygen out of the food, which helps them germinate. |
| **Cooled overnight in a big stock pot on the counter** | This is the main cause. A large, deep pot of hot food cools very slowly and sits for hours in the "danger zone" (roughly 10–52 °C / 50–125 °F). *C. perfringens* can double about every 10 minutes at around 43–47 °C (109–117 °F), so it can reach huge numbers overnight. The middle of a deep pot also has little oxygen, which this bacterium needs. |
| **Reheated before serving** | Warming it up, or reheating it unevenly, often does not kill all the vegetative cells. Many people then swallow very large numbers of live bacteria. |
| **Symptoms 10–12 hours after the meal** | The usual incubation period is **6–24 hours, most often 8–12**. In the gut the bacteria form spores and release an enterotoxin. |
| **Cramps and diarrhea, little vomiting, no fever** | This is the classic picture. Vomiting and fever are uncommon. Illness usually lasts less than 24 hours. |
| **High attack rate (30 of 40 people)** | Typical when one shared dish was heavily contaminated. |

### What else it could be, and why it is less likely

- ***Bacillus cereus* (diarrheal type):** a similar illness (cramps and watery diarrhea 6–15 hours after eating) and similar causes, including meats, gravies and foods cooled slowly. Only lab testing can reliably tell the two apart, and the prevention steps are the same. Its vomiting type is usually linked to rice and starchy foods and starts within 1–6 hours with a lot of vomiting, which does not match your outbreak.
- ***Staphylococcus aureus*:** starts sooner (30 minutes to 8 hours) and causes a lot of vomiting. That does not match.
- **Norovirus:** vomiting is prominent, it spreads from person to person, and it often causes a low fever. That does not match well.
- ***Salmonella* / *Campylobacter*:** usually start 12–72 hours after eating, often with fever and sometimes bloody stools. That does not match well.

## What to do now

1. **Most people get better on their own within 24 hours.** Drink plenty of fluids, such as water, oral rehydration solutions or broth.
2. **See a doctor** if someone has signs of dehydration (very little urine, dizziness, a dry mouth), bloody stools, a high fever, or symptoms lasting more than 2–3 days. Take extra care with elderly people, young children, pregnant people and anyone with a weakened immune system. Severe cases are rare, but they happen most often in older or frail people.
3. **Report it to your local health department.** An illness affecting 30 people counts as an outbreak, and health departments want to hear about it. They may help test leftover food or stool samples to confirm the cause. If any leftovers remain, keep them refrigerated and labeled rather than throwing them out, so they can be tested. Do not eat them.

## How to stop it happening again

The key idea is that **the spores survive cooking, so you have to stop them from multiplying afterward.** Getting the food cold quickly and reheating it hot enough are what matter most.

### 1. Cool food fast, and never on the counter overnight
- Follow the standard **two-stage cooling rule**:
  - from **57 °C (135 °F) down to 21 °C (70 °F) within 2 hours**, then
  - from **21 °C down to 5 °C (41 °F) or below within the next 4 hours**, for 6 hours in total at most.
- **Split large batches.** Divide gravy and meat into **shallow pans, no more than about 5 cm (2 inches) deep**. Never cool food in a deep stock pot.
- **Cut roasts into smaller pieces** (around 2–3 kg / 6 lb or less), or slice them before chilling.
- **Speed up cooling** with an ice-water bath, by stirring the gravy, or with ice wands or "cooling paddles".
- Put the food in the fridge **uncovered, or loosely covered, until it is cold**, and leave space between containers so air can move around them.
- **Use a probe thermometer** to check, and write down the temperatures and times.

### 2. Reheat thoroughly
- Reheat leftovers quickly, within 2 hours, until **every part reaches at least 74 °C (165 °F)**. Bring gravy to a **rolling boil** and stir it.
- Do not use slow cookers or warming trays to reheat food. They warm it too slowly and keep it in the danger zone.

### 3. Hold food at a safe temperature while serving
- Keep hot food at **57 °C (135 °F) or hotter** in chafing dishes, hot-holding units or a low oven, and check it with a thermometer.
- Keep cold food at **5 °C (41 °F) or colder**.
- Throw out food that has stayed at room temperature for more than 2 hours (1 hour in hot weather).

### 4. Plan around the event
- Where possible, **cook the meat on the day** and serve it straight away.
- If you have to cook ahead, plan for fridge space, shallow pans and ice before you start cooking.
- **Make gravy fresh** on the day, or bring cooled gravy back to a boil just before serving.
- Do not save and reuse leftovers from the serving line.

### 5. People and training
- Have at least one volunteer complete a **food handler or food safety course**. Many health departments and extension services offer free or low-cost courses for community and church kitchens.
- Put up a simple **"Cool – Reheat – Hold" temperature chart** in the church kitchen and keep a thermometer there.
- Volunteers who have had vomiting or diarrhea should not cook for **at least 48 hours after their symptoms stop**. This is good practice in general, although it was not the likely cause here.

## The short version

The spores in the beef and gravy survived cooking. Then the pot cooled slowly overnight, and they grew to huge numbers. Reheating did not kill them all. Next time, **chill food quickly in shallow containers in the fridge, reheat it to 74 °C (165 °F) or a boil, and keep it hot (57 °C / 135 °F or more) until you serve it.**
