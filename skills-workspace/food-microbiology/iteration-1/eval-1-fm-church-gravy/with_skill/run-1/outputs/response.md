# Church dinner illness: most likely *Clostridium perfringens*

I'm sorry this happened. Your description fits one organism very closely. Only lab testing can confirm it, though, so read this as the most likely explanation rather than a diagnosis.

## The most likely cause

The most likely cause is ***Clostridium perfringens* (type A) food poisoning**, carried by the roast beef and/or gravy. Each part of your story matches its known pattern.

| What you saw | Why it points to *C. perfringens* |
|---|---|
| Onset **10–12 hours** after the meal | Typical incubation is about **8–16 hours** (often 8–12). That is too slow for a toxin already formed in the food (*S. aureus* takes about 2–6 h) and too fast for infections like *Salmonella* or *Campylobacter* (usually a day or more). |
| **Cramps and diarrhea**, **little vomiting, no fever** | This is the classic picture. The toxin is released in the gut and mainly causes diarrhea and abdominal pain. Vomiting and fever are uncommon. |
| **Roast beef and gravy** | Large cuts of cooked meat, gravies and stews are the classic foods. |
| Cooked the day before, **cooled overnight in a big stock pot on the counter**, then reheated | This is the textbook failure that causes these outbreaks: a large mass of food cooled slowly. |
| **About 75% of guests** (30 of 40) ill | A high attack rate from one shared, heavily contaminated dish is typical. |

**Less likely, but possible: diarrheal *Bacillus cereus*.** It has a similar onset (about 6–14 h), similar diarrhea-and-cramps symptoms, and the same cause (slow cooling of meats and gravies). Only stool or food testing can tell the two apart. The prevention steps are the same for both.

## Why it happened: the microbiology

1. ***C. perfringens* forms spores.** Raw meat and spices often carry its spores, which are found in soil and animal intestines. Normal cooking kills ordinary bacteria but **not the spores**.
2. **Cooking actually helps it.** Heat "activates" the spores so they are ready to germinate. Cooking also kills competing bacteria and drives oxygen out of the food. *C. perfringens* is an anaerobe, so a deep pot of gravy or a thick roast suits it well.
3. **Slow cooling lets it grow.** A large stock pot on the counter can stay in the danger zone (roughly 40–140 °F) for many hours. The centre may stay warm almost all night. *C. perfringens* is among the fastest-growing bacteria known: near 109–113 °F it can double about every **7–10 minutes**. Overnight, a few spores can become millions of cells per gram.
4. **The reheat wasn't enough.** If the food is only warmed rather than brought to a full, even boil or to 165 °F throughout, many cells survive. Reheating also cannot undo hours of growth.
5. **The illness starts in the gut.** People who eat food with very high counts (about a million or more cells per gram) swallow cells that form spores in the intestine. As they do, they release an enterotoxin, which causes the cramps and diarrhea. This is why symptoms appear after many hours rather than quickly.

Most of the risk came from **cooling a large volume slowly at room temperature**. Large-batch slow cooling is one of the most common causes of foodborne outbreaks.

## How to stop it happening again

### 1. Cool food fast (the most important fix)
- **Don't leave cooked food to cool on the counter**, especially in a deep pot.
- Follow the FDA Food Code standard: cool from **135 °F to 70 °F within 2 hours**, then to **41 °F or below within a further 4 hours** (6 hours in total).
- Practical ways to meet it:
  - **Divide food into shallow pans**, about 2 inches deep or less, and refrigerate them uncovered or loosely covered until cold.
  - **Slice or cut roasts** into smaller pieces, or into portions of about 4 inches thick or less, before chilling.
  - Stand the pot of gravy or stock in an **ice-water bath** and stir it. Ice paddles or cooling wands also help.
  - Leave space around containers in the fridge so air can circulate, and don't overload a domestic fridge with hot food.
- Use a **probe thermometer** to check that the food is actually getting cold in time.

### 2. Reheat properly
- Reheat leftovers quickly to **165 °F (74 °C) throughout**. Bring gravy to a **rolling boil**.
- Don't slowly warm food in a roaster, slow cooker or warming oven, because that is more time in the danger zone.

### 3. Hold hot, or hold cold
- Once food is hot, keep it at **135 °F or above**. The textbook cites 140 °F; the current FDA Food Code uses 135 °F. Use chafing dishes or roasters that are actually hot enough, and check with a thermometer.
- Otherwise, keep it at **41 °F or below** until reheating.
- Don't let food sit warm for hours before serving.

### 4. Plan the menu around the risk
- If possible, **cook large roasts and gravy on the day** and serve them straight away. Otherwise, cook the meat ahead and make the gravy fresh.
- Make a simple **checklist for volunteers**: cooling method, temperature checks, reheat temperature, and a log sheet. Church and community kitchens are a classic setting for these outbreaks, because well-meaning cooks prepare large amounts for big groups.
- Consider a **food handler course** for your regular kitchen volunteers. Many local health departments offer short ones.

## What to do now

- **Ill guests:** Most *C. perfringens* illness is short (usually under 24 hours) and resolves on its own. People should drink plenty of fluids. Anyone who is elderly, very young, pregnant or has other health problems should **see a clinician**, as should anyone with severe or lasting symptoms, signs of dehydration, **bloody diarrhea, high fever, or symptoms lasting more than 2–3 days**. Those symptoms would suggest a different cause.
- **Report it to your local health department.** An outbreak affecting 30 people is something they will want to know about. They can collect stool samples and test any **leftover food**, which can confirm the organism. If any roast beef or gravy is left, keep it refrigerated and labelled, and don't eat it or throw it away until they advise you.
- Write down **who ate what**, when symptoms started, and how each dish was prepared and stored. This helps the investigation, and it may show whether the beef, the gravy or both were the source.
