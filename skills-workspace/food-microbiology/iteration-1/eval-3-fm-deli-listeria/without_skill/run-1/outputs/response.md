# Controlling *Listeria monocytogenes* in Cooked, Chilled, Sliced Deli Turkey Breast

## 1. Why Listeria is your biggest hazard

Deli turkey breast carries more risk than almost any other ready-to-eat (RTE) meat:

- **Cooking kills Listeria.** A properly cooked log is effectively free of it. Nearly every outbreak and recall involving deli meat comes from **post-lethality contamination**: the bacteria get onto the product after cooking, usually during bag stripping, slicing, conveying or packaging.
- **It grows in the fridge.** *L. monocytogenes* grows at 0–4 °C, tolerates salt, and does well in moist, low-acid, high-pH turkey with little competing flora. That is especially true of uncured or "natural" turkey that has no nitrite.
- **It settles into plants.** It forms harbourage sites in drains, slicer parts, conveyor belts, hollow rollers, cracked floors, condensate, door gaskets and cooling units. Once established it can persist for years.
- **Your customers include vulnerable people.** Deli meat is eaten without further cooking by pregnant women, the elderly and immunocompromised people. Fatality rates in those groups are about 20–30%.
- **Regulators have no tolerance for it.** In the US, *L. monocytogenes* in an RTE product makes it adulterated. USDA-FSIS sets specific requirements in **9 CFR 430 (the "Listeria Rule")**. Canada (Health Canada Listeria Policy, Category 1 vs 2) and the EU (Reg. 2073/2005: absence in 25 g at production for products that support growth, ≤100 CFU/g through shelf life only if you can show it won't exceed that) have similar frameworks.

**Bottom line:** the cook step makes the product safe. Everything between the cooker and the sealed package decides whether it stays safe.

---

## 2. Choose your Listeria control "Alternative" (US FSIS framework)

Under 9 CFR 430, every establishment making post-lethality-exposed RTE product must pick one of three Alternatives. This choice drives the rest of your plan.

| Alternative | Controls | Sampling burden |
|---|---|---|
| **Alt 1** | Post-lethality treatment (PLT) **plus** antimicrobial agent/process (AMAP) that limits growth | Lowest |
| **Alt 2a** | PLT only | Moderate |
| **Alt 2b** | AMAP (e.g., lactate/diacetate, buffered vinegar) **plus** sanitation | Moderate–higher |
| **Alt 3** | Sanitation program only | Highest; most scrutiny, hold-and-test after repeat positives |

**Recommendation for a small deli-turkey plant:** aim for **Alternative 1**. Put a validated growth inhibitor in the formulation, for example cultured sugar/vinegar, buffered vinegar, or potassium lactate plus sodium diacetate. Add a PLT on the final package, either **high-pressure processing (HPP)** or post-package pasteurization. If you can't fund HPP yet, Alt 2b with a validated inhibitor is a reasonable interim choice. Don't run sliced deli turkey under Alt 3 unless you have an exceptionally strong sanitation and testing program.

## 3. Hazard analysis: main hazards by process step

Typical flow: Receive raw breast and ingredients → cold storage → injection/tumbling with brine → stuff/form into casings or cook-in bags → **cook** → **chill** → strip casing → **slice** → package → (PLT) → label → cold storage → ship.

| Step | Biological hazards | Chemical hazards | Physical hazards |
|---|---|---|---|
| Receiving raw turkey | *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, *L. monocytogenes*, *C. perfringens* spores (reasonably likely; controlled later by cooking) | Drug residues (supplier-controlled) | Bone fragments, foreign material |
| Receiving non-meat ingredients | Spore-formers in spices | **Allergens** (soy, milk, wheat in some brines/flavourings); sodium nitrite if cured | — |
| Brine prep / injection / tumbling | Cross-contamination; injection needles can push surface bacteria inside | Over-addition of nitrite (if cured), phosphate limits | Broken injector needles (**metal**) |
| **Cooking** | **Survival of *Salmonella*, *L. monocytogenes*** if undercooked | — | — |
| **Chilling / stabilization** | **Outgrowth of *C. perfringens* and *C. botulinum*** spores during slow cooling; recontamination if brine/water chilled in permeable casings | — | — |
| Casing stripping / peeling | **Recontamination with *L. monocytogenes*** (product first exposed here) | Sanitizer residues | — |
| **Slicing / conveying / packaging** | **Recontamination with *L. monocytogenes*** from slicer blades, belts, hands, gloves, air, condensate | Sanitizer residues, lubricants | **Metal** from slicer blades, packaging equipment |
| Post-lethality treatment (if used) | Insufficient Listeria reduction | — | — |
| Labelling | — | **Undeclared allergens**, wrong product/label | — |
| Cold storage / distribution | **Listeria growth** if temperature abused; growth over long shelf life | — | — |

## 4. Critical Control Points (CCPs)

The limits below are common, defensible starting values. **Validate each one against your own product, equipment and supporting documentation** (FSIS Appendix A/B as revised, peer-reviewed literature, or in-plant challenge studies).

### CCP 1: Cooking (lethality)
- **Hazard:** survival of *Salmonella* and *L. monocytogenes*.
- **Critical limit:** a validated lethality for poultry (a **7-log *Salmonella* reduction**). The common simple limit is an internal temperature of **≥165 °F (73.9 °C) instantaneous**, measured in the coldest point of the thickest log. A validated time–temperature combination at a lower temperature is also acceptable, provided you meet any humidity requirements in your support document.
- **Monitoring:** calibrated probe in the slowest-heating log, or continuous data loggers, on every batch or oven load. Record the time and temperature.
- **Corrective action:** extend the cook, or hold and evaluate the product, then recook or destroy it. Find the cause (oven load, log size, steam failure) and fix it.
- **Verification:** daily thermometer calibration check (ice point), periodic oven heat-distribution study, and records review before shipment.

### CCP 2: Chilling / stabilization
- **Hazard:** germination and outgrowth of *C. perfringens* and *C. botulinum* spores that survive cooking.
- **Critical limit:** for example, the FSIS stabilization guidance: **130 °F → 80 °F (54.4 → 26.7 °C) within 1.5 h, and 80 °F → 40 °F (26.7 → 4.4 °C) within 5 h**. The goal is ≤1-log *C. perfringens* growth and no *C. botulinum* growth. A different option, such as the longer cooling schedule allowed for cured products, needs its own validation.
- **Monitoring:** core temperature of the largest log at set intervals, or continuous loggers.
- **Corrective action:** model the deviation with a validated predictive tool (e.g., ComBase/PMP or a *C. perfringens* growth model), sample the product, then hold, rework, or destroy it.
- **Note:** if you chill in **brine or chill water**, protect it from Listeria. Use impermeable casings, treat and replace the brine (UV, antimicrobial, scheduled changes), and treat the brine system as a known Listeria niche.

### CCP 3: Post-lethality treatment (if you use one; required for Alt 1/2a)
Use one of these validated options:
- **High-pressure processing:** e.g., about **87,000 psi (≈600 MPa) for 3–4 min** at the validated hold time, often 3–5 log reduction. Monitor pressure, hold time and water temperature on every cycle.
- **Post-package pasteurization (hot water/steam/infrared):** e.g., a validated surface temperature and time for your package size and shape. Monitor water temperature and dwell time.
- **Surface antimicrobial spray/dip before final packaging** (e.g., lauric arginate, LAE). Monitor concentration, application volume and coverage.

- **Critical limit:** the parameters your validation shows achieve **≥1 log** (FSIS minimum to count as a PLT). In practice, target ≥2–3 log.
- **Corrective action:** re-treat, or hold and test or destroy the product. Repair equipment before resuming.

### CCP 4 (or a formulation control / prerequisite): Antimicrobial agent in the formulation
- **Hazard:** Listeria growth during refrigerated shelf life if post-lethality contamination occurs.
- **Critical limit:** the ingredient level shown in a challenge study or validated model to limit growth to **≤2 log over the full shelf life** at the intended storage temperature (FSIS's AMAP expectation). For example, a minimum % of lactate/diacetate or cultured vinegar in the finished product.
- **Monitoring:** weigh and record the inhibitor per batch; periodic analytical verification of finished product. Track pH, moisture and salt, since inhibitor efficacy depends on them.
- Many plants manage this as a formulation control under a prerequisite program. If you claim it for your Alternative, it must be documented and verified.

### CCP 5: Metal detection (physical hazard)
- **Critical limit:** no metal ≥ the validated test-piece size (e.g., Fe 2.0 mm, non-Fe 2.5 mm, SS 3.0 mm, adjusted for your detector and product).
- **Monitoring:** test pieces at start-up, every set interval (e.g., hourly), at changeover and at the end of the shift; reject device verified working.
- **Corrective action:** hold and re-screen everything since the last good check. Investigate the source, such as slicer blades or injector needles.

### Allergen labelling: CCP or preventive control
If any formulation contains a major allergen, verify the label against the formula at every changeover and print run. Undeclared allergens are among the most common causes of meat recalls.

## 5. Prerequisite programs that actually control Listeria

The CCPs mostly control the cook. **Post-lethality Listeria control comes mainly from prerequisite programs and sanitation**, which in the US must be documented in your Sanitation SOPs and supported in your hazard analysis. These programs matter as much as the CCPs.

### 5.1 Plant layout and hygienic zoning
- **Physically separate raw and RTE areas**, with one-way flow of people, product, equipment, tools, pallets and waste.
- Keep **positive air pressure** in the RTE slicing/packaging room relative to raw and outside areas, with filtered, dehumidified air.
- Use dedicated, colour-coded tools, clothing and footwear for the RTE room. Put foot-baths, foamers or boot washers at RTE entrances, and handwash/glove stations at entry.
- Don't let raw-side staff, maintenance or forklift traffic enter RTE without changing and sanitizing.

### 5.2 Hygienic equipment design and maintenance
- Choose slicers, conveyors and packaging machines that fully disassemble for cleaning: no hollow rollers, sealed bearings, no fabric belts.
- Use a **"seek and destroy" mindset**: find and eliminate niches such as cracked welds, worn gaskets, loose screws, damaged plastic belts, insulation and overhead pipes.
- After any maintenance or construction in the RTE room, clean and sanitize, and verify with swabs before production resumes.
- Control **condensate** and keep overhead structures dry. Avoid high-pressure hoses during production, since they aerosolize Listeria from floors and drains.

### 5.3 Sanitation (SSOPs)
- Run a full daily wet clean on the RTE line: dry clean, pre-rinse, alkaline detergent, rinse, inspect, sanitize. Rotate or periodically use an alternative sanitizer (quats, peracetic acid, chlorine) to cover biofilms.
- Schedule periodic **deep cleans**: full slicer teardown, conveyor belts removed, drains, cooling units and evaporator coils, and possibly heat treatment of slicer parts.
- **Drain management:** never use high-pressure hoses on drains during production; use drain sanitizers and dedicated drain tools.
- Pre-operational inspection, plus ATP swabs for cleaning verification. ATP is not a Listeria test.

### 5.4 Employee practices
- Training on Listeria, handwashing, glove changes, and "nothing from the floor touches the product" rules.
- Make dropped product an automatic discard or rework through a validated step.
- Use frequently sanitized gloves and aprons; frequent glove sanitizer dips during slicing.

### 5.5 Temperature control
- RTE room ambient air ≤10 °C (50 °F) where practical; product ≤4 °C (40 °F); storage and shipping coolers ≤4 °C, ideally ≤2 °C.
- Monitor cold-chain temperatures with alarms.

### 5.6 Supplier and ingredient controls
- Approved suppliers, COAs for spices and antimicrobials, and allergen statements.
- Supplier letters of guarantee for packaging materials.

## 6. Environmental monitoring program (EMP): your early warning system

The EMP is how you prove your post-lethality controls work. Design it on the **zone** concept:

| Zone | Examples | What to test for |
|---|---|---|
| **Zone 1** (food-contact surfaces, post-lethality) | Slicer blades, slicer infeed, conveyor belts, packaging film-forming area, gloves, tables, peeler/stripping tables | *Listeria* spp. or *Listeria*-like organisms |
| **Zone 2** (non-contact, close to product) | Slicer frames, motor housings, control panels, undersides of conveyors | *Listeria* spp. |
| **Zone 3** (non-contact, in the RTE room) | Floors, drains, walls, cart wheels, forklifts, floor scrubbers | *Listeria* spp. |
| **Zone 4** (outside RTE room) | Hallways, locker rooms, raw areas, dock | *Listeria* spp. |

**Key practices:**
- **Test for *Listeria* spp. (genus), not only *L. monocytogenes*.** A *Listeria* spp. positive is your signal that conditions allow Listeria to survive. Treat it as you would *L. monocytogenes* on a food-contact surface.
- Swab **during production** (≥3 h into the shift) as well as pre-op. Pre-op swabs only tell you sanitation looks good; mid-shift swabs find harbourage sites that seed the line.
- Use large areas (e.g., sponges over ~1 ft² / 30×30 cm where possible) and rotate sites. Bias sampling toward hard-to-clean spots.
- **Frequency:** at least as often as FSIS guidance recommends for your Alternative. These minimums rise from roughly semi-annual Zone 1 testing per line under Alt 1 to several times per month under Alt 3. A small plant would be wise to swab Zone 1 at least weekly and Zones 2–3 weekly too, regardless of Alternative.
- **Positive-response plan (written in advance):**
  1. **First Zone 1 positive:** clean and sanitize intensively, investigate the cause, re-swab the site and surrounding sites (vector swabbing). Consider holding the product made on that line since the last negative.
  2. **Second consecutive Zone 1 positive:** hold and test affected lots (lot-based product testing for *L. monocytogenes*), carry out a root-cause investigation, possibly a full equipment teardown, and review the design. Under Alt 3 this hold-and-test is required.
  3. **Zone 2/3 positives:** investigate and intensify cleaning, re-swab until 3 consecutive negatives, and look for traffic patterns that could carry contamination to Zone 1.
- **Trend the data:** map positives on a floor plan. Repeated positives at the same site point to a harbourage niche. Consider whole-genome sequencing or subtyping of isolates to tell persistent strains from transient ones.

### Product testing
- Finished product testing for *L. monocytogenes* (e.g., 25 g composites) for verification and after Zone 1 positives.
- **Only release product tested under hold-and-test once results are negative.** Shipping product before test results arrive is a common cause of recalls.

## 7. Shelf life and product-design decisions

- **Uncured/"natural" turkey supports Listeria growth more readily than cured turkey.** A growth inhibitor becomes much more important.
- Set the shelf life from a **challenge study** or a validated predictive model (e.g., the Listeria control model used by FSIS and industry). Do it for your actual formulation, pH, a_w/salt and storage temperature, including a realistic abuse temperature (e.g., 7 °C).
- Consider shorter shelf life for sliced product versus whole logs. Consider whether retail customers re-slice (whole logs/chubs) or you sell pre-sliced: re-slicing at retail is a separate and well-known risk.
- Labelling: "Keep refrigerated", use-by date, and consider "Use within X days of opening".

---

## 8. Verification, validation and records

- **Validation:** keep scientific support for each CCP and each Listeria intervention: Appendix A/B or newer FSIS guidance, published studies, supplier validation data for antimicrobials/HPP, and in-plant challenge studies.
- **Verification:** daily records review by a trained person before shipment; thermometer and metal-detector calibration; HPP cycle records; periodic product and environmental testing; annual HACCP reassessment, plus reassessment after any change in product, process, equipment or layout, or after any Listeria positive trend.
- **Records:** cook and chill logs, PLT logs, inhibitor batch records, EMP results and corrective actions, sanitation records, training, pest control and maintenance logs.
- **Recall readiness:** lot coding tied to production date and line, a written recall plan, and annual mock recall exercises.

---

## 9. Quick summary: the core controls

1. **Cook** to a validated *Salmonella*/Listeria lethality (CCP).
2. **Cool** fast enough to stop spore-former outgrowth (CCP).
3. **Prevent recontamination** after cooking through hygienic zoning, equipment design, sanitation, employee practices and positive air pressure (prerequisite programs and SSOPs).
4. **Inhibit growth** with a validated antimicrobial in the formulation and tight cold-chain control.
5. **Kill post-package contamination** with HPP or post-package pasteurization where feasible (CCP).
6. **Detect early** with an aggressive EMP for *Listeria* spp. (Zones 1–3), a written positive-response plan, and hold-and-test.
7. **Control physical and allergen hazards** with metal detection (CCP) and label verification.
8. **Validate, verify, reassess** the plan and keep the records that prove it.

> **Note:** confirm current requirements with your regulator (USDA-FSIS inspector-in-charge, CFIA, or your national authority). The FSIS *Listeria* Compliance Guideline and the revised Appendix A/B guidance are the key US references. Consider having the plan reviewed by a HACCP-certified food safety professional or process authority before implementation.
