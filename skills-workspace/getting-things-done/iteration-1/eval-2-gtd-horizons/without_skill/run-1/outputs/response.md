# GTD: The Horizons of Focus and Allen's Models for Choosing What to Do

David Allen's *Getting Things Done* (GTD) works at two levels. One is a bottom-up system for capturing and clarifying everything that has your attention. The other is a way to judge, moment by moment, whether you're doing the right thing. The **Horizons of Focus** give you the long view. The **three models for choosing actions** cover the moment of decision.

---

## Part 1: The Six Horizons of Focus

Allen first described these as altitudes, using an airplane metaphor: "Runway" up to "50,000+ feet." Each horizon is a different level of commitment. Your priorities really come from the higher levels, but Allen recommends getting the lower levels under control first. You can't think clearly about your life's purpose while unprocessed stuff is shouting at you.

| Horizon | Old altitude name | What it covers | Typical time frame | How often to review |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ground** | Runway | **Calendar and next actions**: every concrete, physical, visible action you need to take (calls, emails, errands) | Now / today | Daily, constantly |
| **Horizon 1** | 10,000 ft | **Projects**: any outcome you've committed to that takes more than one action step and can be finished within about a year | Weeks to months | Weekly Review |
| **Horizon 2** | 20,000 ft | **Areas of focus and accountability**: the roles you play and the standards you keep (health, finances, family, career, a job function like "staff development") | Ongoing, no end date | Monthly or so |
| **Horizon 3** | 30,000 ft | **Goals and objectives**: what you want to achieve in the next 1-2 years | 1-2 years | Quarterly |
| **Horizon 4** | 40,000 ft | **Vision**: what long-term success looks like, the ideal picture of your work and life | 3-5 years | Yearly |
| **Horizon 5** | 50,000+ ft | **Purpose and principles**: why you exist or why the organization exists, and the core values that guide behavior | Lifetime | Yearly or as needed |

### Key ideas about the horizons

- **Each level drives the one below it.** Purpose shapes vision. Vision suggests goals. Goals and areas of responsibility create projects. Projects create next actions. When an action is hard to choose or hard to make yourself do, it often means a higher horizon isn't clear.
- **Each level checks the one above it.** Areas of focus work as a checklist for spotting missing projects. For example, "Health" with no active project in it may mean something is being neglected.
- **Work bottom-up to get control, top-down to get perspective.** Allen admits he is pragmatic here. Most people get the most immediate relief by gaining control of the runway and projects first. After that they are free to think about the higher horizons.
- **Everything can be reviewed.** The **Weekly Review** keeps Ground and Horizon 1 current. The higher horizons need less frequent but deliberate reviews, such as offsite thinking, annual planning, or a long walk with a notepad.
- **Control and perspective.** The later book *Making It All Work* frames this as a self-management matrix. Control comes from the five-stage workflow: capture, clarify, organize, reflect, engage. Perspective comes from the horizons. You need both. Control without perspective makes you a "micromanager" of the wrong things. Perspective without control makes you a "visionary" who is drowning.

## Part 2: Allen's Three Models for Choosing What to Work On

GTD deliberately avoids assigning fixed priority codes like A/B/C to actions. Allen argues that priorities change constantly, and that you should trust your intuition at the moment of choice. That only works if your system is complete and current. To support that intuition, he offers three complementary models.

### Model 1: The Four-Criteria Model for choosing actions in the moment

When you look at your next-action lists, filter your options in this order:

1. **Context.** What can you do where you are, with the tools you have? Without a phone you can't make @Calls. Away from the office you can't do @Office tasks. This is why GTD organizes action lists by context.
2. **Time available.** How much time do you have before your next commitment? A 10-minute gap calls for a quick action, not a two-hour project.
3. **Energy available.** How much mental and physical energy do you have? Late in the day, low-energy tasks like filing, simple calls, or reading may be the best fit.
4. **Priority.** Of the actions that fit your context, time, and energy, which one has the highest payoff? This is where the Horizons of Focus come in. Priority is judged against your projects, areas, goals, vision, and purpose.

The order matters. Each criterion narrows the field for the next one, so priority is the final judgment on a short list that is actually possible.

### Model 2: The Threefold Model for evaluating daily work

During the day, any work you do falls into one of three activities:

1. **Doing predefined work.** You work from your next-action lists and calendar: things you already captured, clarified, and decided to do.
2. **Doing work as it shows up.** You handle unplanned demands as they arrive, such as a colleague stopping by, an urgent call, or a sudden problem. You decide it matters more than your predefined work and do it right away.
3. **Defining your work.** You process inboxes, clarify meeting notes, and break new projects into next actions. This is what makes predefined work possible.

Allen's point is that all three are legitimate. Choosing to handle surprise work is fine, as long as it's a **conscious trade-off** against your lists. Trouble starts when people spend their whole day in mode 2 and never make time for mode 3. Their lists then go stale and stop being trustworthy. Allen describes many professionals as living in "latest and loudest" mode for this reason.

### Model 3: The Six-Level Model for reviewing your own work

This is the Horizons of Focus used as a prioritization tool. When the first two models leave you unsure what matters most, you look at your work from successively higher altitudes. Real priority comes from the top down: an action is important because of the project it moves forward, and the project matters because of the area, goal, vision, and purpose behind it.

Allen describes this model in the six-level airplane terms (Runway, 10,000 ft, 20,000 ft, 30,000 ft, 40,000 ft, 50,000+ ft). Those are the same horizons covered in Part 1.

## How the pieces fit together

- **The Four-Criteria Model** answers: *"What should I do right now?"* It filters by context, then time, then energy, then priority.
- **The Threefold Model** answers: *"How should I balance planned work, surprises, and processing time?"*
- **The Six-Level Model / Horizons of Focus** answers: *"What actually matters, and why?"* It supplies the "priority" judgment the other two models depend on.

Underneath all three is a basic GTD rule: **you can only trust your intuitive choices when your system is complete and current.** That's why the capture habit, regular clarifying, and the Weekly Review aren't optional. Without them, none of the decision models has good information to work with.
