# GTD: The Horizons of Focus and Allen's Models for Choosing What to Do

*Based on David Allen, **Getting Things Done**, revised edition (Penguin, 2015). Items marked **(book)** were checked against the text. Items marked **(general)** are common GTD explanation, not checked line by line.*

## The short version

Allen does not give you a formula for "what's most important." He gives you **three models** that work at different levels:

1. **The four-criteria model**: how to choose an action *right now*.
2. **The threefold model**: how to think about the kinds of work that fill your day.
3. **The six-level model**: how to review your work from top to bottom. These six levels are the **horizons of focus**. **(book)**

The horizons tell you *why* and *where*. The four criteria tell you *what's next, this minute*.

---

## 1. The horizons of focus (the six-level model for reviewing your work) **(book)**

Allen calls this the six-level model. Think of it as six heights from which you can look at your life. Each level gives meaning and priority to the levels below it.

| Horizon | Name | The question it answers | Typical review frequency **(general)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Horizon 5** | Purpose and principles | Why do I (or my organization) exist? What do I stand for? | Yearly, or when your life changes |
| **Horizon 4** | Vision | What would wild success look like in about 3–5 years? | Yearly |
| **Horizon 3** | Goals | What do I want to achieve in the next 1–2 years? | Quarterly |
| **Horizon 2** | Areas of focus and accountabilities | Which roles and standards do I have to keep up? | Monthly, or every few months |
| **Horizon 1** | Current projects | What outcomes am I committed to finishing? | Weekly |
| **Ground** | Current actions | What physical, visible next actions are on my lists? | Daily |

### What each level holds

- **Ground: Current actions.** Every next action on your lists: calls, emails, errands, things to do at the computer. This level holds the most items. For most people it is what overwhelms them. **(book: level name)**
- **Horizon 1: Current projects.** Allen defines a project as *"any desired result that can be accomplished within a year that requires more than one action step"* **(book)**. Your Projects list sits here. Every project should have at least one next action at Ground level.
- **Horizon 2: Areas of focus and accountabilities.** These are ongoing, not things you finish: health, family, finances, home, career growth, and the parts of your job you are responsible for. Projects grow out of these areas. A review here shows you an area you are neglecting. **(general detail)**
- **Horizon 3: Goals.** These are results you want in the next year or two. They often create new projects or change which areas matter most. **(general detail)**
- **Horizon 4: Vision.** This is a longer-term picture of success, a few years out. **(general detail)**
- **Horizon 5: Purpose and principles.** This is the deepest "why" and the values that set the limits on everything else. It is the final test for any commitment. **(general detail)**

### How to use the horizons

- **Priorities come from the top down.** Your purpose shapes your vision. Your vision shapes your goals. Your goals and areas of responsibility create projects. Projects create next actions. To know whether an action matters, trace it up the levels.
- **Control comes from the bottom up.** Allen's practical advice is to get the Ground and Horizon 1 under control first. Capture everything, clarify it, and keep a full Projects list. When your head is full of open loops, thinking about vision is hard. **(general)**
- **Each level needs its own review.** The **Weekly Review** covers Ground and Horizon 1. It reviews action lists, your calendar, Waiting For, and your projects "and larger outcome" lists **(book)**. The higher horizons need less frequent and more reflective reviews.
- **The horizons supply the direction GTD lacks.** GTD is a workflow method, not a way to rank priorities. You pair it with the horizons to set direction. **(book)**

## 2. The four-criteria model for choosing actions in the moment **(book)**

When you look at your Next Actions lists and ask "what should I do now?", Allen says to apply four criteria **in this order**:

1. **Context.** What can you do where you are, with the tools you have? You cannot make a call without a phone or run an errand from your desk. This is why next actions are sorted into context lists such as @calls, @computer, @errands and @home. **(context examples: general)**
2. **Time available.** How long do you have before your next commitment? Ten minutes before a meeting means choosing a ten-minute action.
3. **Energy available.** How much mental and physical energy do you have? Late in the day you might do routine tasks rather than creative ones.
4. **Priority.** Of the actions that pass the first three filters, which has the most value? This is where the horizons come in. The more clearly you know your projects, areas, goals and purpose, the easier this choice is.

**Why the order matters:** each filter narrows the choices for the next one. Context and time remove most options at once. Priority is judged last, among actions you can actually do now. **(general explanation of the book's ordering)**

## 3. The threefold model for describing daily work **(book)**

At any moment during your workday you are doing one of three kinds of activity:

1. **Doing predefined work.** You work from your lists: next actions you already defined, and calendar items.
2. **Doing work as it shows up.** You deal with interruptions and surprises as they come: a colleague stops by, an urgent email arrives, a problem comes up. You decide on the spot that this matters more than your list.
3. **Defining your work.** You process your in-tray, email and notes. You clarify what each item means and decide its next action. (This is the Clarify and Organize steps.)

**What this model teaches** **(general)**:
- Doing work as it shows up is often right. But it is a trade-off you make on purpose. It is only safe when you know what you are *not* doing, which a current list tells you.
- If you never spend time **defining** your work, your lists become stale. You then live in "work as it shows up" mode by default, which leads to fighting fires.
- A good balance is to set aside regular time to define work, so you can trust your predefined work the rest of the time.

## How the three models fit together

| Model | Time scale | Main use |
|---|---|---|
| Four criteria | This moment | Choose the next action to do now |
| Threefold | Your day | Balance planned work, surprises, and processing |
| Six levels (horizons) | Day → lifetime | Review your commitments so priority has meaning |

A practical rhythm **(general, drawing on the book's practices)**:
- **Many times a day:** check your context lists and use context → time → energy → priority.
- **Every day:** set aside time to *define* work. Get your inboxes to zero so your lists stay complete.
- **Weekly:** do the Weekly Review (get clear, get current, get creative) **(book)** to keep Ground and Horizon 1 current.
- **Monthly or quarterly:** review Horizon 2 (areas) and Horizon 3 (goals). Ask whether your projects match them.
- **Yearly:** review Horizons 4 and 5 (vision and purpose), and let any change flow down into goals, projects and actions.

## One caveat

Allen's horizons give a structure, not answers. Your own purpose, vision and goals are what fill each level. GTD stays tool-agnostic. Paper, an app or a mix of both works, as long as you trust it and review it. **(book)**
