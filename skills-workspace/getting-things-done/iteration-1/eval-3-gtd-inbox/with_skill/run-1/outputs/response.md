# Processing Everything the GTD Way

This walkthrough follows David Allen's *Getting Things Done* (revised edition, 2015). The idea behind it is simple. **Your mind is for having ideas, not holding them.** The overwhelm doesn't come from how much you have to do. It comes from "open loops": commitments you haven't captured, decided on, or parked somewhere you trust. We'll get every loop out of your head, decide what each one means, and put it in the right place. The goal is what Allen calls **"mind like water"**: you respond to each thing in proportion to what it is, without over- or under-reacting.

You'll follow the five steps of mastering workflow: **Capture → Clarify → Organize → Reflect → Engage.**

Set aside a real block of time for the first pass, half a day or more if the backlog is big *(practical estimate, not from the book)*. You only do this full reset once. After that, short daily processing and the Weekly Review keep things clear.

---

## Step 0: Set up your buckets (10 minutes)

Before you start, have these ready in whatever tool you trust (paper, a notes app, a task app; GTD works with any of them):

- **An in-tray / inbox**: one physical tray for paper, plus your email inbox and one "capture" list for notes
- **Projects** list
- **Next Actions** lists, split by context, e.g. `@calls`, `@computer`, `@errands`, `@home`, `@office`
- **Waiting For** list
- **Calendar**
- **Someday/Maybe** list
- **Reference** filing (a simple A–Z paper file and/or a digital folder)
- **Project support** material (folders for the documents that go with each project)
- A **trash can** and a recycling bin, nearby. You'll use them a lot.

Those are Allen's **seven primary list types**, plus the in-tray and the trash. Keep **hard edges** between them. A Next Action list holds only next actions, not reference notes or someday ideas.

---

## Step 1: CAPTURE: get it all out of your head and into "in"

### 1a. Gather the physical stuff
Go around your desk, bag, wallet, car and home. Put **anything that isn't where it belongs, or that has your attention**, into the in-tray: notes, receipts, mail, business cards, things to fix, sticky notes. If something is too big to move, write a note about it ("fix garage shelf") and put the note in.

### 1b. Do a mind sweep
Now empty your head. Write **one item per line or note**. Don't judge, sort or prioritize yet. Just get it all down. Prompts to work through:

- **Work:** projects started or not started, commitments to your boss, team or clients, calls and emails to return, meetings to prepare for, reports, budgets, hiring, admin, things you're waiting on
- **Personal:** home repairs, errands, family, birthdays and events, health appointments, finances and bills, taxes, car, travel, hobbies, things to buy, "I should really..." ideas
- **Vague worries:** write them down too ("think about career move", "worried about Mom's health")

Keep going until your mind goes quiet. It's normal to end up with 100+ items.

### 1c. Corral your digital inboxes
Your email inbox, voicemail, text threads, Slack saves, downloads folder and notes apps are all "in" too. If the email backlog is huge, a common move is to put everything older than a couple of weeks into a "Backlog" folder and process it later *(common practitioner tactic, not from the book)*. Then work the recent inbox down to zero.

## Step 2: CLARIFY: process each item, one at a time, top down

This is the core of the method. Pick up **the top item**. Don't skim and don't choose the fun ones. Run it through these questions:

### Question 1: "What is it?"
Name it plainly. "Letter from insurance company about policy renewal." Not just "insurance stuff."

### Question 2: "Is it actionable?"

**NO, it's not actionable.** It goes one of three ways:
- **Trash**: you don't need it. Toss it without guilt.
- **Incubate**: maybe later. Put it on **Someday/Maybe** ("learn Spanish", "kitchen remodel") or, if it matters on a specific future date, in the **tickler** or on the calendar ("revisit this in March").
- **Reference**: useful information you may need, with no action. **File it** (A–Z or digital folders) so you can find it in under a minute.

**YES, it's actionable.** Ask two more things:

1. **What's the desired outcome?** If getting there takes **more than one action step**, it's a **project**. Allen defines a project as *"any desired result that can be accomplished within a year that requires more than one action step."* Write it on the **Projects** list as an outcome: "Car insurance renewed at a good rate," "Q1 offsite planned."
2. **What's the next action?** The very next **physical, visible** thing you'd do to move it forward. Not "insurance" but "Call agent Dana to ask about renewal options." Not "mom's birthday" but "Browse online for gift ideas for Mom."

### Then decide: Do it, Delegate it, or Defer it

- **The Two-Minute Rule: DO IT.** *"If an action will take less than two minutes, it should be done at the moment it's defined."* Reply to the quick email, sign the form, put the date on the calendar. Doing it now takes less time than tracking it.
- **DELEGATE IT** if someone else should do it. Send it to them, then log it on **Waiting For** with the date: "Waiting For: Sam, budget numbers (sent 11/21)."
- **DEFER IT** if it takes more than two minutes and it's yours:
  - **Calendar** if it must happen **on a specific day or time** ("Dentist Tue 3pm", "Submit grant by Fri"). The calendar is only for things that are truly tied to a date. Don't put "would be nice today" items there.
  - Otherwise, the right **Next Actions** context list: `@calls: Call Dana re: insurance renewal`.

### Processing rules that keep you moving
- **One item at a time, top down.** Never put something back into "in." Once you pick it up, decide.
- **Handle each item once.** If you're tempted to set it aside "to think about," the decision is the action. That might be "Draft pros/cons for job offer" on `@computer`.
- **Emergencies are the exception, not the rule.** If you find something that's on fire, deal with it, then keep going.
- **Email works the same way.** Open, decide, act or move, archive. Get the inbox to empty.

### A quick worked batch

| Item in "in" | Actionable? | Result |

| Article on retirement accounts | No | **Reference** (file under "Finance"), or Someday/Maybe if you want to read it |

## Step 3: ORGANIZE: put results where they belong

By the end of clarifying, each item should be in exactly one place:

1. **Projects list**: every outcome that needs more than one step. Each project needs **at least one next action** on a context list.
2. **Project support material**: plans, notes and files for each project, kept out of the action lists.
3. **Calendar**: only date- or time-specific actions, plus day-specific information.
4. **Next Actions**, grouped by context (`@calls`, `@computer`, `@errands`, `@home`, `@office`, `@agenda-[person]`)
5. **Waiting For**: what you've delegated or are expecting, with dates
6. **Reference**: filed and easy to find
7. **Someday/Maybe**: ideas you aren't committing to yet

For date-based reminders, a physical **tickler file** uses **43 folders**: 31 daily folders and 12 monthly folders. You drop items into the day or month you want them to come back. A digital tool with dated reminders does the same job.

**Check before moving on:** your in-tray, email inbox and capture list are **empty**. Every project has a next action. Nothing important is left only in your head.

---

## Step 4: REFLECT: keep the system trustworthy

The system only works if you **trust** it, and you trust it only if you review it. Look at your calendar and Next Actions lists daily. Then lock in the **Weekly Review**: the same time each week, about an hour or two *(timing is a practical estimate)*. Friday afternoon works well for many people.

**Get clear**
- Collect loose papers and materials
- Get "in" to zero (process everything with the Step 2 questions)
- Empty your head (a short mind sweep)

**Get current**
- Review your Next Actions lists (cross off what's done, add what's missing)
- Review the past calendar (anything left over?)
- Review the upcoming calendar (what do you need to prepare?)
- Review Waiting For (who needs a nudge?)
- Review your Projects list (does each one have a next action?), plus your larger outcomes
- Review any relevant checklists

**Get creative**
- Review Someday/Maybe (move anything that's now active onto Projects)
- Be creative and courageous: add any new ideas or projects

---

## Step 5: ENGAGE: choose what to do with confidence

When it's time to work, use the **four-criteria model** in this order:
1. **Context**: what can you do where you are, with the tools you have? (At your desk, look at `@computer`.)
2. **Time available**: 15 minutes before a meeting means short actions.
3. **Energy available**: tired? Do easy calls or filing.
4. **Priority**: of what's left, what matters most?

Over the course of a day you'll switch between three kinds of work (the **threefold model**): **doing work you defined ahead of time** (your lists), **doing work as it shows up** (interruptions), and **defining your work** (processing "in"). All three are valid. The point is to choose on purpose.

For direction about *what* matters, review from the top now and then, using the **horizons of focus**: purpose and principles (H5) → vision (H4) → goals (H3) → areas of focus (H2) → current projects (H1) → current actions (ground). GTD is a workflow method, not a prioritization formula. The horizons give your lists direction.

---

## If you're overwhelmed right now, start here
1. **Capture first.** Do the mind sweep tonight, even if you process nothing. Just getting it out of your head brings relief.
2. **Use the two-minute rule hard** on your first processing pass. Clearing the small stuff builds momentum fast.
3. **Turn every big worry into one next action.** "Sort out finances" becomes "@computer: download last 3 bank statements." The physical next step is what removes the stress.
4. **Get to inbox zero once,** then put a recurring Weekly Review on your calendar so it stays that way.

You don't need to do it perfectly. You need **one** trusted place for everything and the habit of reviewing it.
