# Processing Everything the GTD Way

When your inbox and your head are both overflowing, the fix in David Allen's *Getting Things Done* is the same either way. Get everything out of your head and into one trusted place. Then decide, one item at a time, what each thing means and what you'll do about it. Below is a step-by-step session. Set aside 2 to 4 hours for the first full pass. Later passes go much faster.

---

## Before you start: set up the minimum

You need:

- **One physical in-tray**, or a box or a spot on your desk, for paper.
- **One digital capture list**, such as a note or a task app's inbox.
- **A place for these lists** (paper, app, anything works):
  - Next Actions (sorted by context)
  - Projects
  - Waiting For
  - Someday/Maybe
  - Calendar
- **Reference storage**: a simple A–Z filing system and/or a digital folder or notes system.
- **A trash can or recycling bin** within reach.

Don't over-engineer the tools. You can refine them later.

---

## Step 1: Capture (empty your head and gather everything)

### 1a. Physical sweep
Walk around your desk, office, bag, car and home. Put anything that holds an "open loop" into the in-tray: notes, bills, receipts, business cards, things to fix, papers to read. If something won't fit, write a note that stands in for it ("Broken lamp in hall").

### 1b. Mind sweep
Take a pad of paper and write down **everything** on your mind. Put one item per line, or one per sheet if you're using paper. Don't judge or organize yet. Just keep writing until your mind runs dry. Use these prompts to jog your memory:

- **Work:** projects started or not started, commitments to your boss, team or clients, calls to return, emails to send, meetings to prepare for, reports, budgets, hiring, performance reviews, pending decisions, things you're waiting on, professional development.
- **Personal:** family commitments, birthdays and gifts, home repairs, errands, finances (bills, taxes, insurance), health appointments, car, travel, hobbies, things to buy, things to learn, friends to contact, community or volunteer commitments.
- **Vague stuff:** "I should really…", "Someday I want to…", lingering worries.

Expect 50–150+ items. That's normal, and getting them out of your head is a relief in itself.

### 1c. Digital sweep
Note every digital inbox you have: email accounts, Slack/Teams DMs, voicemail, text messages, notes apps, downloads folder, desktop files, browser tabs. These all count as inboxes. You'll process each one the same way.

**Goal:** every open loop now sits in a small number of collection buckets and none is left in your head.

---

## Step 2: Clarify (process each item)

This is where the real work happens. Take **one item at a time, from the top**. Rules:

- Handle one item at a time.
- Never put anything **back** into the inbox.
- Don't skip the hard ones or cherry-pick the easy ones.

For each item, ask these questions in order:

### Q1. "What is it?"
Be clear about what it actually is. Is it a bill, a request, an idea, or a reminder?

### Q2. "Is it actionable?"

**If NO**, it goes to one of three places:
1. **Trash.** You don't need it.
2. **Someday/Maybe.** You might want to do it later, but not now ("Learn Italian," "Kitchen remodel"). You can also use a tickler file or calendar reminder to bring it back at a specific time.
3. **Reference.** It's useful information but needs no action (a manual, meeting notes, a contract). File it where you can find it.

**If YES**, continue:

### Q3. "What's the desired outcome?"
If getting there takes **more than one action**, it's a **Project**. Write it on your Projects list as an outcome ("Q1 budget finalized," "New car purchased").

### Q4. "What's the very next physical, visible action?"
This is the core of GTD. Make it concrete, something you could actually go do:
- Vague: "Mom's birthday"
- Concrete: "Call Sarah to ask what Mom wants for her birthday"
- Vague: "Taxes"
- Concrete: "Gather 2026 1099 forms from email into Taxes folder"

Once you know the next action, do one of three things with it:

| Option | When | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Do it** | It takes **less than 2 minutes** | Do it right now. Tracking it would take longer than doing it. |
| **Delegate it** | Someone else is the right person to do it | Hand it off, then add it to your **Waiting For** list with the person's name and the date. |
| **Defer it** | It's yours and takes more than 2 minutes | Put it on your **Calendar** if it must happen on a specific day or time. Otherwise put it on your **Next Actions** list. |

### The whole flow in one view

```
Item → What is it?
        │
        ├─ Not actionable → Trash / Someday-Maybe / Reference
        │
        └─ Actionable → Multi-step? → add to Projects list
                       │
                       └─ Next action?
                            ├─ < 2 min  → DO IT NOW
                            ├─ Not mine → DELEGATE → Waiting For
                            └─ Mine, > 2 min → DEFER → Calendar or Next Actions
```

---

## Step 3: Organize (put things where they belong)

Each list holds one kind of thing:

- **Projects list:** every outcome that takes more than one step to finish. Each active project should have at least one next action on a Next Actions list.
- **Next Actions, grouped by context**, so you can see what's possible where you are:
  - `@Computer`
  - `@Calls`
  - `@Errands`
  - `@Office` / `@Home`
  - `@Agendas`: one list per person or meeting, for things to discuss
  - Use whatever contexts fit your life. Some people sort by energy or time available instead.
- **Waiting For:** delegated items and things you're expecting from others, with dates.
- **Calendar:** only the "hard landscape": appointments, things due on a specific day, and time-specific actions. Don't use it as a wish list.
- **Someday/Maybe:** ideas and possibilities you aren't committed to yet.
- **Reference:** a simple A–Z file, digital or physical. Keep it easy to add to, or you'll stop using it.
- **Project support material:** notes or files that belong to a specific project, kept separate from your action lists.

---

## Processing email specifically

1. **Turn off notifications** for the processing session.
2. Start at the top, or the oldest message, and apply the same questions to each email:
   - Delete or archive it if it needs no action.
   - Reply right away if the reply takes less than 2 minutes.
   - Forward it if someone else should handle it. Track it in Waiting For, for example with a "@Waiting" folder or label.
   - If you need to act and it takes longer, move it to an "@Action" folder **and** add the next action to your list (or rely on the folder as the list itself).
   - Move reference emails into an archive or reference folder. Search usually beats complex folder trees.
3. **Huge backlog?** Declare "email bankruptcy" with care. Move everything older than about 2 weeks into a folder called "Old Inbox – to review." Process the recent mail properly, then skim the old folder quickly for anything critical.
4. Aim for **inbox zero**: an empty inbox, because you've decided what every message means, not because you've done everything in it.

---

## Step 4: Reflect (keep the system trustworthy)

Your system only works if you trust it. That trust comes from reviewing it.

- **Daily:** check your calendar first, then your Next Actions lists. Capture new inputs as they come in.
- **Weekly Review**, the step that matters most for keeping the system alive. Block 1–2 hours, for example on Friday afternoon:
  1. **Get clear:** gather loose papers, process every inbox to zero, and do a new mind sweep.
  2. **Get current:** review Next Actions and cross off what's done. Look at the past two weeks of your calendar for leftovers and the next few weeks for things to prepare. Review Waiting For and send follow-ups. Review the Projects list and make sure each project has a next action.
  3. **Get creative:** scan Someday/Maybe, promote items to active or delete them, and capture new ideas.

---

## Step 5: Engage (decide what to do right now)

When it's time to work, choose from your Next Actions lists using four filters, in this order:

1. **Context:** what can you do where you are, with the tools you have?
2. **Time available:** how long until your next commitment?
3. **Energy:** how much focus do you have right now?
4. **Priority:** of what's left, what matters most given your goals?

You aren't relying on memory any more. You're choosing from a complete menu.

---

## Practical tips for your first big session

- **Do it in one go if possible.** Momentum matters, and a half-processed system feels worse than none.
- **The 2-minute rule is not a time limit on the whole session.** If you have dozens of quick items, doing them all can eat your time. If you're in a rush, defer some.
- **When you're stuck on an item,** ask "What's the next action?" again. Being stuck usually means the item is a project with no defined next step, or you haven't decided whether you're committed to it at all.
- **It's fine to renegotiate commitments.** Moving something to Someday/Maybe or telling someone "I can't do this" are legitimate outcomes.
- **Expect the mental relief right away.** The anxiety mostly comes from open loops held in memory, not from the amount of work.
- **Keep the tools simple.** Paper lists or one basic app beat a complicated setup you'll abandon.

---

## Quick-start checklist

- [ ] Set up an in-tray and the lists: Projects, Next Actions (by context), Waiting For, Someday/Maybe, Calendar, Reference
- [ ] Physical sweep: everything goes into the in-tray
- [ ] Mind sweep: write down every open loop
- [ ] List every digital inbox
- [ ] Process top to bottom: trash, someday, reference, do (<2 min), delegate, or defer
- [ ] Define a next action for every project
- [ ] Get email to zero, or move the backlog into an "Old Inbox" folder
- [ ] Schedule a recurring Weekly Review
- [ ] Work from your context lists from now on

Start with the mind sweep right now. Grab a pad of paper and give it 20 minutes. Then come back and process the list one item at a time.
