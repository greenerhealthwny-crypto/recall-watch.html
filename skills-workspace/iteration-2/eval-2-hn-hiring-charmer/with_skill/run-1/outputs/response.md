# Before You Make the Offer: Checking a Charming Candidate

Your instinct to slow down is right. Robert Greene's *The Laws of Human Nature* has a rule that fits this case: **"Character is destiny."** People repeat their patterns, and an interview is the setting where it is easiest to hide them. Charm, confidence and good stories are what Greene calls the *front*. Character shows in specifics, in patterns, and in how someone talks about the people they used to work for.

None of this proves the candidate is a bad hire. It means you don't have enough evidence yet. Here's how to get it.

---

## 1. What the three signals may mean

| What you saw | Why it matters (Greene's lens) | Innocent explanation to rule out |
|---|---|---|
| **Big claims, vague details** | Greene's toxic type **"the Big Talker"** has exciting ideas and big stories but doesn't follow through, and blames others when things fall apart. Greene also notes that real events are messy and full of detail. Polished, general stories with nothing underneath are a warning sign. | Nerves, or a team project where their own role was smaller than they made it sound. Some people overclaim in interviews and are honest when pushed. |
| **Badmouthed two former bosses** | Greene names "how they talk about past partners and bosses" as a key sign of character. One bad boss is common. Two, raised **in an interview** (where most people are on their best behavior), points to a pattern. It fits the **"Relentless Rebel"**, who can't tolerate anyone above them (and eventually that includes you), or the **"Personalizer"**, who builds up grievances. | They really did have two bad bosses. Health nonprofits do have some. What matters is *how* they told it: did they take any responsibility, or were they purely the victim? |
| **The whole panel loved them** | This is less about the candidate and more about your process. Greene calls it **appearance bias** (we take charm at face value) plus **the group effect**: in a group, enthusiasm spreads and people get more certain. Charismatic people are what Greene calls "inflaming individuals" because they move everyone in the room at once. | Maybe they really are excellent. But "everyone loved them" is a feeling, not evidence. |

Greene's key point: **what looks "out of character" is often the true character.** The charm is what they meant to show you. The vagueness and the badmouthing slipped out, so they're more informative.

---

## 2. What to do before making an offer

### A. Hold a second, focused interview on specifics
Pick the 2–3 biggest projects they described and go into them in depth:
- "Walk me through that project from the start. What was the budget, who funded it, and what were the deliverables?"
- "What did *you* personally do, and what did other people on the team do?"
- "What numbers did you report to the funder? How were they collected?"
- "What went wrong, and what would you do differently?"
- "Who else could I talk to who saw this work up close?"

**What to watch for:** a strong candidate gets *more* concrete as you dig, and the detail is messy and real (names, setbacks, numbers). A Big Talker goes back to vision language, changes the subject, gets irritated, or blames others for the gaps. Notice how they react to being pressed. Greene treats response to scrutiny and criticism as one of the most revealing tests.

Also ask, neutrally: *"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a supervisor. How did it go?"* Then listen for whether they can describe the boss's side fairly and admit anything they did wrong.

### B. Give them a work sample
A program coordinator's job is follow-through, not presenting. Give a short, realistic exercise, such as:
- Draft a simple work plan and timeline for a small grant-funded program.
- Write a two-paragraph progress update for a funder from a set of messy data you provide.
- Prioritize a pile of competing tasks and explain the reasoning.

This tests what talk can't: organization, attention to detail and realism. Greene stresses watching **"conscientiousness in small details."** Give the same exercise to your other finalists so the comparison is fair.

### C. Check references properly, including supervisors
- Ask the candidate for references who were **direct supervisors**, including at least one at the jobs where they had the two bosses they criticized. Pay attention if they hesitate or offer only peers.
- Ask behavioral questions: "What did they personally own on [project]?" "How did they take feedback?" "How did they get along with leadership?" "Would you rehire them?" Pay attention to pauses and lukewarm or careful answers.
- Verify one or two specific claims: a program they say they led, a grant they say they managed, a result they cite.
- If you'll contact anyone the candidate didn't list, get their consent first and follow your nonprofit's HR policy or employment counsel. Rules differ by state.

### D. Have each panelist score independently, then discuss
Before the panel talks again, have each member **score the candidate alone, in writing**, against the job's actual requirements (project management, reporting accuracy, community and partner relations, teamwork), with a specific piece of evidence for each score. Greene's remedy for the group effect is to keep some mental distance from the room's mood. In practice: independent scores first, discussion second. If the scores fall apart once people have to cite evidence, that tells you something.

Also ask one person to argue the opposing case on purpose: "What's the strongest reason *not* to hire them?"

### E. If you hire them anyway, protect the organization
- Use a real **introductory/probationary period** with written goals for 30, 60 and 90 days.
- Set clear, measurable deliverables early, especially anything tied to funder reporting.
- Check in often at first. Greene says people show themselves most clearly **under stress and once they have some power**, so watch how they handle the first missed deadline or the first piece of critical feedback.

---

## 3. Check your own side

Greene says you need to look at your own biases too:
- **Confirmation bias** can go either way. If you've now decided they're a Big Talker, you might read every answer as proof. Go into the follow-up open to being wrong.
- **Scarcity pressure.** Small nonprofits are often stretched and want the seat filled. Hiring under pressure is when charm works best on us. A slow hire costs much less than a bad one in a small team where one coordinator affects funders, partners and community trust.

---

## 4. What you're actually looking for

The opposite of the Big Talker is what Greene calls **the superior character**: someone resilient, consistent and adaptable, who works well with others and **takes responsibility**. In a candidate that shows up as:
- Specific, slightly messy, verifiable stories.
- Credit shared with former teammates.
- Ownership of at least part of what went wrong.
- Fair, even generous, descriptions of difficult former bosses.
- Work-sample quality that matches the interview.

If this candidate shows those things under closer questioning, great: you have someone charming *and* solid. If the vagueness stays and the references are lukewarm, trust the pattern over the performance.

*A note on the source:* Greene's "types" are character archetypes from his synthesis of psychology and history, not clinical diagnoses or proven science. Use them to know what to check, not to label the candidate. And apply the same structured steps to every finalist so the process stays fair and defensible.
