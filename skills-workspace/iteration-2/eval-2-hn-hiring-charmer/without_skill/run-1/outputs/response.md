Your instincts are worth listening to. The panel liked them, but you noticed two of the most reliable warning signs in an interview: **claims that fall apart when you ask for specifics**, and **contempt for former bosses**. Charm shows that someone interviews well. It doesn't show they can do the job. In a small nonprofit, one program coordinator affects funders, partners, clients, and staff morale, so it pays to slow down and verify before you make an offer.

## What the signals may mean (and may not)

- **Vague when pressed.** This could mean they inflated their role ("I led" when they really helped). It could also be nerves, confidentiality rules, or someone who thinks in big pictures. You can't tell yet, so test it.
- **Badmouthing two former bosses.** One bad boss happens to everyone. Two, raised without being asked in an interview where people are on their best behavior, suggests a pattern: they may struggle with authority, avoid responsibility, or blame others. Notice too that they did it *to a potential future boss*. How they talk about past managers is a decent preview of how they'll talk about you.
- **The panel loved them.** Charismatic people are very good at winning a room. That's useful in a coordinator who works with community partners. But "everyone liked them" is a feeling. It isn't evidence about their work.

## What to do before an offer

### 1. Hold a follow-up interview focused on specifics
Choose one or two projects they claimed to have led and go deep:
- "Walk me through it from the start. What was the goal, the budget, the timeline?"
- "What did *you* personally do, compared with your team or your supervisor?"
- "What went wrong, and what did you do about it?"
- "How did you measure success? What were the actual numbers?" (people served, outcomes, grant deliverables)
- "Who could I talk to who saw your work on this?"

People who actually did the work get *more* concrete as you dig: names, dates, tools, mistakes, tradeoffs. People who are inflating get more general, change the subject, or lean on charm. Pay attention to "we" versus "I," and whether they can say what went badly.

### 2. Ask about the former bosses without leading them
- "Tell me about the best manager you've had. What made them good?"
- "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a supervisor. How did it end?"
- "If I called [former boss], what would they say are your strengths and where you need to grow?"

You want to see self-awareness and some sense of their own part in the conflict. If every story ends with them being right and the other person being incompetent, that tells you a lot.

### 3. Do real reference checks, and choose who you call
- Ask specifically for **former direct supervisors**, including at least one of the bosses they criticized if possible. If they refuse or keep steering you to peers and friends, that's informative.
- If you have a mutual connection at one of their past organizations, a backchannel conversation can help. Be discreet, and don't put their current job at risk.
- Ask questions references can answer honestly without having to say anything negative:
  - "What exactly was their role on [project they described]?"
  - "Would you hire them again? Why or why not?"
  - "How did they handle feedback or disagreement with you?"
  - "What kind of support or management did they need to do their best work?"
- Listen for faint praise, pauses, and answers like "they were very personable." A careful "I'd rather just confirm dates" can mean a lot.

### 4. Give them a work sample
Give a short, paid or time-limited exercise that matches the job, for example:
- Draft a simple work plan and timeline for a community health program you describe.
- Write a short program update for a funder from some sample data.
- Prioritize a messy week: a partner cancels, a grant report is due, a volunteer no-shows.

A work sample gets past the charm. It shows organization, follow-through, and writing, which are the core of a coordinator role.

### 5. Verify the facts
- Confirm employment dates and titles.
- Check claimed outcomes where you can: published reports, grant announcements, LinkedIn connections at those organizations, news items about the projects.
- Look for mismatches between the résumé, LinkedIn, and what they said in the room.

### 6. Talk to your panel again
Ask each panelist one question: **"What specific evidence do we have that they can do this job?"** Separate "I liked them" from "they showed X." Also tell the panel what you noticed. They may have been charmed by the same moments that worried you, or they may have seen things you didn't.

### 7. Compare against your other candidates on evidence
Score everyone against the same job criteria (organization, follow-through, communication, collaboration, relevant experience) using what each person *demonstrated*, not your overall impression. A less polished candidate with concrete, verifiable results is often the better hire.

## Decision guide

**Lean toward an offer if** the follow-up interview brings out concrete, consistent detail, references (especially supervisors) confirm their role and say they'd rehire, the work sample is solid, and they can describe past conflicts with some ownership.

**Pass, or keep looking, if** the specifics never come, references are lukewarm or evasive, they won't let you contact former supervisors, their claims don't match what you can verify, or the blame pattern shows up again.

**If you're still unsure but leaning yes:** use a clear probationary period (such as 90 days) with written expectations and regular check-ins, so you can catch problems early. Don't use this to skip the checks above.

## The bottom line
In a small nonprofit, a hire who overstates, deflects blame, and wins people over can do real damage: frayed partner relationships, missed grant deliverables, and team conflict. Checking a few days longer costs very little compared with a bad hire. Give the candidate a fair chance to back up what they said. If they can, great. If they can't, the panel's enthusiasm alone shouldn't decide it.
