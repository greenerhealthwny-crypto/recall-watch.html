# Following a Beloved Director: The Laws That Apply and a 90-Day Plan

You're in one of the hardest leadership positions there is. Greene's book covers it almost directly. Use the laws below as a lens for seeing the situation. Don't treat them as a script for manipulating people. Your staff aren't your enemies. They're grieving a leader they trusted, and they don't know you yet.

---

## The laws that matter most here

### Law 41: Avoid Stepping into a Great Man's Shoes
**This is your central law.** Whatever you do first gets measured against Linda. If you copy her, you'll always be the lesser version. If you openly reject her, you insult the people who loved her. Greene's answer is to make your own space: set up your own identity, priorities and way of working, so people stop running the comparison and start judging you on your own terms.

**In practice:** Never criticize Linda, publicly or privately, because word gets around. Credit her for what she built. Then make clear that the intake process is a *new problem* for a *new time*, not a judgment on her. Use framing like "The volume and requirements have changed since this process was designed," not "Linda's process was a mess."

### Law 45: Preach the Need for Change, but Never Reform Too Much at Once
**This is your operating rule for the intake overhaul.** People are attached to familiar forms even when they're broken. Greene's advice is to keep the *feel* of the old ways (names, rituals, the parts that work) while you change what's underneath. Change too much too fast and you make the old regime look better in hindsight than it really was.

**In practice:** Phase the overhaul. Keep the parts of intake that staff value and that clients like. Change the parts that cause real pain first, and pick the ones staff complain about themselves.

### Law 43: Work on the Hearts and Minds of Others
"That's not how Linda did it" is really about loyalty and loss, not process. You can't force your way past it. You have to earn your place by finding out what each person cares about and showing them that you see it.

### Law 9: Win Through Your Actions, Never Through Argument
You won't win the "Linda" argument by debating it. Every time you argue the point, you lose some standing. A visible, concrete improvement does more than any speech.

### Law 4: Always Say Less Than Necessary
In the first weeks, listen far more than you announce. A new leader who unveils a big plan in week one hands the resisters a target and commits to fixes before understanding the problems.

### Law 19: Know Who You're Dealing With / Law 42: Strike the Shepherd (reinterpreted)
Every team has informal leaders, and they're often the people saying "Linda did it differently" the loudest. In the original law, Greene says to isolate the shepherd. **Don't do that.** In a team you need to keep, the better move is to *recruit* the shepherds. Find out who people listen to. Often it's Linda's most trusted lieutenant. Bring them into the redesign early. If you win them over, the flock follows.

### Law 7 (reversed for leaders): Credit
Greene's version says to take credit for others' work. As a new director, do the opposite. Give credit for the new intake process to the staff who helped design it. It costs you nothing, because everyone knows you led it, and it gives them ownership.

### Law 28: Enter Action with Boldness and Law 29: Plan All the Way to the End
After you've listened, commit fully. A half-hearted rollout that you quietly reverse when people push back teaches staff that resistance works. Before you launch, know what "done" looks like: metrics, training, the transition date, and what happens to the old forms.

### Law 25: Re-Create Yourself
You get one chance to set what kind of director you are. Decide on purpose what you want staff to say about you in a year, for example "she listens, then she fixes things," and act that way consistently.

### Law 46: Never Appear Too Perfect
Admit what you don't know yet. Ask for help. That takes the edge off resentment toward "the new boss" and makes the comparison with Linda feel less like a competition.

### Law 1: Never Outshine the Master (applied upward)
Remember who promoted you. Keep your own boss informed and let them share the win. Their visible support is what you fall back on when resistance escalates.

### Law 47: Do Not Go Past the Mark in Victory
When the new intake process works, stop there. Don't follow it straight away with five more overhauls. Let the win settle in and build trust before your next change.

**Laws to deliberately avoid here:** Law 15 ("Crush your enemy totally"), Law 33 ("Discover each man's thumbscrew") and Law 17 ("Keep others in suspended terror") would damage a team you have to lead for years. They also tend to drive off your best people.

## Your first 90 days

### Days 1–30: Listen and learn (don't change anything yet)

**Goal:** Understand the intake process and the people before touching either.

- **Meet one-on-one with every staff member** (30–45 min). Ask:
  - "What did Linda do that you most want to keep?"
  - "What about client intake frustrates you most?"
  - "If you were in my seat, what's the first thing you'd fix?"
  - "What do you worry I'll change?"
- **Honor Linda openly, once and sincerely.** At an early team meeting, acknowledge what she built and that her absence is a real loss. Say this up front so it doesn't come across as defensive later.
- **Map the current intake process as it actually works.** Sit with staff and watch real intakes. Write down every step, handoff, form, delay and workaround. Measure the basics: time from first contact to first service, drop-off rate, error/rework rate, client complaints, staff hours per intake.
- **Separate Linda-wisdom from Linda-habit.** Some of "how Linda did it" is good judgment you should keep. Some is workarounds she never had time to fix. Your listening will show you which is which.
- **Identify your shepherds and early allies.** Who do people go to? Who is quietly frustrated with the current process and ready for change?
- **Align with your boss** on what success looks like, how much change they'll back, and the timeline.
- **Get one small, visible quick win.** Pick something staff themselves named as annoying and that you can fix fast, such as a redundant form, a broken shared folder or a confusing handoff. Fix it and credit the person who raised it.

### Days 31–60: Diagnose and co-design

**Goal:** Build the new intake process *with* staff, not *for* them.

- **Share back what you heard.** Present the themes from your one-on-ones and the process map: "Here's what you told me." When people recognize their own words, they're more likely to accept the conclusions.
- **Make the case for change with data and client impact, not with criticism of the past.** For example: "Clients wait an average of X days. We lose Y% before first appointment. That's what we're fixing."
- **Form a small intake redesign group** of 3–5 people. Include at least one respected Linda loyalist and at least one frontline person who does intake daily. Give them real authority over the design.
- **Name what stays.** State explicitly which parts of the current process, including Linda's practices, are being kept. This is the Law 45 move, and it lowers anxiety a lot.
- **Design the future process.** Fewer handoffs, one source of truth for client information, clear ownership at each step, standard forms and defined timelines.
- **Pilot on a small scale.** Test with one team, one program or one intake type for 2–4 weeks. Collect feedback and adjust.

### Days 61–90: Launch, support and lock in

**Goal:** Roll out the change with confidence and make it stick.

- **Announce the full rollout with a clear plan:** the date, training, who to ask for help, and when the old process formally ends. Don't run two systems side by side for long.
- **Let the design group present it** alongside you. Their voices carry weight that yours doesn't yet.
- **Train hands-on.** Provide job aids, checklists and a go-to person for questions. Expect performance to dip briefly at first.
- **Handle "that's not how Linda did it" calmly and the same way every time.** For example: "You're right, it isn't. Linda built something that worked for its time, and we're building on it. What specifically isn't working for you in the new version?" That turns nostalgia into useful feedback.
- **Report results at around day 90.** Compare against the baseline metrics from month one and share the gains publicly. Credit the team and make sure your boss sees the results.
- **Stop and consolidate (Law 47).** Don't start the next big change right away. Give people time to trust you and the new system.

## Handling ongoing resistance

- **Separate nostalgia from substance.** When someone says "Linda did it differently," ask what *outcome* they're worried about. Sometimes they've spotted a real flaw in your plan.
- **Address persistent undermining privately, not publicly.** Have a direct, respectful conversation. If someone keeps blocking the change after being consulted and heard, treat it as a performance issue and address it.
- **Expect the comparisons to fade, not end.** By around month 6 they usually drop off once people have their own experience of working with you. You win by being a good director in your own way, not by defeating Linda's memory.

---

## Bottom line

Greene's most relevant insight here is Law 41: **don't compete with Linda; make the comparison irrelevant.** Do that by honoring her, listening first, building the new intake process with your staff rather than imposing it, changing things in phases, and letting results speak for you. In 90 days your aim isn't to replace how Linda did things. It's to give people a new way of working they helped design and would choose to keep.
