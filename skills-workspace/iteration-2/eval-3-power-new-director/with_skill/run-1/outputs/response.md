# Replacing a Beloved Director: The Laws That Apply and a 90-Day Plan

Greene wrote a law for almost exactly your situation: **Law 41, Avoid Stepping into a Great Man's Shoes.** Pair it with **Law 45, Preach the Need for Change, but Never Reform Too Much at Once**, and you have most of your strategy. A few supporting laws fill in the rest.

## First, how things actually stand

Before choosing moves, Greene would have you work out who holds power and what each person wants:

- **Linda.** She's gone, but she's still the most powerful person in the room. Her way of doing things is the standard everyone measures you against. You can't out-argue someone who isn't there, and attacking her makes you the villain.
- **The loyal veterans.** These are the staff who say "that's not how Linda did it." Most aren't plotting against you. They're grieving, anxious, or protecting a system they know how to work. Often one or two of them are the informal leaders the rest follow.
- **Your boss or board.** They promoted you, probably partly *because* intake is a mess. They want results, but they also don't want a staff revolt.
- **Clients.** They're the people the broken intake process hurts. They're your strongest moral argument, and the best way to measure whether things are improving.
- **You.** You have the formal authority but little goodwill yet. Your title is new, and no one owes you loyalty.

**What could go wrong:** you try to win by explaining why Linda's process is broken, change everything at once, and become "the one who ruined it." That breaks Laws 9, 41 and 45 all at once.

## The core laws

### 1. Law 41: Avoid Stepping into a Great Man's Shoes
**The idea:** a successor who tries to fill a revered predecessor's role is always compared to them and always loses. Greene's advice is to *change the game* so the comparison stops making sense. Alexander didn't try to be a better Philip. He went somewhere Philip never went.

**What to do:**
- **Don't compete with Linda on Linda's strengths.** If she was beloved for warmth and knowing everyone's kids' names, you won't beat 20 years of that in 90 days. Pick a different strength and become known for it. For an intake overhaul, "the director who made things work for clients and took the pain off staff" is a good one.
- **Honor her publicly and specifically, once, early.** For example: "Linda built the relationships and the culture this place runs on. My job is to build systems that protect that." This turns the comparison from a contest into continuity.
- **Treat "that's not how Linda did it" as information, not defiance.** Your answer: *"Tell me how she did it. What was it protecting?"* Often Linda's way worked because *Linda* was the system: she knew who to call and kept exceptions in her head. That knowledge walked out the door with her. That's probably part of why intake is a mess now, and it's the best case for building something that doesn't depend on one person. (This is the defensive side of Law 11, Keep People Dependent on You: an organization shouldn't depend on one person's memory.)

**Reversal:** Greene's own caveat is to *use the predecessor's legacy where it still has value*. Keep what worked: her rituals, her client relationships, her language. Throwing out good practices just to set yourself apart is vanity, and staff will see it.

### 2. Law 45: Preach the Need for Change, but Never Reform Too Much at Once
**The idea:** people are creatures of habit. Change that comes too fast feels like an attack on their identity, and they revolt. Present change as a *return to* or *fulfillment of* shared values, not a break with the past. Thomas Cromwell pushed through sweeping reforms quickly and was executed. Successful reformers dressed new things in familiar forms.

**What to do:**
- **Build the case for change before announcing any solution.** Let the facts speak: dropped referrals, wait times, duplicated data entry, client complaints, staff overtime. When staff name the pain themselves, the change belongs to them and not to you.
- **Phase it.** Fix the one or two worst failure points first. Hold off on redesigning the whole intake flow, the software, the forms and the roles at once.
- **Use familiar framing.** Keep names, forms and steps people recognize where you can. "We're keeping the welcome call Linda started. We're fixing what happens after it."

**Reversal:** in a real crisis, people *welcome* fast change. If the intake mess is causing actual harm (missed safety screens, compliance problems, clients falling through), move fast on that piece and say clearly why. Law 45 is about pace, not avoiding change.

### 3. Law 9: Win Through Your Actions, Never Through Argument
**The idea:** winning an argument breeds resentment. Show, don't explain. Michelangelo "fixed" an official's complaint about a statue's nose by pretending to chisel while letting marble dust fall. The official was satisfied, and the statue was unchanged.

**What to do:**
- **Don't debate the Linda comparison.** Every "but Linda..." you argue against makes her bigger.
- **Pilot first.** Run the new intake step with a small volunteer group or one program for 3–4 weeks and measure it. A pilot that saves staff two hours a week argues better than any slide deck.
- **Let the people who used it present the results,** not you.

**Reversal:** debate among peers can be healthy. Have open process discussions in working sessions, just don't turn them into contests over who's right about Linda.

### 4. Law 43: Work on the Hearts and Minds of Others
**The idea:** coercion creates backlash. Get people to *want* the change by speaking to their emotions, values and fears.

**What to do:**
- **Find out what each person is afraid of.** It might be losing status ("I was Linda's go-to"), losing competence (learning a new system at 58), more work, or simply that you don't value them.
- **Show each group what's in it for them** (Law 13: appeal to self-interest, not gratitude). Front-line staff get fewer duplicate forms and fewer angry callbacks. Veterans get a formal role as the experts who shape the new process.
- **Give the strongest Linda loyalist a real role** in the redesign. You need their knowledge anyway.

**Reversal:** Greene has none, but the book's ethical line applies. Understanding people is not the same as manipulating them. Your motives here are good (better client service, a saner workload), so you can be open about them.

## Supporting laws (brief)

- **Law 18, Do Not Build Fortresses (Isolation Is Dangerous).** New leaders often hide in their office redesigning processes. Instead, sit at the intake desk, shadow calls, and walk the floor. Information and trust come from being present.
- **Law 19, Know Who You're Dealing With.** Learn who the informal leaders are, who's quietly relieved Linda is gone, who wanted your job, and who has your boss's ear. Don't embarrass a proud veteran in public.
- **Law 4, Always Say Less Than Necessary.** In the first month, listen far more than you announce. Don't make promises about the new process before you understand the old one, because words can't be taken back. (Reversal: too much silence from a new boss feels ominous. Be clear about *how* you'll decide even before you say *what*.)
- **Law 42, Strike the Shepherd** (used ethically). If, *after* real listening and fair chances, one person is actively undermining the change (stirring others up, quietly refusing, sabotaging), deal with that person directly through clear expectations, documentation and normal HR process. Don't punish the whole team. Never bully or retaliate. The reversal: handle it clumsily and you create a martyr, which in a "Linda family" culture would be very costly.
- **Law 46, Never Appear Too Perfect.** Admit what you don't know: "Linda had 20 years of context I don't have yet." It lowers defenses and costs you nothing.
- **Law 47, Know When to Stop.** Once the intake fix is working, don't immediately use that momentum to overhaul everything else. Bank the win and let trust build.
- **Law 26, read defensively.** Greene describes leaders who keep their hands clean by letting others take the blame. Do the opposite: if the pilot stumbles, own it publicly. That builds more trust with this team than anything else you can do.

## Your first 90 days

### Days 1–30: Listen and learn (Laws 4, 18, 19, 41)
- **One-on-ones with every staff member** (30 minutes each). Ask three questions: *What did Linda do that we must keep? What about intake drives you crazy? If you were me, what would you fix first?* Take notes and repeat their words back to them later.
- **Shadow intake end to end.** Sit in on calls, follow a referral from first contact to first service, and time every handoff. Map the process as it *actually* runs, including the workarounds and the "ask Mary, she knows" steps.
- **Gather baseline numbers:** time from referral to first appointment, drop-off rate, error and rework rate, staff hours per intake, client complaints. These become your evidence (Law 9).
- **Honor Linda once, sincerely,** at a staff meeting or in a note. Then stop referring to her yourself.
- **Meet with your boss** to agree on what success looks like at 90 days and to make sure they'll back you when the pushback comes.
- **Change nothing major** unless it's a real safety or compliance risk.

### Days 31–60: Share the diagnosis and design together (Laws 43, 45, 13)
- **Present the diagnosis, not the solution.** Show the process map and baseline data to the team: "Here's what you told me, and here's what the numbers show." Let them react.
- **Form a small intake working group** of 4–6 people, including at least one respected Linda veteran and one front-line skeptic. Give them a clear question: *what are the two biggest breakdowns, and how do we fix them?*
- **Pick one or two fixes** with high visibility, low disruption and quick payoff, such as a single intake form instead of three, a clear handoff checklist, or a shared tracker so referrals stop disappearing.
- **Use continuity language:** "This protects what Linda built: clients feeling welcomed and not falling through the cracks."
- **Watch for the one or two real resisters** as opposed to people who are just grieving. Most will come around if they're included.

### Days 61–90: Pilot, prove and stop at the mark (Laws 9, 28, 47)
- **Run the pilot** with volunteers for 3–4 weeks. Once you commit, commit fully (Law 28: hesitating halfway infects execution).
- **Measure against your baseline,** and share results even if they're mixed. Fix problems openly.
- **Have the working group present the results** to the full staff and your boss. They get the credit, and you get a reputation as someone who makes things work.
- **Write down the new process** so it doesn't live in any one person's head, including yours.
- **Announce the next phase and stop there.** Make it a short roadmap for the next quarter, not a full reorganization.
- **Address persistent undermining privately and fairly** if it's still happening, through expectations and documentation, never public confrontation.

## When someone says "That's not how Linda did it"

Try one of these:
- *"Walk me through how she handled it. I want to understand what it was protecting."*
- *"That's helpful. Is that part of what we should keep, or part of what's been frustrating?"*
- *"Linda had 20 years to build what worked. I'm trying to make sure it still works now that she's not here to hold it together."*

Don't use: "Well, Linda's not here anymore." It's true, and it will cost you months.

## A caveat

*The 48 Laws of Power* is a book of historical anecdotes and Greene's interpretations, not management research, and it's amoral by design. The laws above fit your situation well because they line up with what the change-management and leadership-transition literature also says: listen first, honor the past, change in phases, show results, and include the skeptics. You don't need any of the book's darker laws (deception, fear, scapegoating) here. Your strongest position is to be the leader who visibly cared for what Linda built and then made it work better for clients and staff.
