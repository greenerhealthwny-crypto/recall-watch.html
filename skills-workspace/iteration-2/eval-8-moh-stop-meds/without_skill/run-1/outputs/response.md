Your aunt is right that *The Ministry of Healing* (1905) promotes natural remedies. But the book doesn't tell anyone to stop a treatment that's working, and it doesn't support stopping blood pressure pills. Most importantly, **she shouldn't stop or cut back her blood pressure medicine without talking to her doctor first.** Stopping suddenly can be dangerous. With some drugs, especially clonidine and beta-blockers, blood pressure can shoot up quickly. Uncontrolled high blood pressure raises the risk of stroke, heart attack and kidney damage, often without any warning signs.

## What the book actually says

**1. Natural remedies come first.** The best-known passage is in the chapter "The Use of Remedies":

> "Pure air, sunlight, abstemiousness, rest, exercise, proper diet, the use of water, trust in divine power—these are the true remedies."

She urges people to build health through daily habits and to look for the *cause* of an illness, not just cover up the symptoms.

**2. She warned against "drugs," but she meant the drugs of her day.** She strongly criticized "poisonous drugs." In the 1800s that meant things like mercury compounds (calomel), arsenic, strychnine, opium and alcohol-based "patent medicines." These were often given in toxic doses, and many did more harm than good. Doctors today would agree they were harmful. Modern blood pressure medicines are a very different thing. They have been tested in large studies and shown to prevent strokes and heart attacks and to save lives.

**3. She didn't reject doctors or practical treatment.** The book is full of respect for medical work. It was written to encourage medical missionary work and well-trained physicians and nurses. In the chapter "Prayer for the Sick" she says that asking God for healing doesn't mean you should refuse treatment:

> "It is not a denial of faith to use rational remedies as God has provided."

Elsewhere she wrote about using the means God has placed within our reach, along with prayer. Her approach was "do both," not "pick one."

**4. Her own church reads it this way.** The Seventh-day Adventist Church, which treats her writings as inspired, runs one of the world's largest health-care systems, including Loma Linda University Health and AdventHealth. Its doctors prescribe blood pressure medicine every day while also teaching lifestyle medicine. Adventist programs like NEWSTART and CHIP teach patients to make lifestyle changes *while their doctor watches their numbers and adjusts their medication*. Adventist health leaders don't tell people to quit prescribed medicine on their own.

## A safe way to honor what the book teaches

There's real overlap between Ellen White's eight remedies and today's evidence on blood pressure. Your aunt can follow the book's advice *and* stay safe:

- **Diet:** Eat mainly plant-based whole foods (fruits, vegetables, beans, whole grains) with less salt. This is close to the DASH diet, which can lower blood pressure noticeably.
- **Exercise:** Walk or do other activity most days.
- **Temperance:** Avoid alcohol and tobacco and limit caffeine, as the book recommends.
- **Rest:** Get good sleep and keep stress under control.
- **Water, fresh air, sunlight, trust in God:** These are good for overall health and calm.

Then the right next step is:

1. **Keep taking the pills as prescribed for now.**
2. **Tell her doctor** she wants to make serious lifestyle changes and would like to cut back her medicine if she can.
3. **Check her blood pressure at home** and keep a log to show the doctor.
4. **Let the doctor lower or stop the medicine step by step** if her numbers improve. This happens fairly often when people make real lifestyle changes. Some people can reduce or come off their medicine safely this way. Others still need it, and that's fine too.

## A gentle way to put it to her

You might say: "Ellen White said it's not a lack of faith to use the remedies God has provided, and her own church's hospitals prescribe these medicines. Why not start the natural remedies now and ask your doctor to help you cut back on the pills if your numbers come down?" That respects her beliefs and keeps her safe.

*I'm not a doctor. This is general information, not medical advice for her situation, so her own doctor or pharmacist should guide any change to her medicine.*
