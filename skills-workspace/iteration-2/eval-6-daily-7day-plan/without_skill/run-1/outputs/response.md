# Your First 7 Days: A Daily Laws Practice Plan for a Hospital Admin Office

*The Daily Laws* (Robert Greene, 2021) gives you one meditation per day, and each month has its own theme. For someone starting a new job, the most useful months are **February (Apprenticeship)**, **April (The Perfect Courtier)**, **May (Supposed Nonplayers and Toxic Types)**, **October (The Emotional Self)** and **November (The Rational Human)**. This plan takes one idea from each and turns it into a small daily practice for your setting.

**A note on "not becoming a snake":** Greene's work is mostly about *seeing* power clearly. You can use it as a lens or as a weapon. In a hospital, your reputation for reliability and discretion is worth more than any clever move, and a misstep with patient information or a clinician's trust is hard to undo. So read each day's law as **defense and awareness, not manipulation**. Where Greene's advice turns cynical, I've noted the honest version.

*(These are paraphrases of the book's ideas and themes, not exact quotations of specific daily entries.)*

---

## Day 1: Be an apprentice first (February, *Apprenticeship*)
**The idea:** The first months of any role are for absorbing, not for proving yourself. Greene's apprentice puts learning ahead of money, status and looking clever. The aim is to watch how the place *actually* works.

**Practice today:**
- Keep a private "field notebook" (not on a work computer). Write down how things really get done: who approves the schedule changes, who knows the EHR or billing system workarounds, which forms always get sent back.
- Ask at least three "how does this usually work here?" questions, and don't follow any of them with "at my old job we…"

**Evening reflection:** *What did I see today that isn't in the org chart?*

---

## Day 2: Map the real power (April, *The Perfect Courtier*)
**The idea:** Every court has a formal hierarchy and a real one. The perfect courtier sees both without making a fuss about it.

**Practice today:**
- Sketch two maps: the official one (director, manager, you) and the informal one. In hospital admin the informal one usually includes the long-tenured unit secretary, the scheduler who controls OR or clinic slots, the person in revenue cycle who fixes denials, and whoever the physicians actually listen to.
- Pick out the **gatekeepers**, the people whose goodwill makes your work easier. Learn their names, their priorities and their pressure points (month-end close, Joint Commission prep, staffing shortages).

**Not a snake:** Map these people so you can help them and work with them, not so you can use them. Gatekeepers in a hospital are usually overworked, and they remember who made their day harder.

---

## Day 3: Don't outshine the master (April; also Law 1 of *The 48 Laws of Power*)
**The idea:** Make your superiors feel secure and capable. People who show up the boss, even by accident, start to feel the cold.

**Practice today:**
- When you have an improvement idea (a quicker intake form, a better tracking sheet), take it to your manager privately first and frame it as something that serves *their* goals. For example: "Would something like this help with the backlog you mentioned?"
- In meetings, don't correct your manager in front of others. Bring up corrections one-on-one.

**Not a snake:** You aren't hiding competence or flattering anyone. You're respecting the chain of credit. Do excellent work and let it be seen through the proper channels.

---

## Day 4: Say less than necessary (April / June themes on discretion)
**The idea:** People who talk too much give away their intentions, their judgments and their weak spots. Restraint reads as maturity.

**Practice today:**
- Run a "gossip fast." When someone vents about a coworker, a physician or leadership, listen, stay neutral, and don't add your own opinion. Lines that work: "That sounds frustrating." "I'm still learning how everyone works."
- Before you send any email that criticizes a person or process, wait an hour.

**Hospital-specific:** Discretion here is also a compliance issue. Never pass along patient details, even as a "can you believe this case" story. Your HIPAA discipline *is* part of your political reputation.

**Not a snake:** Staying quiet isn't scheming. It keeps you out of factions you don't understand yet.

---

## Day 5: Recognize the toxic types (May, *Supposed Nonplayers and Toxic Types*)
**The idea:** Greene warns that some of the most political people pose as "above politics." Watch for patterns instead of first impressions: the person who is overly friendly in your first week, the chronic complainer recruiting allies, the passive-aggressive colleague, the person who takes credit, and the one who plays the victim.

**Practice today:**
- Pay attention to who was *very* warm to you on day one. That's often perfectly genuine. Sometimes, though, it's someone looking for an ally in an existing feud. Be friendly to everyone and don't sign up with anyone yet.
- Watch for **patterns of behavior, not single incidents**. Note what people *do*, especially under pressure, rather than what they say about themselves.

**Not a snake:** You aren't labeling people. You're declining to be recruited. Your protection is being *cordial to all and aligned with none* until you understand the history.

---

## Day 6: Master your emotional self (October, *The Emotional Self*)
**The idea:** Your own reactions are the biggest threat to your strategy: defensiveness, wanting to be liked, irritation, and taking things personally. Greene's answer is to create a gap between the feeling and what you do about it.

**Practice today:**
- Some hospital moment will test you today: a curt physician, a nurse manager who blames admin, a patient family taking their stress out on the front desk. When it comes, use a **three-breath rule** before you respond.
- Name the emotion to yourself ("I feel dismissed"), then ask: *What response serves the work and my reputation in six months?*
- Most hospital rudeness comes from fatigue and the stakes of the job. It isn't about you. Treat it that way.

**Evening reflection:** *Where did I react, and where did I respond?*

---

## Day 7: Think long-term with your rational self (November, *The Rational Human*)
**The idea:** The rational person looks past the moment's drama to long-term consequences and asks what *actually* matters.

**Practice today (weekly review, 20 minutes):**
1. Reread your field notebook from Days 1–6. Update your two power maps.
2. Write down your **reputation in one sentence** as you want it to be in a year. For example: *"The person who gets things done accurately, keeps confidences, and makes clinicians' lives easier."*
3. List three small actions for next week that build that reputation. Make them concrete, like "close the loop on every request within 24 hours."
4. Pick one relationship to invest in, chosen for mutual usefulness and not just to get ahead. Offer help before you ask for any.

---

## Your guardrails

| Greene tactic you'll see in the books | Use it like this | Not like this |
|---|---|---|
| Conceal your intentions | Keep opinions to yourself while you're still learning | Mislead people about what you're doing |
| Court attention | Make your good work *visible* through updates | Steal credit or put on a show |
| Use selective honesty | Lead with generosity to build trust | Use generosity as bait |
| Know your enemies | Spot toxic patterns and avoid entanglement | Plot against or undermine people |
| Make yourself indispensable | Become the reliable expert on a process | Hoard knowledge so others fail without you |

**The bottom line:** Greene's own *Mastery* argues that real, lasting power comes from skill and from relationships built on trust. Learn the game so nobody blindsides you. Then win it by being excellent and trustworthy.

**After Day 7:** Repeat the cycle in week two, but trade Day 1 for a deeper study of one process you own, and Day 5 for noticing who the *quietly respected* people are. Those are the people you want to emulate.
