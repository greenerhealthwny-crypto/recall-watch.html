# 7 Days in the Court: A Daily Laws Plan for a New Hospital Admin Job

Congratulations on the new job. Greene's April theme in *The Daily Laws* is **"The Perfect Courtier,"** and his point is that every workplace is a court. Power is always in play, whether people admit it or not. The courtier who lasts isn't the schemer. It's the person who sees clearly, stays calm and becomes quietly indispensable. May (**"The Supposed Nonplayers"**) is about the defensive skill of reading people by what they do, not what they say. November (**"The Rational Human"**) is about the self-control that holds it all together.

I picked seven entries for your first week. Each day has the calendar title it comes from, a short reflection, one action and one question for the evening. I left out the darker entries on purpose ("Seem Dumber Than Your Mark," "Be Merciless with Your Enemies," "Sow the Seeds of Doubt"). You can understand them so you recognize them when someone uses them on you, but they aren't for you to practice.

---

## Day 1: Find Out Who Holds the Strings *(April 3)*

The org chart shows who has authority. It doesn't show who has influence. In a hospital admin office, the real hubs are often the long-tenured scheduler, the department secretary who controls the director's calendar, the billing lead everyone asks for help, or the person IT actually answers. A new courtier's first job is to see the hidden map.

- **Action:** Watch quietly. Notice who people go to with questions, whose "let me check with ___" comes up most, and who gets copied on everything. Sketch a private two-column map: *formal authority* and *real influence*.
- **Evening question:** Who has the most influence on how my days go, and do they know who I am yet?

## Day 2: Always Say Less Than Necessary *(April 10)*

New people over-explain, over-share and give opinions on how things "should" work before they understand why things are the way they are. Each extra word gives away information and commits you to positions too early. Being quiet early on isn't sneaky. It's humility you can see, and it gives you time to learn.

- **Action:** In every meeting today, ask one good question for every opinion you give. When someone vents about a coworker, listen and say something neutral ("That sounds frustrating"). Don't agree and don't add a story of your own.
- **Evening question:** What did I say today that I didn't need to say?

## Day 3: Never Outshine the Master *(April 1)*, read the honest way

Greene's law is about not making your superior feel insecure. You don't need to hide your talent to follow it. You aim your talent at your manager's success. If you spot a broken workflow, bring it to your boss as something that helps their goals ("I noticed X. Would it help if I drafted a fix for you to look at?"). Don't announce it in a group meeting as proof the old way was dumb.

- **Action:** Ask your manager directly: *"What does a great first 90 days look like from your side? What are you under pressure on right now?"* Then make sure your first visible wins line up with their answer.
- **Evening question:** Does my boss see me as a help or a threat? What gave them that impression?

## Day 4: Know When to Take and Give Credit *(April 4)*

Credit is a currency at work, and how you handle it shows people who you are. If you hoard it, you make enemies. If you give all of it away, you disappear. The honest middle is to give credit generously and specifically in public, and make sure your own contributions are written down where they count.

- **Action:** Today, thank one person by name in front of others (or in an email that copies their manager) for something concrete they did to help you get up to speed. Separately, start a private "wins log" with dates, tasks and results for your first review.
- **Evening question:** Who has helped me most this week, and have I said so where it counts?

## Day 5: Judge Them on Their Behavior, Not on Their Words *(May 3)*

In your first weeks, everyone shows their best mask. The person who's warmest on day one may be lonely, may be recruiting you to their side, or may be both. Greene's defense is to watch patterns. Does this person keep small promises? How do they talk about people who aren't in the room? How do they treat people with less power, like transporters, temps and the cleaning staff?

- **Action:** Choose two people you're unsure about. For one week, write down only what they *do*, not what they say. Hold off on any conclusions until Friday.
- **Evening question:** Who has been very friendly very fast, and what might they want?

## Day 6: Stay Above the Fray *(April 17)*

Every office has factions. In hospitals they often form around departments, shifts, or "the old director's people" versus "the new director's people." If you join a side in your first month, you inherit its enemies before you even know who they are. Greene's courtier stays cordial with everyone and committed to none of the camps. That's how you avoid becoming a snake: you don't need to play factions because you aren't in one.

- **Action:** Make a rule for yourself: no gossip in, no gossip out. If someone pulls you in, steer back to the work ("I'm still figuring out how everything fits. What's your take on the new intake process?"). Never repeat or comment on anything about patients or coworkers' health. That's both an ethics issue and a privacy-compliance issue.
- **Evening question:** Did I take a side today without meaning to?

## Day 7: Increase Your Reaction Time *(November 3)* and review the week

Every other law this week depends on one skill: the space between a trigger and your response. Someone takes credit for your work, a doctor's office snaps at you over the phone, a coworker forwards your mistake to the whole team. The courtier who wins is the one who doesn't react in the moment. Greene puts it this way: rationality is not a trait you either have or don't. It's a skill you train.

- **Action:** Use a personal rule for anything that stings: *no reply for one hour, and no reply in writing while upset.* Then spend 15 minutes reviewing the week. Go over your influence map (Day 1), your wins log (Day 4) and your behavior notes (Day 5).
- **Evening question:** What did I learn about this place that I couldn't have known on Day 1, and what do I still not know?

---

### The one-line version of the week

> **See the real map, speak less, make your boss look good, share credit, judge by actions, avoid factions, and never react in the heat of the moment.**

None of that requires you to deceive anyone. Greene's courtier is dangerous to fools and manipulators mainly because they're hard to read and hard to provoke. You can be that and still be the most trustworthy person in the office.
