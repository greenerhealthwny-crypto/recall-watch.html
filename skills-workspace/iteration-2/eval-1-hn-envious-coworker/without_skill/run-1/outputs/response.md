That's a disorienting thing to learn, especially about someone who went out of her way to welcome you. Before deciding what's going on, it helps to separate what you know from what you're inferring. Then you can respond in a way that protects you whatever her motives turn out to be.

## What might be going on

**1. Envy or a sense of threat (the classic pattern).** Very warm, eager friendliness toward a newcomer, followed by quiet undermining once that person starts doing well, is a recognizable pattern. Early on, "showing you the ropes" puts her in the senior, helpful role. If you're now leading a visible project, maybe one she wanted or feels she should have had, you've moved from protégé to competitor. Public praise and private doubt fit together here: the praise keeps her looking like a team player, and the private comments plant doubt where it counts. "Risky" and "in over her head" are well-chosen words. They sound like concern, not attack, so they're hard to push back on.

**2. Real concern, said badly.** Maybe she does think the project has risks, or that you're stretched thin, and she raised it with the manager because that felt safer than telling you directly. Many people avoid conflict this way. It still isn't great, since she should have told you, but it isn't sabotage.

**3. The message got distorted.** You *heard* this, so it's secondhand. "The timeline looks aggressive, I hope they have support" can turn into "she said you're in over your head" after a retelling or two. Also consider who told you and why. People sometimes pass these things along to stir things up.

The right move is the same whichever of these is true, which is useful.

## How to handle it

**Don't confront her or go cold yet.** If you accuse her using secondhand information, you lose. She can deny it, you look paranoid, and if she *is* undermining you, you've shown her that you know and confirmed that you're rattled. Stay friendly and professional. Be a bit more guarded about what you share with her: your worries, your mistakes, your half-formed ideas. That's exactly the material that can get repackaged as "she's struggling."

**Go to your manager, but about your work, not about Dana.** Your manager's opinion is what counts here, so shape it directly. Set up a check-in and get ahead of any doubts:
- "I want to make sure we're aligned on [project]. Here's where it stands, here are the main risks I see, and here's how I'm handling each one."
- "Do you have any concerns about scope, timeline, or my approach? I'd rather hear them early."

This works in every scenario. If Dana raised concerns, your manager now hears a confident, clear-eyed answer from you. If your manager *does* have doubts, you get them out in the open. Naming the risks yourself also takes the sting out of "risky." A project lead who sees and manages risk looks competent, not in over their head.

**Make your progress visible.** Send short, regular written updates (wins, milestones, risks and how you're mitigating them) to your manager and relevant stakeholders. The record then speaks for you, and offhand comments carry less weight.

**Keep notes quietly.** Write down dates, what you heard, and from whom, along with anything specific you notice later. Most likely you'll never need them. If a pattern develops, you'll have facts instead of impressions.

**Consider pulling her in.** One option that disarms people: ask Dana for input on a specific part of the project. "You've been here longer, I'd value your take on X." If she's honestly concerned, she'll tell you and you may learn something. If she's threatened, being included lowers the stakes, and once she's helped shape the project, attacking it is harder. Just don't share anything that would hurt you if it got passed on.

**Talk to her directly only if it keeps happening and you have something concrete.** Keep it calm and focused on the future: "I heard you might have some concerns about the project. I'd really prefer you bring them to me directly. I'm open to feedback." No accusations, no "how could you." That sets a boundary without starting a fight.

## Watch over the next few weeks

- **Is it a pattern?** More undercutting, credit-taking, information held back, or invisible effort to make you look bad points toward envy. One awkward comment points toward clumsiness.
- **How does she react when you succeed?** Real warmth vs. faint praise, quickly changing the subject, or a "yes, but…"
- **Does she do this to others?** Ask a trusted colleague casually. If she's done it to past newcomers, you'll learn a lot.
- **What does your manager actually think?** That matters more than anything Dana said.

## The bottom line

You can't control Dana's motives, but you can control the story your manager hears about you, and in the end your results tell it. Stay warm, be a bit more guarded, communicate more, and let your work make her comments look out of touch. If it becomes a real pattern that affects your standing, you'll have the documentation and the relationship with your manager to deal with it directly.

Three months in and already leading something visible enough to make someone nervous isn't a bad sign about you.
