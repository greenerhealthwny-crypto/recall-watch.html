# What's going on with Dana, and how to handle it

Short version: what you're describing fits what Robert Greene, in *The Laws of Human Nature*, calls **envy disguised as friendship**. It usually shows up as **passive aggression**: she's warm in public and plants doubts about you in private. Before you act on that, though, check the facts. You heard this secondhand, and that matters.

---

## 1. Reading the pattern

**Praise in public, undermining in private.** Greene's main point about envy (Law 10, *Beware the Fragile Ego*) is that envy is shameful to admit, so people almost never show it openly. It comes out as "concern," gossip, faint praise, or friendliness. Calling your project "risky" and saying you're "in over your head" is a clear example. It doesn't sound like an attack. It sounds like she's worried about the team, and it still damages how your manager sees you.

**The early friendliness.** Greene lists "too-eager friendship early on" as one of the classic signs of envy. His illustrating story is Jane Williams, who became a close friend of Mary Shelley and then quietly undermined her behind her back. This doesn't mean Dana's lunch and her offer to show you the ropes were fake. People are mixed, and she may really have liked you at first. A common sequence is this: a newcomer seems harmless, gets a warm welcome, then starts doing well, gets a visible project, or gets noticed by the manager. At that point the welcome turns into rivalry. **Envy is most common between peers**, and the trigger is usually a peer pulling ahead.

**The specific tactic.** Greene's chapter on aggression (Law 16, *See the Hostility Behind the Friendly Façade*) describes a passive-aggressive tactic he calls the **Insinuating-Doubt strategy**: comments, often presented as concern, that make other people doubt your competence. She doesn't have to criticize your work directly. She only has to give your manager a story ("risky," "in over her head") that colors how they see everything you do next.

**Which type she might be.** Greene describes several kinds of envier. Two could fit, depending on what you know about her:
- **The Status Fiend** measures herself by rank and feels threatened by anyone gaining ground near her. Ask yourself: has she been passed over for something? Did she want your project?
- **The Attacher** gets close to people who look promising, then turns on them when they start to shine.

His **Passive-Aggressive Charmer** (Law 9) also fits: someone whose niceness covers hostility she doesn't admit, maybe not even to herself. She may honestly think she's "just being realistic about the risks."

## 2. Check your own side first

Greene insists on this step, and it's especially important here.

- **You heard this secondhand.** Who told you, and what do they gain? Office gossip can distort a comment or add to it. "She had some questions about the timeline" can turn into "she says you're in over your head" after a couple of retellings.
- **Avoid confirmation bias** (Law 1). Once you have the label "she's envious of me," every small thing she does will seem to confirm it. Treat it as a strong guess, not a conclusion.
- **Check whether there's any truth in it.** Three months into a job, it's normal to be stretched. If your project does carry real risk, her motives don't matter as much as getting in front of that risk yourself. That also takes away her ammunition.

None of this means you're imagining things. Public praise combined with private undermining is a recognizable pattern. Just stay calm and observe her before you decide anything.

## 3. How to handle it

### Don't
- **Don't confront her angrily or accuse her of lying.** You can't prove what she said. She'll deny it, you'll look emotional and paranoid, and you'll have confirmed her "in over her head" story.
- **Don't retaliate or gossip about her.** That pulls you to her level and spreads the conflict across the team.
- **Don't keep confiding in her.** Stop sharing worries, setbacks, or plans that she could turn into "concern."

### Do

**1. Manage your manager directly, and soon.** This matters most. Don't mention Dana. Book time to walk your manager through the project yourself: goals, progress, the real risks, and your plan for each one. Something like: *"I want to make sure you have full visibility on X. Here's where it stands, here are the risks I'm watching, and here's how I'm handling them."* Then send regular short updates. You're replacing her secondhand story with firsthand evidence. A manager who hears about the risks from you, with a plan attached, won't see you as "in over your head."

**2. Keep facts on record.** Write down milestones, decisions, and who agreed to what. Greene's counter to passive aggressors is to *record facts and refuse to absorb the doubt*. Documentation protects you without starting a fight.

**3. Stay cordial with her, but more guarded.** Keep being friendly and professional. Pulling away coldly gives her a story ("she's become difficult"). Just be more careful about what you share with her. Greene's advice is to stop taking it personally and study her "as the mineralogist studies a specimen." Watch how her face changes when you get good news, look for mixed praise ("great work, but…"), and notice who she talks to.

**4. Lower the envy trigger.** Greene's defenses against enviers are to downplay your success, show a few harmless flaws, share credit, and not flaunt your wins. In meetings, give credit to teammates, and to Dana where she's actually helped. Ask her opinion on something she knows well. Envy feeds on feeling diminished, so making her feel included and respected often takes the edge off. This isn't caving. It's strategy.

**5. Optional: a calm, light check-in.** If you're sure about what she said, you can bring it up without any accusation: *"Hey, I've heard there are some concerns about my project being risky. If you see problems, I'd really value hearing them from you directly. You know this place better than I do."* This tells her you know, without blaming her. It gives her a respectable way to say things to your face, and it makes going around you a little riskier for her. Use it only if you can stay completely calm.

**6. Build other allies.** Don't let Dana be your main guide to the office. Build your own relationships with your manager and other colleagues, so no single person shapes how you're seen.

## 4. The bigger picture

Greene's goal isn't only defense. He wants you to become a calm, strategic observer, operating from what he calls your **Inner Athena**: the rational self that sees people as they are and doesn't react from hurt. Dana's behavior probably has more to do with her own insecurities than with you. If you stay steady, do excellent work, keep your manager informed, and don't take the bait, her insinuations will usually lose credibility over time. Results are hard to argue with.

Also keep an eye on your own reaction. If you notice resentment or a wish to "beat" her growing, use it as fuel for your own work and don't aim it at her. Greene calls this *controlled aggression*: put the energy into excellence instead of conflict.

---

*A caveat: Greene's framework is a synthesis of psychology, history, and his own interpretation. It's a useful lens, not a diagnosis. You know Dana and your workplace better than any book does. If the undermining escalates into something like sabotaging your work, false claims in writing, or harassment, take it to HR with your documentation.*
