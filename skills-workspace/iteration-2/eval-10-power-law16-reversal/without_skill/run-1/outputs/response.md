## Law 16: Use Absence to Increase Respect and Honor

The core idea is that too much presence lowers your price. If you are always available, people take you for granted. If you withdraw at the right moment, they start to miss you, talk about you, and value you more. Greene calls this the law of scarcity.

### The Reversal (read this first)

Greene says the law **only works once you already have a certain level of power or value in the other person's eyes.** If you withdraw too early, before you've made yourself necessary, you won't be missed. You'll be forgotten and replaced. So his order is: **first make yourself visible and needed, and only then use absence.** He also warns that absence has to be timed and measured. If you stay away too long, people get used to life without you, and someone else fills the gap.

**For you as a consultant, the key question is this:** does the client already feel they depend on you? Ask yourself:
- Would losing you leave a real gap in knowledge, relationships or results, or could another firm step in within a month?
- Do they come to you, or are you still the one chasing them?
- Is there a budget cycle, renewal or procurement review coming up where "less visible" could simply look like "less valuable"?

If your answers lean toward "replaceable", the Reversal says don't pull back yet. Build your presence first.

### Historical examples Greene uses

I'm working from memory of the book, so the details here are worth checking against your copy.

- **Guillaume de Balaun and Madame Guillelma de Javiac (medieval courtly love).** A troubadour-knight saw that his lady valued him more after a separation and a reconciliation, so he faked a quarrel and stayed away on purpose. The game worked until she turned the tactic back on him. In the end he had to perform a humiliating penance (tearing out a fingernail and sending it to her with a poem) to win her back. It shows absence can raise your value, and also that the other side can play the same game.
- **Deioces of the Medes (from Herodotus).** Deioces made his name as a fair judge, and the people came to rely on him. Then he withdrew and refused to judge any more. Without him, lawlessness came back, and the Medes begged him to return and made him king. Once he had power, he shut himself inside his palace and was seen only rarely, and his mystique and authority grew. This is the main example of the law done right, and it follows the Reversal's sequence: he made himself indispensable first and withdrew second.
- **Supporting material in the Keys / Authority sections.** Napoleon's line that if he were seen too often at the theater, people would stop noticing him. The economics of scarcity, such as the Dutch tulip mania, where rarity alone drove value. And the general point that rulers who keep themselves remote are treated with more awe.

### Does another law contradict it?

Yes. Several laws pull against it, and Greene intends this. The 48 Laws are meant to be weighed against each other, not followed as fixed rules.

- **Law 6: Court Attention at All Costs.** This is the most direct contradiction. Law 6 says to stay visible and never get lost in the crowd. Greene's reconciliation is about sequence, and it's the same as the Reversal: use Law 6 to build your presence, then use Law 16 to make it scarce.
- **Law 18: Do Not Build Fortresses to Protect Yourself (Isolation Is Dangerous).** Withdrawing cuts you off from information and relationships. A consultant who pulls back may miss the internal politics, a new stakeholder, or the RFP being written for a competitor.
- **Law 11: Learn to Keep People Dependent on You.** This one is a precondition more than a contradiction. Absence only creates value if the client depends on you. Without that dependence, absence just teaches them to manage without you.
- **Related laws that work alongside it:** Law 17 (Keep Others in Suspended Terror: Cultivate an Air of Unpredictability) and Law 35 (Master the Art of Timing). The moment you choose to withdraw matters as much as the withdrawal itself.

### Practical take for your situation

Dramatically "going dark" on a paying client is risky. In business, unlike in courtly love, the usual reaction to an unresponsive vendor is to find another vendor. Quieter forms of scarcity tend to work better:

- **Scarcity built into the structure.** Limited capacity ("I take on X clients per quarter"), set office hours, fewer but more valuable meetings, and no longer answering instantly on routine matters.
- **Stop doing low-value work** that makes you look like staff rather than an advisor. Be present for the strategic moments and absent from the busywork.
- **Time it to a high point.** Step back right after you've delivered a clear win, while your value is fresh in their minds. Don't do it after a stumble or right before a renewal decision.
- **Keep it short and planned.** Know how you'll re-engage, for example with a well-timed insight, an offer for the next phase, or a check-in. Then the absence reads as "in demand", not "checked out".
- **Stay honest about obligations.** Never let strategic distance become missed deliverables or broken contract terms. That damages your reputation, which Law 5 says to guard with your life, and it hurts you more than any scarcity tactic can help.

**Bottom line:** Law 16 can work for you, but only after the client already feels they need you, and only in measured, well-timed doses. If you aren't sure they'd miss you, follow the Reversal and Law 6: become more visible and more essential first, and save the absence for later.
