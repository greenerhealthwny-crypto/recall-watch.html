# Law 16 for a consultant: the reversal, Greene's examples, and the laws that pull against it

**Law 16: Use Absence to Increase Respect and Honor.** Greene's argument is that too much presence lowers your value. When people see you all the time, you start to look ordinary. When you pull back, people notice the gap you leave and value you more. Scarcity creates value.

## 1. The reversal

The reversal is about timing. **Absence only works after you are established.** If you haven't yet made yourself valued and hard to replace, people won't miss you when you pull back. They'll forget you. Early on, you need to be visible. Withdrawal only helps once your presence has become something people want.

For a consultant, that means asking yourself three questions before you pull back:

- **Does this client actually depend on you?** Is your expertise hard to replace, or could another firm or an internal hire step in next quarter? If your role is easy to fill, they'll fill it.
- **Have you already delivered visible results?** If the relationship is new, or your impact isn't clear yet, pulling back looks like you've disengaged, not like you're scarce.
- **What happens if they don't call you back?** Greene's warning applies here: an absence that lasts too long, or comes too early, makes you forgettable, and a competitor fills the space.

## 2. Greene's historical examples

*(A note on sources: my reference notes summarize Laws 8–48 from the book's own judgments, and the stories for those laws come from general knowledge of the book. So I'll keep these short.)*

- **Deioces of the Medes** (from Herodotus). This is the main positive example. Deioces became known as a fair and indispensable judge of disputes. Then he withdrew and stopped judging. Disorder returned, and the Medes begged him to come back. They made him king, and as king he stayed largely hidden behind palace walls, which made him even more revered. Note the order: **he made himself indispensable first, and only then withdrew.**
- **Guillaume de Balaun and his lady** (a troubadour tale from medieval Provence). Here absence is used inside a relationship. The lady withdraws from the troubadour, and the withdrawal makes his love stronger. It shows how holding back can raise desire, and also how easily the game turns into manipulation and hurt.
- In the Keys to Power, Greene also quotes **Napoleon**, roughly: *if I am seen too often at the theatre, people will stop noticing me.* He also points to **scarcity in markets**, such as the Dutch tulip mania, where rarity alone drove value.

## 3. Laws that contradict it

Yes. Greene says himself that every law has a counter-law and that you have to judge which one fits. Three are relevant to you:

| Law | How it pulls against Law 16 | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Law 6: Court Attention at All Cost** | "What is unseen counts for nothing." Be visible, stand out, keep people interested. This is the most direct contradiction. | The two laws apply at different stages. Use Law 6 to build your reputation and Law 16 once you have one. |
| **Law 18: Do Not Build Fortresses — Isolation Is Dangerous** | Withdrawing cuts you off from information and allies. Greene uses Qin Shi Huang, who hid in his palaces and lost touch. | If you pull back, you stop hearing about internal politics, budget shifts, and the rival vendor taking your seat. Brief solitude is fine. Extended absence is dangerous. |
| **Law 11: Learn to Keep People Dependent on You** | It's less a contradiction than a precondition. Absence only works if they *need* you, and dependence is built by being present and delivering. | Before you pull back, make sure the client depends on you for something specific and hard to replace. |

Law 16 also clashes in spirit with **Law 9 (Win Through Your Actions)**. With a client, your value shows in the results you deliver. If they can't see you delivering, they have less evidence of your worth.

## 4. How to apply it as a consultant

A cruder version of Law 16 would be going quiet, slowing your replies, or dropping out of meetings to "make them miss me." That's risky. Clients usually read it as worse service, not as scarcity. It also gives them a reason to test life without you. A cleaner version builds **scarcity in how you're structured**, not in how responsive you are:

- **Narrow your scope to the highest-value work.** Stop doing the low-level execution that makes you look like ordinary staff. Being less present on routine work and very present on strategic work is Law 16 done well.
- **Make your time a defined resource.** Use set office hours, a fixed number of strategy sessions, or a retainer with clear limits. Being "available on a schedule" signals scarcity without looking like you've disappeared.
- **Be selective about which meetings you attend.** Skip status meetings and show up, prepared, for decision meetings.
- **Take on other clients and say so, briefly.** Clients value you more when other people want your time too. You don't need to vanish from this client to show that.
- **Keep the line open.** Stay in touch with your sponsor and one or two allies inside the client so you still hear what's going on (Law 18). Being scarce is fine. Being out of touch is not.
- **Tie it to a natural transition.** For example, at the end of a phase you can say: "Phase 1 is done; I'll step back while your team implements, and we'll reconvene in six weeks." That's an absence with a clear purpose and a planned return.

**Signs it's backfiring:** they stop inviting you, they start building the capability in-house, they bring in another vendor "to cover gaps," or renewal talks get vague. If you see any of these, go back to Law 6 and Law 11 right away: be visible and deliver something they can see.

**Defensive note:** clients and vendors use this law too. If a supplier suddenly becomes hard to reach just before a price talk, recognize it as Law 16 and judge them by what they deliver, not by how scarce they seem.

---

*Caveat: The 48 Laws is Greene's interpretation of historical anecdotes, not empirical research. In professional services, the more reliable way to be valued is to be clearly excellent and well-positioned, with scarcity as a deliberate part of how you structure the work. Pulling back as a mind game is the weaker version.*
