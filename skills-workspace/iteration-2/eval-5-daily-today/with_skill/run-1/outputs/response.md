# October 4: Analyze, Scrutinize, Question

**This month's theme is The Emotional Self: Coming to Terms with Our Dark Side.** October in *The Daily Laws* is built on Greene's *The Laws of Human Nature*. Its starting point is that we are emotional creatures first and rational ones second, often a distant second. Yesterday's entry introduced the "Inner Athena", the calm part of you that sees things clearly. Today's entry is about how you actually use her: you turn your attention on your own reactions and question them.

## Today's meditation

*(My own reflection in the spirit of the entry, not Greene's text.)*

In July 1863, after General Meade let Lee's army escape across the Potomac, Abraham Lincoln wrote him a furious letter. He never sent it. It was found years later in his papers, marked "never sent or signed." Lincoln had a habit of writing these "hot letters": he put the anger on paper, let it cool, and then decided what was actually worth doing.

When an emotion hits hard, it narrows your vision. One sentence fills the whole screen, and whatever you do next feels both urgent and justified. Greene's advice is to treat that feeling as information to examine, not an order to follow. Ask what you're really feeling. Ask whether the strength of it fits the event. Ask where it really comes from. Usually the trigger is small and the source is older and deeper: fear for something you've built, pride in your work, exhaustion. Once you've traced an emotion to its roots, it loses most of its hold on you. Then you get to choose how to respond, and the moment stops choosing for you.

**Daily Law:** Before you act on a strong emotion, question it. Ask what it is, where it comes from and whether it fits the facts. The pause gives you back your power.

## Your practice for today: the bakery "hot letter" rule

A bad review of your bakery stings because the bakery is *you*: your early mornings, your recipes, your money. That's why the replies come out angry. Here's a way to put today's law into practice:

1. **Write the hot reply, but not in the review app.** Use a notes app or paper. Say everything. Don't post it.
2. **Wait 24 hours** (or at least until after closing). This is Lincoln's drawer. Most of the heat will be gone by then.
3. **Question it before you respond.** In two lines, write down:
   - *What exactly am I feeling?* (Hurt? Embarrassed? Afraid it'll cost me customers?)
   - *What part of this review, if any, is true?* (A stale croissant, a long wait, a rude moment at the counter?)
4. **Write for the next customer, not the reviewer.** Future customers are reading your reply much more closely than the person who left the review. Keep it short and calm: thank them, acknowledge any real problem, say what you'll do about it, and offer to make it right offline. For example: *"Thanks for letting us know. We're sorry the order wasn't ready on time. We're changing how we schedule pickups. Please ask for me next visit so I can make it right."* If a review is plainly false or abusive, the strongest reply is often a brief, polite one, or none at all.

**Journal question for tonight:** *Which review made me angriest, and what was it really touching? My pride, my fear of failing, or something I already suspected was true?*

Your angry replies aren't really a character flaw. They show how much you care about the bakery. Today's job is to slow that caring down enough that it works in your favor.
