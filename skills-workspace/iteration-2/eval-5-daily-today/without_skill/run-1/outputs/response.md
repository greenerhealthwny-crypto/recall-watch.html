## October 4: The Emotional Self

In Robert Greene's *The Daily Laws*, October is about **the emotional self**: seeing how your feelings and irrational impulses take over your judgment, and learning to stay in control of them.

**I can't quote the exact October 4 entry from memory, and I'd rather not make one up.** If you have the book, read the actual page. What follows is the core idea of this month in my own words, applied to your situation. It isn't Greene's text.

> **Today's law (paraphrased): Feel the emotion, but don't act on it in the moment.** When something stings, your mind shrinks to a defensive reaction that feels completely justified. Make it a rule that you never act while that feeling is at its peak. Wait until it passes and you can see the situation for what it is.

Greene comes back to this often. He calls the rational side of us the "Inner Athena," the part that can step back and choose. In *The 48 Laws of Power* he also warns about letting others see you lose your temper. In public, the person who stays calm looks like the one in the right.

### What this means for the bakery

A bad review hurts because the bakery is personal. You get up early, you make everything by hand, and a stranger writes you off in two sentences. Being angry about that is normal. But an angry reply doesn't speak to that one reviewer. It speaks to **every future customer who reads it**, and what they learn is that the owner gets defensive. The bad review costs you a little. The angry reply can cost you much more.

**A practice for today:**

1. **24-hour rule.** Don't reply to any negative review on the day you read it. Write the furious version in your notes app if that helps, then delete it.
2. **Name the feeling before you write.** "I feel insulted / embarrassed / like my work isn't respected." Naming it takes some of its force away.
3. **Read it as information, not an attack.** Ask what part of it might be true, even 10%. Stale croissant? Long wait? Rude interaction on a busy Saturday? If you find something, it's useful. If you don't, the review tells you more about the reviewer than about you.
4. **Write for the audience, not the critic.** Keep replies short, calm and gracious:
   > "Thanks for letting us know. I'm sorry your visit didn't meet the standard we aim for. I'd love the chance to make it right, so please ask for [your name] next time you're in, or reach us at [email]."
   
   Leave out the excuses, the arguments about facts, and the sarcasm.
5. **Some reviews need no reply.** For obvious trolls or rants, saying nothing is often the strongest answer.

**Tonight's journal prompt:** *Which review got to me most, and what did it touch that was already sore?* Usually the anger isn't really about the review. It's about a fear the review woke up, like "I'm not good enough" or "all this work goes unnoticed." Once you see that, you'll find it much easier to answer calmly.
