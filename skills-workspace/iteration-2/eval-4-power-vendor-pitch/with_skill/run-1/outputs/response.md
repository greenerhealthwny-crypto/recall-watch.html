# Short answer: yes, it's a standard playbook. That doesn't make it a bad product.

You aren't being scammed. You're being *sold to*, with a set of pressure tactics so common that Robert Greene's *The 48 Laws of Power* names almost every one of them. The product may still be good. The setup is built so you decide quickly, with little information, on their terms. Your job is to take that setup apart and judge the software on its merits.

## What's being done to you, and the law behind each move

| What the rep is doing | The law at work | What it's for |
|---|---|---|
| "Free" setup and training if you sign | **Law 40: Despise the Free Lunch** and **Law 12: Selective Generosity** | A gift right before the ask lowers your guard and makes you feel you owe something. Setup isn't free. Its cost is spread across 36 months of fees. |
| Two pricing options, both 3-year terms | **Law 31: Control the Options** | You feel like you're choosing, but both choices give them the thing they want most, a long lock-in. The real question ("Should we commit for 3 years at all?") never comes up. |
| "You're so smart for seeing the value" | **Law 21: Play a Sucker / flattery** and **Law 33: Find the Thumbscrew** | Flattery tied to the decision makes it harder to back out. Saying no would mean you weren't so smart after all. Greene's example is the great diamond hoax, where sharp financiers were taken in because they were sure they were the clever ones. |
| "Sign this week" | **Law 35: Master the Art of Timing** (forced time) | A deadline that hurries you is a deadline that benefits them, usually because of the rep's quarter-end quota and not your clinic's needs. Greene: *never seem in a hurry*. |

Greene's general counter (§50 of his defensive checklist) fits here exactly: **slow down, say less, judge by actions over time, and keep your own goals in view.**

## What to do

### 1. Break the clock (Law 35)
Tell them, calmly: *"We don't make 3-year commitments on a one-week timeline. We'll have a decision by [date 2–4 weeks out]."* Then watch what happens. If the "free" setup disappears, it was never a gift. It was a lever. In practice, offers like this nearly always come back, often at the end of the next sales quarter.

### 2. Ask for the third option (Law 31's defense)
Their menu isn't the only one. Ask for:
- **A 1-year term**, or month-to-month after the first year
- **A 3-year price with a termination-for-convenience clause**, or an opt-out after year 1
- **A paid pilot** of 60–90 days, or a phased rollout
- **The setup and training fees shown as separate line items**, so you can see what "free" is actually worth

Whatever they refuse to put in writing tells you where the profit in the deal is.

### 3. Separate the gift from the decision (Laws 12 and 40)
Price the deal as though the setup and training cost full price. Work out the **total 3-year cost** (license + per-user/per-provider fees + annual escalators + add-ons + support tiers) and compare it with alternatives. If the deal only looks good because of the "free" part, it isn't a good deal.

### 4. Defuse the flattery (Law 33 defense)
Know your thumbscrew. Most of us like being seen as the sharp one. A neutral reply works well: *"Thanks. Being careful is part of seeing the value, so here's what I need before we sign."* You don't have to argue with a compliment. You just don't let it change your timeline.

### 5. Say less (Law 4)
Don't tell the rep your budget, your frustration with your current system, or how badly you need a fix. Each of those helps them and costs you. Ask questions, and let the silences sit.

### 6. Create real alternatives (Laws 16 and 20: don't commit, let them come to you)
Get quotes or demos from **2–3 competing vendors**. Nothing improves your terms like a believable alternative. Mention it simply: "We're evaluating a couple of options." Don't use it as a threat.

### 7. Plan all the way to the end (Law 29)
Greene's point is that the *ending* decides everything. With a 3-year clinic software contract, the ending is when you leave, so negotiate the exit now while you still have leverage:
- **Data export:** In what format, at what cost, and how quickly will you get *all* patient and practice data back if you leave?
- **Auto-renewal:** Watch for clauses that silently renew for another multi-year term, and for short notice windows.
- **Price escalators:** Cap annual increases. Look for "up to X%" language.
- **Termination rights:** What happens if they miss uptime/SLA commitments, get acquired, or drop a feature you depend on?
- **HIPAA:** If the software touches patient data, a **Business Associate Agreement (BAA)** isn't optional. Confirm security, breach notification and where the data is hosted.
- **Who owns the data:** It should say plainly that it's yours.

### 8. Verify with people, not pitches (Law 9: judge by actions, not words)
Ask for **references from clinics your size**, and also find one or two on your own (a peer network, your specialty society, online reviews). Ask them: How did implementation actually go? Did the training cover what was promised? How hard was it to get support? Did prices go up at renewal?

### 9. Get a second set of eyes
Before signing anything multi-year, have a healthcare attorney, or at least your practice manager, billing lead or an IT consultant, read the contract. A few hundred dollars of review is cheap next to 36 months of being locked in.

## The reversal (Greene always includes one)

- **Sometimes the free lunch really is worth taking** (Law 40's reversal): if the product is the right fit, the 3-year price beats the alternatives *even counting setup at full price*, and you've negotiated a real exit and data rights, then signing is a smart move, not a trap.
- **Don't let suspicion become its own trap.** The rep is doing their job, and these are ordinary sales methods, not fraud. No need to be hostile. Stay pleasant, take your time and negotiate. You'll get better terms and keep a good working relationship if you do sign.
- **Don't wait forever.** If your current system is causing real harm (billing errors, compliance gaps, burned-out staff), a reasonable deal signed in three weeks beats a perfect one in six months. Slow down enough to see clearly, and no more.

## A script you can use

> "We're interested, and I appreciate the offer. Before we can commit, I need three things: (1) pricing for a 1-year term and for a 3-year term with an opt-out after year one, (2) the setup and training fees itemized, and (3) the full contract including data-export, renewal, and BAA terms, plus two references from clinics our size. We'll have a decision by [date]. If the setup offer can't be extended to then, that helps us understand the deal too."

**Bottom line:** the "free" setup, the two-option menu, the flattery and the deadline all push you in one direction. Slow down, add a third option, price everything at full cost and secure your exit. If the deal still looks good after that, sign it with confidence.

*(A note on the source: Greene's book draws on historical stories and his own interpretation, not research. It's useful here mainly as a guide to spotting these tactics, and the advice above is standard contract caution either way.)*
