# Your Entrepreneurial Quotient, read through Mortimer Levitt

Mortimer Levitt founded The Custom Shop shirt chain and wrote *How to Start Your Own Business Without Losing Your Shirt*. He gave would-be founders an "Entrepreneurial Quotient," a self-check built on traits like **obsessive drive**, the **X factor**, and **happenstance**. I'm working from memory of his main ideas, not exact quotes. Treat the scores below as an honest reading of what you told me, not a precise test.

## Your profile at a glance
- **Age 34.** This is a good age to start. You have work experience and time to recover if it goes wrong.
- **Hate risk.** Levitt would say this is not a dealbreaker. His whole book is about starting *without* losing your shirt.
- **Love people.** This is a real asset in any business built on selling, service, or relationships.
- **$2,000 saved.** That's very thin. It's enough to test an idea, not to open a business with rent and stock.
- **Full-time job.** It's a safety net, and it also limits your time.

---

## 1. Obsessive drive: **unknown, so prove it** (provisional 4/10)

For Levitt, this is the trait that matters most: a need to build something that keeps you working when it's boring, tiring or scary. Nothing you told me shows it yet. Loving people and disliking risk say nothing about drive either way.

He'd ask:
- Is there one idea you keep coming back to, even when nobody is pushing you?
- Would you give up most evenings and weekends for a year to make it work?

**How to find out:** spend 10 hours a week on a small business test for 90 days. If you keep doing it without having to force yourself, the drive is there. If you keep finding reasons to skip it, you have your answer, and it cost you little.

## 2. The X factor: **promising** (provisional 6/10)

Levitt used "X factor" for the hard-to-name quality some people have: judgment, instinct for what customers want, the ability to make people trust and buy from you. Your love of people points in that direction, since most small businesses live or die on selling and service.

But liking people is not the same as **selling** to them. The test is whether you can ask strangers for money, hear "no," and come back the next day. Watch for that early.

## 3. Happenstance: **not yet present** (2/10, and that's fine)

Levitt was honest that luck and timing play a big part. The right opening, the right contact, a skill that suddenly matters. He didn't think you could plan happenstance, but he thought you could **put yourself where it happens**: in an industry, among customers, close to a problem you understand.

Right now you haven't named an idea or a market, so there's nothing for luck to catch on yet. You raise your odds by:
- Picking a field you already know from your job or your life
- Talking to many potential customers. Every conversation is a chance for an opening.
- Watching for a gap your employer, suppliers or customers keep complaining about

## Overall
**Rough EQ: mid-range, with the outcome hinging on drive.** You have the people side. You lack capital and haven't shown drive yet. Levitt wouldn't tell you to quit and leap. He'd tell you to **start small, keep the paycheck, and let results decide the next step**.

---

## How you should start

1. **Keep your job.** At $2,000, your salary is your investor. Levitt warned against starting with too little money, and quitting now would make that mistake for you.
2. **Pick a people-heavy, low-capital idea.** Choose something where your main input is time and skill, not stock or rent. Examples: a service business (cleaning, organizing, tutoring, event help, pet care), consulting or freelancing in what you already do, or a local sales or coordination role. Avoid anything that needs a lease, staff or inventory up front.
3. **Answer Levitt's key question before spending money:** *"Why would a customer buy from me instead of someone else?"* Write it in one sentence. Then get 10 real people to react to it.
4. **Give more without charging more.** Beat competitors with extras that cost you little: faster replies, follow-up calls, small guarantees, personal attention. This is where loving people becomes a business advantage.
5. **Run a 90-day test on nights and weekends.** Use your $2,000 for the basics only: simple branding, a basic website or social page, insurance or licensing if needed, and a small marketing test. Goal: land your first 3–5 paying customers.
6. **Set a clear milestone before you scale.** For example, when side income reaches a set share of your salary for three months in a row, *and* you have 6–12 months of living costs saved, then think about going full-time.
7. **Build your savings at the same time.** Even $300–$500 a month from your job gives you a real cushion within a year or two.

## What Levitt would say, in short
You don't need to love risk to start a business. You need to **shrink the risk** until you can live with it. Keep the job, test cheaply, let your people skills do the selling, and find out over 90 days whether the obsessive drive is really there. If it is, scale slowly. If it isn't, you've lost little and learned a lot.
