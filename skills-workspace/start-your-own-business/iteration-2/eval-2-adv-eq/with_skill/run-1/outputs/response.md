# Your Entrepreneurial Quotient, read through Mortimer Levitt

This draws on Mortimer Levitt's *How to Start Your Own Business Without Losing Your Shirt* (1988). Levitt founded The Custom Shop shirtmakers in 1937 with about $1,000.

## First, what the E.Q. quiz is and isn't

The Entrepreneurial Quotient quiz in the book was prepared by Northwestern Mutual Life. Levitt includes it while "not suggesting that you take this test too seriously"; he calls it "interesting or, at the very least, amusing." He says plainly that it **can't predict your success** and that entrepreneurial skills can be learned. So treat this as a conversation starter about motivation, risk and resilience, not a verdict.

I also can't give you a full numeric score. The quiz has many items (childhood, school record, family background, work habits) that you haven't told me about. The book's score bands are also given by label rather than with cut-off numbers I could apply. What I *can* do is score the parts you described against the items the quiz uses.

## Obsessive drive: what Levitt would say

This is the trait he cares about most. Relentless motivation is the one element common to all seventeen founders in the book: "motivation that is above and beyond the norm. It should be obsessive," and "a will to shine, not merely survive." He also says: "There is no such thing as a nine-to-five entrepreneur." He expects founders to put the idea ahead of sports, culture "and even family".

Notice what you *didn't* say. You told me what you dislike (risk) and what you enjoy (people), but not that there is something you can't stop thinking about. Levitt's hardest case is Arthur Imperatore, who had no angle at all, just two under-used Army trucks and all-consuming drive, and built the third most profitable trucking company in the US. Levitt's point: "without this all-consuming drive, few new business ideas, even brilliant ones, would get off the ground."

So the question for you is: **is there a problem or idea that pulls at you hard enough that you would give up evenings and weekends for it, for years?** If the honest answer is "not yet," that's the missing piece, and no amount of planning replaces it.

One note of fairness: Levitt admits his own "seemingly languid example". He stepped back from day-to-day operations in 1941, four years after starting. His drive was in finding and protecting the idea ("It's my idea, and it's my business"), not in endless hours forever.

## The X factor and happenstance in your case

**The X factor** is Levitt's name for "the single, totally unexpected situation that no research can uncover": new legislation, a depression, a new competitor with a better idea. **Happenstance** is luck, and it cuts both ways. He bought 550 acres in California that sat as dead money for 25 years after rezoning, and a Fifth Avenue building that he sold 30 years later for about 28 times what he paid: "No brains, just happenstance."

What this means for someone who hates risk:
- **You cannot remove the X factor.** Every business is exposed to it, which is why risk-averse people find founding so stressful. Levitt himself lost most of his savings in the August 1937 market crash, falling from $10,000 to $1,000, just before opening. He borrowed against his life insurance and went ahead anyway. Ask yourself whether you could have done that.
- **You can limit how much it can cost you.** With $2,000 and a paycheck, an X factor can't bankrupt you if you don't sign leases, take on loans or quit your job. Your situation is fragile in capital, but strong in survivability.
- **$2,000 is very little capital.** Undercapitalization is the most common cause of failure on Levitt's list. It is enough to *test* an idea, not to *open* a business that needs premises, stock or staff. Levitt also lists "an inclination to gamble" as a cause of failure, so don't borrow heavily to make up the gap.
- **Bad happenstance hurts least when you have a cushion.** Good happenstance helps most when you are already in the game. A small, low-cost start keeps you in the game without betting your living.

## Your real strength: loving people

The quiz may mark it down, but Levitt's own cases show its value:
- **Ed Mitchell** of Mitchells of Westport built a clothing business on the owner's personal warmth and family touch. Levitt admits he couldn't recreate it across 81 stores.
- **The Custom Shop** was, in Levitt's words, "a service business": "If we don't improve the image of our customer, he goes elsewhere."
- **The Silver Palate** grew with no ad budget through publicity and goodwill, holding luncheons for food editors and giving recipes away.

Liking people makes service businesses, sales, word of mouth and hiring easier. It also helps with a weakness Levitt names as a common cause of failure: "failure to attract and nurture managerial talent."

## How you should start

Levitt's case of **Bill Raveis** fits you well. Raveis quit Westinghouse in anger and went into real estate with no experience. Levitt asks whether he should have kept his job and explored real estate part-time first, or worked for a broker to learn. For a risk-averse person with a job and $2,000, that is the path:

1. **Keep your job.** Don't trade a salary for a bet you can't afford to lose.
2. **Answer "the one important question" before spending anything:** *Why should customers come to me instead of continuing to go where they have been going?* Write it in one sentence. "Better service" doesn't count unless something concrete is behind it. Look for an angle that comes from a need you have personally seen, as Levitt did with his skinny neck and broad shoulders, or Brodie with his aging parents.
3. **Pick something people-based and cheap to test,** in line with your strength: a service, coaching, events, a local service business, or a sales-driven product. Choose one that needs no lease, inventory or staff to start.
4. **Apply "give more without charging more."** Find a mechanism that lets you offer more than the alternatives at the same price, as Levitt's three-shirt minimum and mill-direct buying did.
5. **Run a small, measured test on evenings and weekends,** spending a few hundred dollars at most. Test and measure everything; Levitt kept an unprofitable catalogue going for years before checking it, and once he fixed it, mail sales went from $100,000 to $700,000.
6. **Watch your own drive.** After a few months, ask whether you are pulled in or just pushing through. If the idea doesn't take over your thinking, that is useful information.
7. **Get experience inside the field first,** as Levitt suggests for Raveis: work part-time or take a job in the industry you're considering.
8. **Set a rule in advance** for what you'll risk: how much money, how many months, and what results would justify going further.

**Bottom line:** on Levitt's terms you are not yet a classic founder. You dislike risk, you have little capital, and you haven't named an obsession. But you have people skills, an income and time. Levitt would tell you not to quit and open a shop. He'd tell you to find your angle first, test it cheaply on the side, and let your drive, or the lack of it, make the decision. If you decide not to go ahead, he'd say you kept your shirt.

*This is general guidance based on a 1988 book and a light-hearted quiz, not financial or legal advice. For a real plan, talk to an accountant or a free SCORE / SBA mentor.*
