# Stress-testing a used-furniture resale shop, the Levitt way

This draws on Mortimer Levitt's *How to Start Your Own Business Without Losing Your Shirt* (1988). Levitt opened The Custom Shop in 1937 with about $1,000, and fifty years later he owned 81 stores outright. His test is blunt, and a town that already has two thrift stores and a Goodwill is exactly where he would apply it hardest.

---

## 1. The question you must answer first

Levitt calls it **"the one important question"**:

> *Why should customers come to me for their goods/service/whatever, instead of continuing to go where they have been going?*

In his view most new businesses fail because they have "no substantive answer" to it, and he says not to open until you have a serious one.

For you the question has **two sides**, because a resale shop has two sets of customers:

1. **Buyers:** why would someone drive past Goodwill and the two thrift stores to buy a used sofa from you?
2. **Suppliers (sellers and donors):** why would someone bring a good dresser to you instead of dropping it at Goodwill for a tax receipt, or listing it on Facebook Marketplace?

Write the answer to each in **one sentence**. If either one reads "better quality", "nicer store" or "better service" with nothing concrete behind it, Levitt would say you don't have an answer yet.

**Note who you are really competing with.** The three stores you named are only the visible rivals. Your buyers also shop on Facebook Marketplace, Craigslist, OfferUp, estate sales and IKEA. Your sellers can use those same apps for free. Levitt's warning that "there will always be a lot of competition" applies here twice over.

**Where the thrift stores are weak.** A gap is only an angle if you can fill it at a price close to theirs. Thrift and charity stores tend to have these traits:
- Mixed stock, where furniture is a sideline to clothing and housewares.
- No delivery, or delivery for a fee and on their schedule.
- Little cleaning, repair or curation. Prices are set fast, by volume.
- No guarantee, and "as is, all sales final".
- No help with space, measurements or style.

Each of these is a candidate angle, but only if you can solve it without raising prices beyond what customers will pay.

### Answers that might pass Levitt's test

| Candidate answer | Who it serves | Does it hold up? |
|---|---|---|
| "Furniture only, cleaned and checked, with free local delivery, at thrift-store prices" | Buyers with no truck (renters, students, young families, older people) | Strong if delivery cost is built in. Delivery is the main thing Marketplace and thrift stores don't give |
| "We pick it up free and pay you cash or consignment, so you don't have to list it or haul it" | Sellers: downsizers, estates, people moving | Strong. It solves your hardest problem, which is supply |
| "Everything is guaranteed: no wobbly legs, no bedbugs, no smoke smell, 7-day return" | Buyers who fear used upholstery | Good, and cheap to deliver if your intake standards are strict |
| "Restored solid-wood pieces at the price of new flat-pack" | Buyers who would otherwise go to IKEA or Wayfair | Possible, but labor-heavy. See the product section |
| "Vintage / mid-century specialist" | Collectors and decorators | A real niche, but it competes with dealers and Etsy rather than thrift stores, and prices are higher |

Levitt's own angle was concrete and **arithmetical**: a custom shirt for $2.15 when ready-made Arrow shirts cost $1.85 and upstairs custom shirtmakers charged $6–10. Your answer needs the same kind of number behind it.

## 2. How you could "give more without charging more"

His credo: "Success in business is not for the greedy—on the contrary; success results from giving more without charging more, and the possibilities are infinite."

The key point is that Levitt did not just *offer* more. He found a **mechanism** that made it pay:
- He bought fabric direct from the mills and saved 40%.
- A three-shirt minimum covered the cost of measuring and raised each sale.
- Cash and carry meant no credit losses.
- No inventory meant tiny, cheap, street-level shops.
- An assembly line of 31 operations replaced one skilled sewer per shirt.
- Customers' deposits funded the work.

So the question for you is: **what about your setup lets you give more at the same price?** Here are mechanisms that suit used furniture.

### Mechanisms on the supply side (your "mill-direct")

- **Free pickup in exchange for a low buy price or consignment.** The seller gets convenience, and you get stock below what the thrift stores pay through donations plus sorting. This is your version of bypassing the jobber.
- **Relationships with the people who control flows of furniture:** estate-sale companies, senior-living move managers, real-estate agents staging and clearing houses, landlords and property managers turning over units, moving companies, and hotels refurbishing rooms. One relationship brings in many pieces. Levitt's case study Julian Brodie made the same move by selling to employers, the payer who reaches many customers at once, rather than to individuals one at a time.
- **Consignment.** Like Levitt's deposits, it means the customer's goods fund your stock. You pay the seller only when the piece sells, so your inventory risk falls sharply.

### Mechanisms on the operations side (your "assembly line")

- **A standard intake routine** of the same steps for every piece: inspect, clean, tighten, touch up, photograph, measure, price, list. Doing the same steps every time is how you make cleaning and checking cheap enough to include in the price.
- **Strict intake rules.** Refuse anything with upholstery stains, pests, smells or particleboard damage. Saying no is free and protects your guarantee.
- **Batched delivery runs** on fixed days and routes, so "free delivery" costs you one van-day a week, not a trip per sale.
- **Online listings of the whole floor,** with dimensions and photos. Customers can measure at home before they come, which cuts returns and wasted trips.

### Mechanisms on the pricing side

- **A clear, visible markdown schedule** (for example, 20% off after 30 days and 40% off after 60). Stock keeps moving, buyers get a reason to come back weekly, and you avoid the slow-moving inventory that kills furniture shops.
- **A bundle price, like Levitt's three-shirt minimum.** For example, a "room set" price with delivery included when someone buys three or more pieces. It raises the size of each sale and spreads the delivery cost.

**The test:** list each "more" you plan to give (delivery, cleaning, guarantee, pickup), put a cost per piece next to it, and show which mechanism pays for it. If a "more" has no mechanism, you are charging less margin, not giving more for the same price. That is how undercapitalized shops bleed out.

## 3. The complete package: which parts are most at risk

Levitt says an answer is only the beginning. You need the **complete package**: presentation, service, product, finance, timing and execution.

| Element | Risk | Why, in your case |
|---|---|---|
| **Product** (supply of good stock) | **Highest** | Used furniture is a supply-constrained business. Goodwill gets free donations and tax receipts to offer. Marketplace lets sellers keep 100%. If you can't reliably get good pieces cheaply, nothing else matters. Track one number from day one: **good pieces brought in per week, and what each cost you.** |
| **Finance** (cash and space) | **Very high** | Undercapitalization is the most common cause of failure on Levitt's list. Furniture needs a lot of floor space, plus a van or truck, insurance, a storage or workshop area, and labor for lifting and cleaning. Stock you buy outright ties up cash. Slow pieces consume rent. Consignment and pickup-for-stock reduce this risk. |
| **Execution** (operations) | **High** | Pickups, repairs, cleaning, delivery, pricing and markdowns are a lot of physical, daily work. Levitt's own lesson: "a good idea deserves constant watching." He also failed for 18 years to develop a second-in-command. If you get sick or hurt your back, who drives the van? |
| **Presentation** | Medium, and it is your easiest edge | This is where you can beat thrift stores cheaply: room-style vignettes, good lighting, clean floors, price tags with dimensions, good photos online. Levitt's walnut-and-velvet stores and striped awning made a $2 shirt feel like a custom purchase. Watch his **naming** lesson: "The Custom Shop" was too impersonal and people misremembered it. A personal, memorable name (for example your own name plus "Furniture Finds") may help. |
| **Service** | Medium | Delivery, guarantees and pickup are your differentiators, but they cost money. Each promise you make must be priced in, as shown in section 2. Levitt saw his business as "a 'make the customer look better' service", not a shirt business. Yours may be a "furnish your place this weekend without a truck" service, not a furniture business. |
| **Timing** | Medium | Resale is somewhat counter-cyclical, since people buy used when money is tight. Demand also peaks around move-in seasons, such as late summer for students and renters, and around local housing turnover. Opening just before a seasonal peak helps. |

### Competitors, imitators and X factors

- **Competitor response:** Goodwill or a thrift store could add a furniture section or delivery. Levitt saw more than 25 imitators, including three billion-dollar conglomerates, fail against him because his mechanism was structural. Ask whether yours is: a supply network of estate-sale and move-manager relationships is hard to copy fast, while "nicer displays" is easy.
- **Marketplace apps:** the permanent free alternative for both your buyers and your sellers. Your service has to be worth more than "free, but haul it yourself".
- **X factors**, Levitt's "single, totally unexpected situation that no research can uncover." For you that could be a lease that isn't renewed, a bedbug outbreak traced to one piece (which could wreck your reputation; a reason for strict intake), a new big-box discount store, a local housing slump that cuts move-outs, or a zoning change. You can't predict these, but you can survive them by keeping fixed costs low.

### Advertising: be careful

Levitt's own **$84 to bring in one customer** from a *New York Times* ad, on a $200 sale earning $20 profit, is a warning. Test every channel small and measure it, as he finally did with his catalogue (cutting it in half and putting the savings into a 10% reorder discount took mail sales from $100,000 to $700,000). For a furniture shop, posting each piece online, publicity and word of mouth may beat paid ads, as with The Silver Palate's luncheons for food editors.

## 4. Closest case study: Steve Israel, Great American Salvage

Steve Israel built a business rescuing architectural artifacts from buildings being demolished, with a Vermont warehouse plus stores in New York and elsewhere. His lesson is to **see value in what others throw away**. Levitt called him "not at all a businessman", a romantic, yet he did extraordinarily well. The lesson for you: his edge was a **source of supply** others ignored, plus a **specialty** (architectural pieces, not general junk). Ask what your equivalent is: a supply source the thrift stores don't reach, and a specialty they don't curate.

**Stew Leonard** is a useful second example. He had only 600 items against a supermarket's 14,000, but presented them as an experience. A focused range, well shown, can beat a bigger mixed one.

---

## 5. Candid verdict and next steps

As described, "a used-furniture resale shop" does not yet answer the one important question. A town with three secondhand stores already has somewhere people go. It becomes worth doing only if you can fill a gap they leave, and that gap is most likely **delivery, condition and a guarantee for buyers, plus free pickup for sellers**, backed by a supply network.

Levitt would also note that a shop like this can easily become what he called "an alternative way of making a living": you are the boss, but you work much harder for an income no better than middle management. That can be a fine choice, but make it knowingly.

**Before signing a lease:**
1. **Write your one-sentence answer** for buyers and another for sellers.
2. **Test supply first.** For 4–6 weeks, run pickups out of a garage or storage unit and sell online and at a weekend pop-up. Can you get, say, 15–20 good pieces a week at a cost that leaves margin? (Set your own target from your numbers.)
3. **Cost every "more."** Put a per-piece cost next to delivery, cleaning, the guarantee and pickup, and show the mechanism that pays for each.
4. **Visit the competition as a customer and as a seller.** Try to donate or sell to each of the three stores and note what they refuse, how they price and what they don't offer.
5. **Build a cash cushion.** Budget for rent, the van, insurance and several slow months. Undercapitalization is the failure Levitt saw most often.
6. **Plan for a second person early.** Furniture is two-person lifting work, and Levitt's long failure to find a deputy is a warning.

If after this the numbers don't work, Levitt would say you kept your shirt, and he would congratulate you for it.

*This is general business guidance based on a 1988 book, not financial or legal advice. Check local zoning, resale and secondhand-dealer licensing, upholstery or bedding rules, sales tax and insurance with your local authorities, an accountant or your local SBA/SCORE office.*
