# Stress-testing a used-furniture resale shop, Mortimer Levitt style

Mortimer Levitt founded The Custom Shop, a chain of made-to-measure shirt stores, and wrote *How to Start Your Own Business Without Losing Your Shirt*. His view is practical. Most new businesses fail because the founder never answers one plain question, and because the whole offer (product, price, location, service, store, people) has a weak link somewhere. Here is your idea run through that lens. I'm working from memory of the book's main ideas, not quoting it word for word.

---

## 1. The question you must answer

> **"Why would a customer buy from me instead of from the competition?"**

Levitt pushes this hard. "There's demand for used furniture" is not an answer. Your town already has two thrift stores and a Goodwill, so demand is already being met. You need a concrete reason someone drives past Goodwill to come to you.

Things that do **not** count as an answer:
- "Better prices." Goodwill gets its stock donated and pays volunteer-level labor costs. You will lose a price war.
- "Nicer people." That's welcome, but it's a tie-breaker, not a reason to switch stores.
- "More stuff." Thrift stores already have volume. Volume is their model.

Answers that could work, if they're true in your town:
- **Curated and checked.** Every piece is cleaned, sturdy, free of pests and smells, and repaired where needed. Thrift furniture is a gamble. Yours is not.
- **Furniture only, done well.** You'd be the furniture specialist, with real sofas, dining sets and dressers, measured and shown in room settings. The thrift stores are general stores where furniture is an afterthought.
- **Delivery and pickup.** Thrift stores rarely deliver. Many buyers have no truck. That alone can decide the sale.
- **A different supply source.** Estate sales, downsizing seniors, moving families, hotel and office clear-outs. That gets you better-quality stock than donation bins.
- **Consignment.** Sellers get paid, so better pieces come to you instead of being donated to Goodwill.

**Test:** write your answer in one sentence. Then ask 20 people in town who have bought used furniture in the last two years whether that sentence would make them switch. If fewer than about a quarter say yes without hesitating, you don't have an answer yet.

## 2. "Give more without charging more"

Levitt's point is to beat competitors on value, not price. You give the customer more at a similar price, and you pick extras that cost you little but matter a lot to them. For your shop:

| Give more | Cost to you | Value to the customer |
|---|---|---|
| **Free local delivery** above a set amount (say $150) | Low with a used van and one helper on fixed delivery days | High. It removes the biggest barrier to buying a large item |
| **Cleaned, sanitized and inspected tag** ("checked for bedbugs, wobble-tested, odor-free") | Minutes of labor per piece | High. It removes the fear that comes with thrift furniture |
| **7-day "fits your space" exchange** | Low, since few people use it, and you can resell the returns | High. It takes the risk out of buying |
| **Measurements on every tag and online** | Almost nothing | Saves a trip and a mistake |
| **Hold service** (48 hours with a small deposit) | Almost nothing | Lets couples decide together |
| **"Want list"** where you text people when a matching item comes in | Low | Turns one-time buyers into repeat buyers, and helps your stock sell faster |
| **Free pickup from sellers/consignors** | Shares the same van and route | Gets you the best stock before Goodwill does |
| **Small touch-up work** (new knobs, felt pads, minor refinishing) | Low materials cost | Pieces look "ready to use" instead of "project" |

Price these near the upper end of thrift pricing, well below new furniture. Don't undercut Goodwill. Beat it on certainty, convenience and quality.

## 3. The complete package: which parts are most at risk

Levitt treats the business as a package that's only as strong as its weakest part. Ranked from most to least at risk for you:

### 1. Supply/merchandise (highest risk)
This is the real business. The thrift stores get free donated stock. You have to buy, take on consignment, or pick up good furniture steadily, at about 30–40% of your selling price or less. If supply is thin, the showroom looks empty and people stop coming back.
- **Stress test:** before you sign a lease, try to get 40–60 good pieces in 60 days through estate sales, Facebook Marketplace, movers, realtors and senior-living communities. Track what each piece cost you. If you can't, stop here.

### 2. Location and rent
Furniture needs floor space, and floor space costs money. A cheap spot on the edge of town needs a destination reason (see section 1). A visible spot costs more per square foot.
- **Stress test:** work out the sales per square foot you need to cover rent. Compare it with what you think you can sell. Look at cheap industrial or flex space with easy truck loading, and use online listings to bring people in.

### 3. Price/margin
You sit between free-donation thrift and new retail. Your margin has to pay for buying stock, cleaning, delivery, storage and markdowns.
- **Stress test:** build a per-piece model. Buy at $X, add about $15–$40 in cleaning, repair and handling, sell at $Y, and assume 20–30% of pieces go to markdown. Check that what's left covers rent and your pay.

### 4. Logistics (delivery, pickup, storage)
This is your main difference from the thrift stores, and also where costs and injuries hide. You need a van, a second person for heavy items, insurance, and a backroom for stock waiting for cleaning.

### 5. Store presentation
If your store looks like a thrift store, you'll be judged on thrift-store prices. Room settings, good lighting, clean floors and clear tags are what justify charging more.

### 6. Service/people
This part is lower risk if you run the store yourself. It becomes a risk once you hire, because the "checked and guaranteed" promise depends on someone actually doing the checking.

### 7. Marketing
This is the cheapest part to fix. Most used-furniture buying starts online, so post every piece on Facebook Marketplace, Instagram and your own simple site, with measurements. Use "new arrivals" posts to drive weekly visits.

## 4. A low-risk way to test it (Levitt's "don't lose your shirt" approach)

1. **Start before the store exists.** Run it as an online and pop-up business from a garage or storage unit for 3–6 months, selling through Marketplace with delivery.
2. **Track three numbers:** cost per piece you get, days to sell, and gross margin per piece.
3. **Open the shop only if** supply is steady, pieces sell in under 30 days on average, and margin covers a projected rent.
4. **Keep your fixed costs small.** Take a short lease, buy a used van, and work there yourself instead of hiring.
5. **Have enough money behind you.** Plan to cover 6–12 months of costs before the shop pays its way.

## Bottom line
The town doesn't need a fourth thrift store. It might pay for **a furniture-only shop where every piece is checked and delivery is easy**, with better stock than donation bins provide. Your biggest risk is getting that stock. Prove you can get it before you lease anything.
