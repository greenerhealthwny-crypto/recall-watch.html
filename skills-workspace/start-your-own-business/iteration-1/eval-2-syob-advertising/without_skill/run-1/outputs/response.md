# Mortimer Levitt on Advertising, Direct Mail and His Own Mistakes

## First, a caveat

I'm answering from memory, without the book in front of me. Mortimer Levitt (1907–2005) founded **The Custom Shop, Shirtmakers** in New York in 1937. He wrote *How to Start Your Own Business Without Losing Your Shirt: Secrets of the Artful Entrepreneur* (1988). His other books include *The Executive Look* and *Class: What It Is and How to Acquire It*.

I know the broad outline of his story well. I don't trust my memory of the book's chapter-level details: exact figures, specific ad campaigns, mailing response rates, or a numbered list of his admitted mistakes. I won't invent quotes or statistics. If you're making real decisions from his book, check the passages below against a copy. Most libraries have it, and used copies are cheap.

## What I'm reasonably confident about

**The business model was the marketing.** Levitt's main insight was positioning. He offered made-to-measure shirts at prices close to good ready-made shirts, which made "custom" affordable for ordinary professional men. His ads pushed that one value proposition hard: custom fit and quality without the luxury price. The lesson is that advertising works best when it carries a clear, specific, believable offer. It cannot make up for an offer that isn't clear.

**He started small and grew from cash flow.** The Custom Shop began on very little capital and grew into a chain of stores. Levitt stressed staying solvent: don't spend money you don't have, and don't let growth outrun your finances. His title, "without losing your shirt," is the thesis.

**He treated marketing as something to measure.** His general view, shared by most direct-response retailers of his era, was that every advertising dollar should be tracked against sales. He thought you should keep running what produces customers and drop what doesn't, however good it looks. He was skeptical of "image" spending that couldn't be tied to results.

**Customer lists and repeat business mattered.** A made-to-measure business keeps each customer's measurements on file. That makes reordering easy and makes mailing past customers an obvious, efficient channel. The general lesson is that your existing customers are your cheapest, most responsive audience. Mailing people who already know you tends to beat trying to reach strangers.

## Where I'm less certain

I can't reliably reconstruct these from memory:

- which publications he advertised in, and which ones he concluded didn't pay
- specific direct-mail tests, response rates or costs per order
- the exact list of mistakes he admitted to. I recall the book is candid about missteps, roughly in the areas of expansion, partnerships or management, and costly marketing experiments. I'd be making things up if I listed them as his specific confessions.

If those details matter to you, the book's chapters on advertising and on what went wrong are where to look.

## How to apply this to your own small business

Whatever the exact text says, the principles above still hold up:

1. **Sharpen the offer before buying attention.** Can you state in one sentence why someone should buy from you instead of the alternative? If not, fix that first.
2. **Test small, measure everything.** Use trackable channels: unique codes, landing pages, "mention this ad," call tracking. Run a small test before committing a budget.
3. **Cut what doesn't pay, even if you like it.** Judge each channel by cost per customer acquired, compared with what a customer is worth to you over time.
4. **Build and work your customer list from day one.** Collect emails, addresses or phone numbers with permission. Today, email and SMS do what Levitt's mailings did, at a fraction of the cost.
5. **Design for repeat purchases.** Levitt's measurements on file are the model: make it effortless for a satisfied customer to buy again.
6. **Protect your cash.** Don't scale ad spend faster than the results justify, and don't sign long, expensive contracts (ads, leases, agencies) before you've proven the channel.
