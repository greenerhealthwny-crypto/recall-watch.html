# What Mortimer Levitt learned about marketing at The Custom Shop

Levitt gave Chapter 4 of *How to Start Your Own Business Without Losing Your Shirt* (1988) the title "lessons in failure". It's an unusually frank list. Two of the lessons are about promotion. Below are those two, then his other admitted mistakes, then what they suggest for marketing your own business.

*(The dollar figures are from the 1980s. The ratios matter more than the amounts.)*

---

## 1. Advertising didn't pay

- **The arithmetic:** a *New York Times* ad cost him about **$84 to bring in one new customer**. That customer's average sale was about **$200**, which earned only about **$20 in pretax profit**. Every new customer from the ad lost him roughly $64 on the first sale.
- **He tested it properly.** In 1985–86 he tried **8 different advertising approaches in 50 cities** and got the same result every time.
- **What he did instead:** he opened **50 out-of-town stores without an opening ad**.
- **What it made him wonder:** "How could it be that the whole world advertised, most of it pretending a competitive advantage that did not truly exist?" His point is that much advertising tries to make up for a missing answer to his "one important question". Customers came to him because the product was a real advantage: a custom-fitted shirt for about the price of a ready-made one. Ads didn't add much to that.

## 2. Direct mail: a simple test fixed it

- **The mistake:** for years he mailed a **12-page color catalogue**. Each mailing cost about **$80,000** and brought in about **$100,000 in sales**. That barely covered itself once the cost of making the shirts was counted. He admits he kept doing it without checking whether it worked.
- **The fix:**
  1. He cut the catalogue down to **custom shirtings only**, which **halved the cost to about $40,000**.
  2. He used the money saved to give a **10% discount on mail reorders**.
- **The result:** mail sales went **from $100,000 to $700,000**.
- **The lesson:** test, measure, and give the savings back to the customer. This is his "giving more without charging more" applied to marketing. The money spent on glossy pages went to customers as a discount, and they responded.

## The other mistakes he admitted

| # | Mistake | What happened | His lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | **A weak name** | "The Custom Shop" is impersonal. People remembered it wrongly as "The Custom *Shirt* Shop", and it sounded like an "English Tea Shoppe". He thought a personal name such as "David London, Shirtmaker" would have worked better. The striped-dome **awning trademark** is what made the stores recognizable. | Pick a name that is personal and memorable. A strong visual mark can make up for a weak name. |
| 4 | **The window ad's wording** | His first window sign said "We have devised a method whereby we can afford to custom-make shirts… at $2.15." Competitors used "devised a method" to spread rumors that the shirts weren't really custom-made. The rumor lasted even after the *Times*, the Better Business Bureau and the FTC confirmed 13 individual measurements. | Don't hint at a secret that rivals can twist against you. Say plainly what you do. |
| 5 | **No second-in-command** | From 1941 to 1959 he left the business to three men who lacked merchandising experience. It took him 18 years to find a professional (Glenn Bernbaum) and three more to find Anthony Bergamo. He still had no understudy for Bergamo and admitted the business was "not safe" without one. | Develop a capable number two early. |
| 6 | **Staff turnover** | Each year he needed about 24 new managers and assistants. To get them he had to hire 72, and only 6 lasted five years. Customers hired away his well-trained staff, and competitors lured plant managers with 50% raises. With 81 stores he couldn't keep the family warmth of an owner-run shop like Mitchells of Westport. | Expect to keep losing people. Build systems and a culture that keep good staff (he admired McDonald's systems and Friendly's family feel). |
| 7 | **Customers undid the service** | Customers ruined shirts with cheap laundering and ignored advice on color coordination. Reorders came in lower than he expected. | Your service only works if customers keep doing their part. |
| 8 | **The image seminars: his "biggest and most disappointing failure"** | He offered companies a 2½-hour image consultation for each executive, a full wardrobe plan and a 20% discount. The company paid $100, and he took a loss to win loyal customers who would spread the word. After almost ten years it was "a complete and total failure", because executives don't want to talk about how they dress. | A good idea fails if customers won't make the change in behavior it asks of them. |

He also admits a "seemingly languid example": he stepped back from day-to-day operations in 1941, just four years after opening, even though he insists elsewhere that "there is no such thing as a nine-to-five entrepreneur."

His summary: "We are in the image business, a service business. If we don't improve the image of our customer, he goes elsewhere."

---

## What this means for marketing your business

1. **Know what one customer is worth before you buy one.** Work out what a new customer earns you in profit, not sales: on the first purchase and over the time they keep buying from you. Then measure what each channel costs per new customer. Levitt's ad was paying $84 for $20 of profit. If your numbers look like that, stop or change the approach.
2. **Test small, measure, and stop what doesn't pay.** He kept the catalogue going for years before he checked it. Today that might mean a small trial on one ad platform with tracking links or discount codes, or a single local mailer with its own offer code. Run it, count the results, then decide.
3. **Put money back into the customer rather than the medium.** His best marketing move was a cheaper catalogue plus a 10% reorder discount. That could mean a returning-customer discount, a referral credit, or a better product rather than a bigger ad budget.
4. **Look after the customers you already have.** His mail campaign worked once it was aimed at reorders from existing customers. A list of past customers is usually the cheapest marketing you'll ever have.
5. **Look at free and earned attention.** In the same book, The Silver Palate had no ad budget. They held lunches for food editors and gave recipes away, and word of mouth did the rest. Levitt opened 50 stores with no opening ads, relying on prime street locations, a distinctive awning and a strong offer.
6. **Get the name and the message right from the start.** Choose a name people remember correctly. Make your claim plain and easy to check, so competitors can't turn it into a rumor.
7. **Above all, have a real answer to the one important question:** "Why should customers come to me… instead of continuing to go where they have been going?" Levitt's view was that advertising often makes up for an advantage the business doesn't really have. If your angle is strong, your marketing can be cheaper and simpler. If it isn't, no amount of ad spend will fix that.

**A caveat:** Levitt's results came from one business, a high-margin custom-shirt service, in the 1980s. Online ads with tight targeting and tracking work differently from a 1980s newspaper ad, and for some businesses paid ads do pay. Take his method rather than his conclusion: work out your cost per customer and your profit per customer, test before you commit, and keep only what pays.
