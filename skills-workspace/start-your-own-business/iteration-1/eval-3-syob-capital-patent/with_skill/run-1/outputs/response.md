# Funding and protecting your kitchen gadget: what Levitt's book says, and what has changed

Mortimer Levitt's *How to Start Your Own Business Without Losing Your Shirt* came out in **1988**. Chapter 21 covers raising capital and protecting an idea, and his advice on approach still holds. **Most of his dollar figures, loan limits and patent, copyright and trademark rules are out of date.** I've marked each one below. Items marked **(verify)** are current general information, not legal or financial advice. Check them with sba.gov, uspto.gov, a patent attorney or agent, and an accountant before you rely on them.

---

## Step zero: Levitt's "one important question"

Before money or patents, Levitt would make you answer one question in a sentence:

> *Why should customers buy my gadget instead of what they already use?*

He believed most new businesses fail because they have no real answer to it. "Better quality" or "more convenient" isn't enough. Name the specific job it does that nothing on the shelf does, ideally while **giving more without charging more**. His own angle was a custom-fitted shirt for roughly the price of a ready-made one. That sentence matters for both halves of your question. Investors will want to hear it in the first five minutes, and it often points to the feature worth patenting.

He also expected competition. More than 25 businesses copied The Custom Shop, including three billion-dollar conglomerates. Kitchen gadgets are easy to copy, so plan for imitators whatever protection you get.

## Part 1: Where to look for start-up money

### What the book recommends

| Source | Levitt's view (1988) | What's changed (verify) |
|---|---|---|
| **Wealthy individuals ("angels")** | **His first choice.** Ask your banker, lawyer or accountant for introductions. As an investor himself, he looked for a founder **risking his own money** (a "determining factor"), an angle he liked, a serious person, and a founder who would run the business actively. | Organized angel groups and networks are now common, and so are local pitch events. His checklist is still what angels look for. |
| **Venture capitalists** | Found through VC association directories. They want companies likely to go public or be bought, with **40–60% annual returns over 5–7 years**. | Return expectations and fund structures have changed. VC rarely funds a single consumer gadget unless it could become a large brand or platform. For most kitchen products it's a poor fit. |
| **SBA-guaranteed loans** | Private lenders make the loans and the SBA guarantees **up to 90%, with a maximum guarantee of $500,000**. Interest was capped at about 2¼–2¾ points over prime, and you had to be turned down for a conventional loan first. | **Out of date.** Today's programs are 7(a) (loans up to about **$5 million**), 504 and microloans, with different guarantee percentages and rate rules. Check sba.gov and talk to an SBA-participating lender. |
| **SBICs / MESBICs** | Privately owned investment companies licensed by the SBA. Investments ran from under $100k to over $1M, with management help. | SBICs still exist but mostly fund established firms. **MESBICs were renamed SSBICs and later folded into the SBIC program.** |
| **Commercial banks** | Want security and guarantees, at prime plus 1–4 points. | Still true in spirit: expect collateral and a **personal guarantee**. Rates are entirely different now. |
| **Business development corporations** | State-chartered private lenders offering flexible packages, often SBA-guaranteed. | Varies widely by state. |
| **State venture funds** | More than 20 states had start-up programs. Contact your state economic development office. | Many states now run innovation funds and grants. If your gadget involves real engineering, federal **SBIR/STTR** grants may also apply (verify eligibility). |
| **Public or private stock offerings** | Need experienced securities lawyers for federal law and state "blue-sky" laws. | New options exist: **equity crowdfunding (Reg CF)** and **Reg A+ / Reg D** exemptions. Get a securities lawyer. |

### The business plan, Levitt's way

- **Write it yourself.** He said investors can tell when a plan was written by outsiders, and that canned computer projections put them off.
- **Lead with the executive summary.** He cites Joseph Mancuso's point that investors spend about **five minutes** screening a plan.
- His suggested reading (Mancuso's *How to Write a Winning Business Plan*; Rich and Gumpert's *Business Plans That Win $$$*) is dated. The free SBA "Management Aids" he mentions have been replaced by SBA online resources and local SCORE and Small Business Development Center (SBDC) mentors (verify).

### Options the book couldn't mention

These are not in the book, but they matter for a kitchen gadget (verify):
- **Reward-based crowdfunding** (Kickstarter, Indiegogo). It lets you pre-sell units and prove demand before you pay for tooling. Kitchen products are a common category there. It works on Levitt's own principle: test before you spend.
- **Licensing the invention** to an established housewares company instead of building a company. You'd get royalties without inventory risk. Levitt liked businesses with no inventory risk, so it's worth weighing.
- **Retail and TV-shopping pitch programs and product-sourcing marketplaces.** These are newer routes to market that can bring a purchase order before you raise money.

### Levitt's warnings that still apply

- **Undercapitalization** was the most common cause of failure he saw. Estimate tooling, minimum order quantities, packaging, liability insurance, patent costs and a year of living expenses, then add a margin.
- **Put in your own money.** It's what he wanted to see as an investor.
- **Keep control.** He turned down a partner who demanded half the business for $5,000 ("It's my idea, and it's my business"), and he believed that refusal saved him $50 million. Be careful about how much equity you give away early.
- **Don't gamble.** He listed an "inclination to gamble" among the causes of failure.

## Part 2: How to protect the idea

### Levitt's advice that still holds

1. **Research what's already out there.** He wanted a "fair amount of research" to confirm your angle is unique. For a gadget, that means searching existing products and patents. Google Patents and the USPTO's Patent Public Search are free (verify).
2. **Get the product evaluated.** He mentions an independent evaluation center, the Wisconsin Innovation Service Center, which did a report for about $100. The center and the price are 1988 details. Look for current university or state inventor-assistance programs and get a candid outside opinion before you spend heavily.
3. **Assume nothing is confidential.** Keep a **dated log** of how you developed the invention and talk to a lawyer early. Today the usual first step is an **NDA** before showing it to manufacturers or investors, though many investors won't sign one.
4. **Beware "invention brokers"** who work on contingency or ask for fees up front. His warning is still very relevant. Invention-promotion firms remain a common trap. Be skeptical of anyone who promises results for money paid in advance (the USPTO publishes complaints about such firms; verify).
5. **Hire a registered patent attorney or agent.** He said that drafting the application yourself and having a professional refine it cuts fees, which is still reasonable. Under current law, a professional should check that the claims are broad enough.

### The forms of protection, then and now

| Protection | What the book says (1988) | Current position (verify) |
|---|---|---|
| **Utility patent** (how the gadget *works*) | For a new and useful machine, article or improvement. Refused if obvious to a person skilled in the field. The hardest and costliest to get. Filing fees **under $300**, maintenance fees **totaling $1,400**, a search **about $300+**, legal fees **$2,500–3,500+**. | **The fees are badly out of date.** Government fees are much higher now, with **small- and micro-entity discounts**. A professionally drafted utility application typically costs many thousands of dollars in total. The term is about **20 years from filing**. The rules on obviousness still apply. Check uspto.gov's fee schedule. |
| **Provisional application** | *Not in the book.* | A lower-cost filing that gives you a priority date and lets you use "patent pending" for **12 months**, during which you must file the full application. It's often the sensible first move for an inventor still testing the market. |
| **Design patent** (how it *looks*) | Easier and cheaper, but "may be of little value" because a slight change in design avoids infringement. | His caution about copycats is still fair. The term is now **15 years from grant** for applications filed since May 2015. For a kitchen product with a distinctive shape, it can be a useful extra layer alongside a utility patent. |
| **Trademark** (your brand name or logo) | Do a clearance search before using a name. PTO filing fee **$175**. Protection for **20 years**, renewable every 20. | **Out of date.** Registrations are now **renewed every 10 years** (with a maintenance filing between the 5th and 6th year), and the fees are different. File online through the USPTO. Your brand may end up being your most durable protection once a patent is designed around. |
| **Copyright** (manuals, packaging art, recipes booklet text, website) | Life of the author **plus 50 years**; works for hire **75 years** from publication or 100 from creation. | **Out of date:** now life **plus 70**, and works for hire **95 years from publication or 120 from creation**, whichever is shorter. Copyright doesn't protect how the gadget functions. |

### Book advice that is now wrong or obsolete

- **"Keep witnessed notebooks to prove you invented it first."** In 1988 the US decided patents on **first to invent**, so witnessed daily notes and signed invention disclosures were real evidence. **Since March 2013 the US has been first-inventor-to-file**, so the *filing date* is what counts. Notebooks are still good practice, but they won't beat someone who filed before you. **File early**, at least a provisional.
- **The PTO Disclosure Document Program.** Levitt mentions it as a way to record a disclosure for two years. **It was discontinued in 2007.** A provisional application replaces it.
- **Public disclosure.** The book doesn't stress this enough for today. Showing, selling or crowdfunding your gadget before filing can cost you patent rights. The US gives a limited one-year grace period for your own disclosures, but **many other countries require absolute novelty**, so disclosing first can bar a patent abroad. Talk to a patent professional **before** a Kickstarter launch, a trade show or a public demo.
- **"Patent Pending."** Levitt correctly says it deters copiers but gives no enforceable rights until a patent issues. That's still true.
- **His referral services.** The AIPLA's free half-hour Inventor Consultation Service and MacCracken's *Handbook for Inventors* are period references. Look instead at the USPTO's **Pro Bono Program**, the **Patent and Trademark Resource Centers**, and the USPTO's inventor and entrepreneur resources (verify).

### A practical point in Levitt's spirit

A patent is a license to sue, and lawsuits are expensive. In the book, Gene Ballin held several patents and still had ventures hurt by happenstance; Levitt's lesson from him was resilience, not legal protection. Levitt's own defense against copycats was **execution**: five months of staff training, a full package, and better value than his imitators could match. For a gadget, that means:
- getting to market fast;
- building a brand (a trademark) customers ask for by name;
- keeping costs low enough that copies can't undercut you much;
- improving the product before the knock-offs catch up.

## Business structure

The book's tax figures are out of date, but this matters as soon as you take money.

- **Levitt (1988):** form a corporation to protect your home and savings from business creditors. He favored an **S corporation** under the tax law of the time, and he cites a combined C-corporation tax of about 58% in New York in 1985.
- **Now (verify):** the federal C-corporation rate has been a **flat 21% since 2018**, and the **LLC** is the most common small-business form. Product-liability risk is real for kitchen gadgets (blades, heat, food contact), so liability protection and **product-liability insurance** matter. If you plan to raise money from VCs, they usually want a C corporation. Decide with an accountant and a lawyer.

## Suggested next steps

1. Write your one-sentence answer to Levitt's question, and test it on people who cook, not on friends being kind.
2. Do a free patent and product search, then book a consultation with a registered patent attorney or agent **before any public disclosure**. Ask whether a provisional filing makes sense now.
3. Build a rough budget covering prototype, tooling, a first production run, IP, insurance and your living costs. That number decides which funding sources fit.
4. Decide whether you want to **build a company** (angels, crowdfunding, SBA loan) or **license the invention** (royalties, far less capital).
5. Write the plan yourself, executive summary first, and show investors that you're putting in your own money.

Levitt's own caution: four out of five new businesses fail, and some readers "keep their shirts" by deciding not to start. He didn't mean that to discourage you. He meant you should go in with a real angle, enough capital and obsessive commitment.

*This is general information based on a 1988 book, not legal or financial advice. Patent, SBA and tax rules change. Confirm the specifics with the USPTO, the SBA, a registered patent practitioner and an accountant.*
