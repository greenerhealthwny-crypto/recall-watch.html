## 1. Where to look for start-up money

Classic small-business guides usually rank funding sources from closest to you to furthest away. That ranking still holds:

1. **Your own savings ("bootstrapping").** This is the usual first step. Lenders and investors expect you to put your own money in, because it shows commitment. A gadget can often be prototyped cheaply: 3D printing, a small run of parts, a handmade batch.
2. **Friends and family.** This is common, but put it in writing. Say whether each amount is a loan or a stake in the company, what the repayment terms are, and what happens if the business fails.
3. **Banks and loans.**
   - Conventional bank loans usually require collateral and a track record, which is hard for a new product company.
   - Older guides tend to point to the U.S. **Small Business Administration (SBA)**. The SBA mostly guarantees loans made by banks; it seldom lends directly. Today the main programs are 7(a) loans, 504 loans and SBA Microloans (up to $50,000).
   - **Community lenders (CDFIs)** and credit unions are often friendlier to first-time founders.
4. **Suppliers and customers.**
   - Ask manufacturers for trade credit (net-30 or net-60 payment terms).
   - Take pre-orders, or deposits from retailers, before you pay for production.
5. **Private investors.**
   - **Angel investors** and local angel networks.
   - **Venture capital** is usually a poor fit for a single consumer gadget, because VCs want very large, scalable markets.
   - If you give up equity, get a lawyer to review the terms.
6. **Licensing instead of manufacturing.** Many kitchen-gadget inventors never build the company at all. They license the design to an established housewares brand for a royalty, typically a low single-digit to mid-single-digit percentage of sales. That needs little capital and is worth weighing against starting a company.
7. **Grants and competitions.**
   - Pure product businesses rarely qualify for grants.
   - University and local pitch competitions, inventor clubs and state economic-development programs sometimes give small awards or free help.
   - SBDCs (Small Business Development Centers) and SCORE give free advice on financing.

### Newer funding options an older book may not cover
- **Rewards crowdfunding (Kickstarter, Indiegogo).** This suits consumer kitchen products well. It raises production money and tests demand at the same time.
  - Plan carefully for manufacturing costs, shipping costs and delays. Failed fulfilment is the classic crowdfunding failure.
  - A public campaign also discloses your invention publicly, which starts patent clocks (see below).
- **Equity crowdfunding (Regulation Crowdfunding, legal in the U.S. since 2016).** This lets ordinary people invest in your company through registered portals such as Wefunder or StartEngine.
- **Amazon and online-first launches.** Selling direct online lowers the capital you need compared with the old route of getting into retail stores first.
- **Revenue-based financing and online lenders.** These are faster than banks but often more expensive. Read the true annual cost before you sign.
- **SAFEs and convertible notes.** These are now the standard paperwork for early angel money. Older books describe direct sales of shares instead.

## 2. How to protect the idea

### Know the basic tools
- **Utility patent.** Protects how the gadget works or what it does: its mechanism or function. This is the main protection for a new kitchen tool.
- **Design patent.** Protects only how the gadget looks. Design patents are cheaper and faster than utility patents, and useful against knock-offs that copy the appearance.
- **Trademark.** Protects the brand name and logo, not the gadget itself. Often this is the most valuable protection over time for a consumer product.
- **Trade secret.** Protects things you keep confidential, such as a manufacturing process or a recipe. A physical gadget can be taken apart, so this rarely protects the product itself.
- **Copyright.** Protects packaging art, manuals and marketing copy. It does not protect the function of the product.

### Practical steps
1. **Keep dated records** of your design work: sketches, CAD files and prototypes.
2. **Search before you spend.** Run a prior-art search on Google Patents and the USPTO's patent search tools, and look at existing products on Amazon. Kitchen gadgets are a crowded field.
3. **Consider a provisional patent application.**
   - It costs relatively little and gives you "patent pending" status for 12 months while you test the market.
   - You must file the full non-provisional application within those 12 months, or the provisional lapses.
   - The provisional only helps you to the extent it describes the invention well, so don't file a thin one.
4. **File before public disclosure if you can.**
   - In the U.S., you have a one-year grace period after your own public disclosure. That includes crowdfunding, trade shows and sales.
   - Most other countries give no grace period. If you might sell abroad, file before you show the gadget publicly.
5. **Use NDAs** with manufacturers, designers and potential partners. Be aware that many large companies and investors will refuse to sign one.
6. **Hire a registered patent attorney or patent agent** for the actual filing. As rough U.S. figures, a professionally drafted utility patent often costs from several thousand dollars to over $15,000 by the time it is granted. Small-entity and micro-entity status cut USPTO fees substantially.
7. **Register the trademark** with the USPTO once you've picked a brand name and checked it's clear.
8. **Make sure the company owns the IP.** Get signed assignment agreements from yourself, any co-founders and any contractors, so that investors can see the company holds the rights.

### Be realistic
- A patent gives you the right to sue. It does not enforce itself, and litigation is expensive.
- For low-cost kitchen gadgets, many successful companies rely on a combination of measures:
  - getting to market quickly
  - a strong brand and trademark
  - retailer relationships
  - a design patent against look-alikes
  - a utility patent only where the mechanism is genuinely new and valuable.

## 3. Likely out-of-date points to check in the book

If the book was written before the dates below, check these claims carefully:

| Topic | Older advice you may see | Current position (U.S.) |
|---|---|---|
| Who gets the patent | "First to invent" wins; keep notebooks to prove the date you invented it | Since **March 16, 2013** (America Invents Act), the U.S. is **first-inventor-to-file**. Filing early matters far more than dated notebooks. |
| Utility patent term | 17 years from the date the patent is granted | **20 years from the filing date** for applications filed on or after June 8, 1995 (adjustments can apply) |
| Design patent term | 14 years | **15 years from grant** for applications filed on or after May 13, 2015 |
| Provisional applications | Not mentioned (they didn't exist before mid-1995) | Widely used, low-cost 12-month placeholder |
| "Poor man's patent" (mailing yourself a sealed description) | Sometimes suggested as protection | **Gives no patent rights.** It was always weak evidence, and it is irrelevant under first-to-file. The USPTO's old Disclosure Document Program was also ended in 2007. |
| Invention promotion firms | Sometimes recommended as a route to market | Many are scams. The FTC and USPTO warn about them. Check complaints and get their success rates in writing before paying anyone. |
| Fees | Specific dollar amounts | USPTO fees change regularly, and there is now a **micro-entity** discount of 80% off many fees. Check the current USPTO fee schedule. |
| Equity crowdfunding | Selling shares to the public needs a costly registration, or is limited to wealthy investors | **Regulation Crowdfunding (2016)** and **Reg A+** allow small public raises |
| Rewards crowdfunding, Amazon FBA, online direct-to-consumer sales | Not covered | Now major routes for funding and launching consumer gadgets |
| SBA programs | May describe direct SBA loans or programs that no longer exist | The SBA mainly **guarantees** bank loans (7(a), 504, Microloan). Program names, limits and rates change, so check sba.gov. |
| Interest rates and loan figures | Rates from when the book was written | Rates change constantly. Ignore any specific numbers in the book. |
| Searching prior art | Visit a patent depository library | Free online search: Google Patents and the USPTO's Patent Public Search |

**Bottom line:**
- Fund the gadget as cheaply and early as you can: your own money, pre-orders or crowdfunding, a small loan, then outside investors only if needed.
- Before you show it publicly, search the prior art and file at least a well-drafted provisional patent application.
- Use a trademark and a design patent to protect the brand and the product's look.
- Talk to a registered patent attorney before relying on anything the book says about patent law. That is the area most likely to have changed since it was written.

*This is general information, not legal or financial advice.*
