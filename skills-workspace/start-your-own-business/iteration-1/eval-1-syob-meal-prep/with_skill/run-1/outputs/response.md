# How Mortimer Levitt would test your meal-prep idea

Levitt wrote *How to Start Your Own Business Without Losing Your Shirt* (1988) partly to warn people. He reminds readers that four out of five new businesses fail. He also says that some readers will keep their shirts by deciding not to start. So he wouldn't cheer you on. He would make you answer some hard questions before you hand in your notice. Here is how he would run the evaluation.

---

## 1. Answer "the one important question" in one sentence

Everything in the book starts here:

> *Why should customers come to me for their goods/service/whatever, instead of continuing to go where they have been going?*

He believed most new businesses fail because they have **no substantive answer** to this question. He said not to open until you have studied it seriously and have a serious answer.

In your town, customers who want help with dinner already go somewhere. That includes the two meal-kit companies, and also grocery-store prepared food, takeout, delivery apps and home cooking. You have to say why someone would switch to you.

**Answers Levitt would reject:** "better quality", "fresher", "better service", "local". Every business claims these. He was scornful of advertisers "pretending a competitive advantage that did not truly exist."

**Where an answer might be:** meal kits and meal prep are not the same product. A kit still makes the customer shop-free but not cook-free: they get ingredients and a recipe and still have to cook and clean up. Ready-to-eat prepared meals remove that work. That difference *could* be the start of an angle, but only if it's concrete. For example:
- "Fully cooked, macro-counted lunches and dinners for people training at local gyms, delivered twice a week, for about what a kit costs per serving."
- "Heat-and-eat dinners for older residents or new parents, who can't or won't cook a kit."

**Test yourself:** finish this sentence without using the words quality, fresh or service. *"People will stop using [Kit A / Kit B / takeout] and buy from me because ___."* If you can't finish it, Levitt would tell you to keep your job for now.

## 2. Can you "give more without charging more"?

This is his credo: "success results from giving more without charging more, and the possibilities are infinite."

His own angle shows what he means. In 1937 he sold a custom-made shirt for **$2.15**, close to Arrow's **$1.85** ready-made. Upstairs custom shirtmakers charged **$6–10**. Generosity alone didn't make that possible. He had a **mechanism**:
- He bought fabric direct from the mills, not from jobbers, which saved about 40%.
- A three-shirt minimum cut his handling costs and raised the size of each sale.
- Cash and carry meant no credit losses.
- He used assembly-line sewing (31 operations) instead of one skilled tailor per shirt.
- Customer deposits funded production, so he carried **no inventory risk**.

So the question for you is: **what is your mechanism?** These are the meal-prep equivalents to check:
- **Order-ahead and deposits.** If customers pay before you cook, you avoid food waste and you don't need much working capital. This is like Levitt cutting nothing without a deposit.
- **Minimums or subscriptions.** For example, a 5-meal minimum or a weekly plan. Like his three-shirt rule, it spreads delivery and packaging costs over a bigger order.
- **A narrow menu.** Stew Leonard stocked about 600 items against a supermarket's 14,000. A short, rotating menu lets you buy in volume and cook in batches.
- **Buying direct.** Can you get produce and protein from local farms or wholesalers at a cost the national kit companies can't match locally?
- **Route density.** Delivering in one town means short routes, which is a cost advantage over national shippers.

Do the arithmetic the way he did it "on his fingers". Work out ingredients, packaging, kitchen time, delivery and payment fees **per meal**. Compare that with what customers pay now for a kit serving or a takeout meal. If you can only give more by charging more, or by earning less than your current salary, he would say you don't have an angle yet.

## 3. Walk through the "complete package"

Levitt said an answer to the one question is "only the beginning". You need the complete package: **presentation, service, product, finance, timing and execution.**

| Element | Questions for your business |
|---|---|
| **Presentation** | Name, packaging and brand. Levitt regretted the impersonal name "The Custom Shop". He thought a personal name such as "David London, Shirtmaker" would have been better. In a local food business, *your* name and face may be your strongest asset. |
| **Service** | He insisted he ran "a service business, not a shirt business". What is your service? It might be saving customers' evenings, hitting their nutrition goals, or reliable delivery windows. Define it and deliver it every week without fail. |
| **Product** | Is the food consistently good on day three in the fridge, after reheating? Keep to what you do best. He refused to sell socks or pajamas, and stayed with shirts for decades. |
| **Finance** | Covered in section 5 below. |
| **Timing** | Is local demand growing, for example from new housing, a new employer or gym culture? Or are people cutting back on delivery spending? |
| **Execution** | Early Custom Shop orders had late shirts, poor fit and mixed-up collars. Expect the same kind of teething problems with missed deliveries and wrong meals. Who handles cooking, delivery and customer complaints when you're one person? |

## 4. Plan for competitors, imitators and the X factor

- **Today's competitors.** Levitt warned that "there will always be a lot of competition." Before you start, find out what the two kit companies charge per serving, their delivery days, their minimums, their cancellation policies, and where reviews show customers are unhappy. Their customers' complaints show where your angle could be.
- **Imitators.** If you succeed, others will copy you, "perhaps do it even better". More than 25 rivals tried to copy The Custom Shop, including three billion-dollar conglomerates, and all of them failed. They failed because their structure couldn't match his costs. Genesco's factory, for example, also had to make its own profit. Ask yourself what would stop a local restaurant or gym from starting its own meal-prep line in six months.
- **The X factor.** This is "the single, totally unexpected situation that no research can uncover". For you it might be a national meal-delivery chain entering your town, a jump in food prices, the loss of your kitchen lease, a change in health rules, or a recession. Happenstance can also help, as when IBM built next to his Armonk land. You can't predict the X factor, but you can avoid a plan that only works if nothing goes wrong.

## 5. Check capital and be honest about your drive

**Capital.** Levitt said undercapitalization is the *most common* cause of failure. Questions to answer:
- How many months can you live with no salary while the business builds up?
- What does a licensed kitchen cost? Will you rent time in a commissary or shared kitchen, or build out your own?
- What do packaging, refrigeration, a delivery vehicle, insurance and an ordering website cost?

He also warned against an "inclination to gamble". Quitting your job before you have tested demand is close to that.

**Advertising.** One of his hardest lessons was that paid advertising didn't pay. A *New York Times* ad cost him **$84 to bring in one new customer**, and that customer's average sale earned only about $20 in profit. Don't build your plan around buying customers with ads. The Silver Palate grew on publicity, tastings and word of mouth. For you that could mean sampling at gyms, offices and farmers' markets.

**Test and measure.** When Levitt finally tested his catalogue, he cut its cost in half and passed the savings to customers as a 10% reorder discount. Mail sales went from $100,000 to $700,000. Track your real cost to win each customer and your reorder rate from the first week.

**Drive.** All seventeen of his founders shared "motivation that is above and beyond the norm. It should be obsessive." He said, "there is no such thing as a nine-to-five entrepreneur." Meal prep means shopping, cooking, packing and delivering on a fixed schedule every week. Ask yourself honestly whether you want that life for years, not just whether you like cooking.

The book's **Entrepreneurial Quotient quiz** asks things like these: would you spend your savings, would you work "as long as it takes", and would you start another business right away if this one failed? Levitt himself said not to take it too seriously. It is still useful for an honest look at yourself.

## 6. Lessons from the founders in the book

- **The Silver Palate (Rosso and Lukins).** This is the case closest to yours. Their angle came from a need they had themselves: they wanted to entertain stylishly without cooking. They built the business on publicity and generosity, giving away recipes and hosting food editors, not on paid ads. They also made products that were easy for customers to use successfully. **Lesson:** start from a specific need, and win customers through tasting and word of mouth.
- **Julian Brodie.** He sold pre-retirement counseling *to employers*, who reached many customers at once. **Lesson:** consider selling "wholesale". That could mean meal programs for local employers, gyms, clinics or senior-living communities, instead of only one household at a time.
- **Stew Leonard.** He turned his father's failing milk routes into a store with about 600 items and $80 million a year in sales. **Lesson:** a focused range plus a memorable experience can beat a broader competitor.
- **Ed Mitchell.** His family clothing store won customers through the owners' personal warmth. Levitt admitted he couldn't reproduce that across 81 stores. **Lesson:** at your size, personal presence is an advantage the national kit companies can't copy.
- **Bill Raveis.** He quit to go into real estate with no experience and did well. Even so, Levitt asks whether Raveis should have explored the business part-time first, or worked for a broker to gain experience. **Lesson:** this applies directly to you (see below).

## 7. Levitt's likely verdict: test before you quit

Levitt would be candid. As described, "a meal-prep delivery business in my town" doesn't yet answer the one important question. Two companies already deliver. Unless you can name a concrete angle and the mechanism that lets you give more without charging more, you risk running what he called "an alternative way of making a living." You would work much harder than now for an income no better than you have.

What would fit his book:
1. **Write your one-sentence answer** and test it on 20 people who use the kit services now. Ask what they dislike about them.
2. **Run a small paid pilot while you keep your job.** For example, offer pre-ordered meals with a deposit for a few weeks, cooked in a rented licensed kitchen. Track your cost per meal, your cost to win each customer, and the share of customers who reorder.
3. **Quit only when the numbers work.** That means customers are reordering, the margins cover a salary, and you have enough capital to survive slow months and an X factor.

If the pilot shows a real angle, you will be starting with evidence, not hope. If it doesn't, Levitt would congratulate you on keeping your shirt.

---

### Caveats

- **The book is from 1988.** Its views are Levitt's opinions, and its figures are from his era.
- **Today's rules for food businesses are your responsibility to check.** These include cottage-food and commercial-kitchen requirements, health permits and inspections, food-handler certification, labeling and allergen rules, sales tax and insurance. They vary by state and county. Check with your local health department and your state's small-business resources, or with the SBA (sba.gov) or SCORE.
- **Get professional advice on structure and taxes.** Talk to an accountant or lawyer about business structure (many small businesses now use an LLC), taxes, and personal liability before you launch.
- This is general guidance drawn from the book, not financial or legal advice.
