# Evaluating Your Meal-Prep Idea the Mortimer Levitt Way

**A note on sources first.** Mortimer Levitt founded The Custom Shop, a chain of made-to-measure shirt stores. He started it in 1937 with very little capital and built it into a national business. *How to Start Your Own Business Without Losing Your Shirt* (1988) is his practical, opinionated guide for would-be entrepreneurs. I'm working from memory of the book's main themes, not from the text in front of me, so I haven't quoted him directly. Take the framing below as his approach applied to your situation. Check the specific chapters if you want his exact wording.

---

## 1. "What's your edge?" Don't start a me-too business

Levitt's first question would be: **what will you do that is different from, or better than, what's already there?** The Custom Shop worked because it found a gap. Custom shirts had been a luxury. He made them affordable and easy to buy. He was skeptical of businesses that copy an existing offering and hope to win on effort alone.

Applied to you:
- Two meal-kit companies already deliver in your town. Meal kits (raw ingredients plus a recipe, the customer cooks) are **not the same product** as prepared meal-prep (ready-to-eat meals). That difference may be your opening, or it may not. You need to state it precisely.
- Write down in one sentence why a customer would choose you over them. "Local," "fresher," or "better quality" are weak unless customers can see and feel the difference. Stronger angles are ones the national players can't easily copy: a specific diet niche (athletes, post-surgery or medical diets, seniors, new parents), local sourcing people can verify, flexible same-day ordering, or a personal relationship with the cook.
- If you can't say clearly what your edge is, Levitt would tell you that you don't have a business yet. You have a job you've created for yourself, and it comes with more risk.

## 2. Know the customer and the market from the outside in

Levitt stressed understanding who buys, why they buy, and how much they will pay before you commit money.
- **Who exactly is the customer?** Busy professionals, gym-goers, families, older people living alone? Each group wants different menus, portion sizes, prices and delivery times.
- **Study the competition as a customer.** Order from both meal-kit companies. Note price per meal, packaging, delivery windows, and what annoys you. Read their reviews. Customers' complaints are a free map of the gaps.
- **Is the town big enough?** Estimate how many households could realistically order every week. Meal prep only pays off with repeat orders, so what matters is how many repeat customers you can reach, not how many people try you once.

## 3. Test small before you quit. Don't bet the shirt

The title is the thesis: **limit your downside.** Levitt grew his business carefully, opening new shops as earlier ones proved themselves rather than over-expanding. He would almost certainly advise against quitting your job before you have evidence.
- Run a **pilot while you're still employed**: a small weekly menu for friends, coworkers, a gym or an office, delivered on weekends or evenings.
- Track the numbers that matter. Do people **reorder**? What does it cost to get a customer? How many meals can you make in a shift?
- Set a clear test ("X paying repeat customers at Y price for Z weeks"). Quit only when the pilot passes it.

## 4. Do the arithmetic honestly

Levitt was a merchant at heart. He paid close attention to margins, overhead and cash. For meal prep, work out:
- **Cost per meal:** ingredients, packaging, labor (including your own time at a realistic wage), delivery (fuel, time, or a driver), payment fees and food waste.
- **Price per meal** that the market will actually pay, set against what the meal-kit companies and grocery-store prepared foods charge.
- **Fixed costs:** commercial kitchen rental (most places require a licensed kitchen for food you sell), insurance, permits, a website or ordering system, marketing.
- **Break-even:** how many meals a week before you make a living wage? Is that number realistic for your town?
- **Cash cushion:** how many months can you pay your personal and business bills with no profit? Levitt warned that running out of cash kills more businesses than a lack of ideas does. Plan for the business to take longer and cost more than you expect.

## 5. Keep overhead low and stay hands-on

He favored starting lean: no fancy premises and no large staff until revenue justifies it. For you, that means renting shared or commissary kitchen time instead of building one, keeping the menu tight to cut waste, and doing deliveries yourself at first so you learn what customers say.

## 6. Look honestly at yourself as the entrepreneur

A recurring theme in his writing is that the founder's own character and discipline matter as much as the idea: persistence, salesmanship, attention to detail, willingness to do unglamorous work.
- Do you like cooking at volume, on a schedule, every week? Meal prep is a repetitive production business, not a creative restaurant.
- Can you sell? Most of your early customers will come from you asking people directly.
- Does your household support the income risk and the hours?

## 7. Mind the legal and practical basics

Before you take money from customers, check your local food-handling licensing, cottage-food or commercial-kitchen rules, liability insurance and labeling requirements (allergens especially). Learning these after launch is exactly the kind of avoidable loss the book's title warns about.

---

## A Levitt-style checklist before you quit

1. Can I state in one sentence how I'm clearly different from the two meal-kit companies, and do potential customers agree?
2. Have I run a paid pilot, and did people **reorder**?
3. Do I know my real cost per meal and my weekly break-even volume?
4. Do I have enough savings to cover 6 to 12 months (or more) without a profit?
5. Do I have a licensed kitchen arrangement and the required permits?
6. Is my plan to grow step by step, expanding only after each step proves itself?

If you can answer yes to these, you're starting the way Levitt would recommend: from strength, with a real edge and a limited downside. If not, keep your job, keep testing, and let the evidence decide when you jump.
