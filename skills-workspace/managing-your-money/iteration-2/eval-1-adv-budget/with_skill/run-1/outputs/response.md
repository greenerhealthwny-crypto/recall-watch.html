# A Four-Step Budget and 12-Month Plan (Consumer Reports *Complete Guide to Managing Your Money*, 1989)

**Your situation:** $2,400 a month in income, $3,100 on a credit card at 24% APR, and no savings.

**Labels used below:**
- *(book)* means the advice comes from the 1989 Consumer Reports guide.
- *(std, verify)* means it is current general practice that you should confirm with an official source.
- All dollar splits are worked examples. Replace them with your own numbers from Step 1.

**One assumption to check:** I've treated $2,400 as **take-home pay** (after tax), because that is the money you actually budget. If $2,400 is your **gross** (pre-tax) income, your take-home is lower. In that case, shrink the flexible categories in Step 3 and keep the debt and savings lines as close to the plan as you can.

---

## The book's framework in brief

The guide splits budgeting into four steps *(book)*:

1. **Cash-flow analysis.** Find out where your money actually went in the past. This is not a budget yet.
2. **Set budget goals.**
3. **Allocate resources** to spending categories.
4. **Year-end review.** Total each category, compare it with the budget, confirm that income (not counting savings) is greater than spending, track progress such as debt repayment, and set next year's goals.

It adds three supporting habits *(book)*: **budget for the unexpected**, **keep good records**, and **make the budget work** (adjust it rather than abandon it).

Two of the book's rules matter most for you:
- **Debt limit:** monthly payments on all debts except a mortgage should not exceed **15% of gross income** *(book)*. On $2,400 that is a **$360 a month** ceiling. Your card is under that today, but at 24% APR it is the most expensive money in your life.
- **Order of priorities:** build an emergency cushion and get debts under control *before* investing. Don't buy stocks until debt is in check and you have adequate insurance *(book)*.

## Step 1: Cash-flow analysis (weeks 1–2)

Before you set any targets, see where the last 2–3 months of money went.

1. Pull your bank and card statements for the last 60–90 days.
2. Sort every transaction into a category:
   - **Fixed:** rent, utilities, phone, insurance, transport, minimum card payment.
   - **Variable needs:** groceries, gas, household items, medical.
   - **Wants:** dining out, subscriptions, entertainment, shopping.
   - **Irregular:** car registration, gifts, annual fees, repairs.
3. Average each category per month.
4. Compare total spending with $2,400.
   - If spending is higher than income, the card balance is probably filling the gap. Fix that first.
   - If spending is lower, you have found the surplus that pays for this plan.
5. Find the leaks. The book's banking chapters say to "recognize your value to the bank" *(book)*:
   - Avoid account fees by keeping a minimum balance or switching to a no-fee account or a credit union.
   - Never use check-cashing stores, which take a high percentage of each check *(book)*.

**Important:** stop adding to the card while you do this. Use a debit card or cash for daily spending.

---

## Step 2: Set budget goals

These are concrete, measurable 12-month goals in the book's order: cushion first, then debt, then a longer-term emergency fund.

| # | Goal | Target | By |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Starter emergency fund**, so a surprise bill doesn't go back on the card | $600 | End of month 2 |
| 2 | **Pay off the $3,100 card** (24% APR) | $0 balance | Month 12 |
| 3 | **Keep building the emergency fund** | About $1,200+ by month 12, then grow it toward 3–6 months of essential expenses *(std, verify)* | Year 2 |
| 4 | **No new card debt.** Pay any new charges in full each month | $0 carried | Ongoing |
| 5 | **Check your credit report** for errors, free *(std, verify)* | Once | Month 1 |

**Why a starter fund before attacking the debt?** You have no savings. Without a cushion, any car repair or medical bill goes straight back on the card at 24% and undoes your progress. The book's "budget for the unexpected" Close-Up *(book)* makes exactly this point.

**About the size of the emergency fund:** the book calls it "a personal matter". It quotes some experts suggesting $10,000–$15,000 and others suggesting a number of months of expenses *(book)*. In 1989 dollars that range doesn't fit your income. Use the months-of-expenses approach instead: 3–6 months of **essential** expenses is the common guide today *(std, verify)*.

## Step 3: Allocate resources (a sample monthly budget)

This uses the $2,400 take-home figure. Adjust the categories to match what you found in Step 1. The one line to protect is **$400 a month for debt plus savings**.

| Category | Monthly | % of $2,400 |
|---|---|---|
| Rent / housing (incl. renter's insurance) | $900 | 37.5% |
| Utilities + phone + internet | $180 | 7.5% |
| Groceries / household | $330 | 13.75% |
| Transportation (gas, transit, car insurance) | $250 | 10.4% |
| Health (premiums / copays / prescriptions) | $100 | 4.2% |
| Irregular-expense set-aside (gifts, repairs, annual bills) | $60 | 2.5% |
| Personal / dining / entertainment / subscriptions | $180 | 7.5% |
| **Debt payoff + savings (the plan)** | **$400** | **16.7%** |
| **Total** | **$2,400** | 100% |

**How the $400 is split over the year:**
- **Months 1–2:** $300 to savings and $100 to the card. The $100 should at least meet your minimum payment, so check your statement.
- **Months 3–11:** $340 to the card and $60 to savings.
- **Month 12:** about $302 to finish the card and the rest (about $98) to savings.

If housing or other fixed costs are higher than this sample, cut the flexible lines (personal spending and dining) before you cut the $400.

**Make the budget work** *(book)*:
- **Automate it.** Schedule the card payment and a savings transfer for the day after payday.
- **Keep the emergency fund at a separate bank** in an FDIC-insured savings or money-market deposit account. Keep it liquid, not in a CD or stocks. The book's own rule is that money you may need quickly belongs where liquidity and safety are high *(book)*.
- **Keep records** *(book)*: save statements and receipts, and log your spending weekly against the budget.

## The 12-month plan

Assumptions: 24% APR is about 2% interest a month on the balance, and nothing new is charged.

| Month | Card payment | Interest charged | Card balance after | Savings deposit | Emergency fund |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $100 | $62 | $3,062 | $300 | $300 |
| 2 | $100 | $61 | $3,023 | $300 | $600 |
| 3 | $340 | $60 | $2,744 | $60 | $660 |
| 4 | $340 | $55 | $2,459 | $60 | $720 |
| 5 | $340 | $49 | $2,168 | $60 | $780 |
| 6 | $340 | $43 | $1,871 | $60 | $840 |
| 7 | $340 | $37 | $1,569 | $60 | $900 |
| 8 | $340 | $31 | $1,260 | $60 | $960 |
| 9 | $340 | $25 | $945 | $60 | $1,020 |
| 10 | $340 | $19 | $624 | $60 | $1,080 |
| 11 | $340 | $12 | $296 | $60 | $1,140 |
| 12 | $302 | $6 | **$0** | $98 | **≈ $1,238** |

**Result after 12 months:** the card is paid off, total interest is about **$460**, and you have about **$1,240** in emergency savings. From month 13, the full $400 a month goes to savings.

**Why this beats paying the minimum:**

| Monthly payment | Months to pay off | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| $100 | About 49 | About $1,790 |
| $200 | About 19 | About $650 |
| $300 | About 12 | About $410 |
| $360 (your 15% ceiling) | About 10 | About $340 |

**Milestones:**
- **Month 1:** finish the cash-flow analysis, set up the automatic transfers, open the separate savings account, and pull your free credit reports.
- **Month 2:** the $600 starter fund is in place. Raise the card payment to $340.
- **Month 3:** call the card issuer and ask for a lower APR. Expect to be paid for your business *(book)*. A credit union card may also be cheaper *(book)*.
- **Month 6 (mid-year check):** the card should be below about $1,900. If you're behind, trim wants or put a windfall (tax refund, bonus) toward the card.
- **Month 9:** the card should be below $1,000.
- **Month 12:** the card is at $0. Do the year-end review.

**Speed-ups (optional):**
- **Windfalls:** put a tax refund or extra income on the card, but only after the $600 starter fund is complete.
- **0% balance transfer** *(std, verify)*: if your credit qualifies, a 0% promotional transfer (usually with a 3–5% fee) can save most of the remaining interest. This only works if you pay the balance off within the promotional period and stop charging on the old card.
- **Credit union personal loan** *(std, verify)*: a fixed loan at a lower rate than 24% can also cut the cost.

**If something goes wrong:**
- **An unexpected bill:** use the emergency fund, not the card, then rebuild the fund before raising card payments again.
- **Income drops:** pay at least the minimum on the card and protect the starter fund. Call the issuer early about a hardship plan.
- **Debt becomes unmanageable:** a nonprofit credit counseling agency (NFCC member) can set up a debt-management plan *(std, verify)*. The book's bankruptcy chapter is not a guide to today's law (see the flags below).

## Step 4: Year-end review (month 12)

Follow the book's checklist *(book)*:

1. **Total each category** for the year and compare it with the budget. Where did you overspend or underspend?
2. **Compare with your Step 1 cash-flow figures.** How much did each category change?
3. **Confirm that income (not counting savings) was greater than spending.** If it wasn't, find out which category caused it.
4. **Graph your progress.** Chart the card balance falling from $3,100 to $0 and the emergency fund rising to about $1,240. The book recommends tracking progress such as debt repayment visually.
5. **Set next year's goals.** For example:
   - Grow the emergency fund toward 3–6 months of essential expenses *(std, verify)*.
   - Start retirement saving. The book says retirement planning should begin with your first job *(book)*. Take any employer 401(k) match first, then consider an IRA (current limits are on IRS.gov *(std, verify)*).
   - Review your insurance: health, renter's, auto liability, and disability if you depend on your paycheck *(book)*.
   - Only after all of this, invest the surplus. Until it reaches about $10,000–$20,000, the book suggests short-term CDs, Treasury bills or a low-minimum fund *(book)*. Today a low-cost index fund fills the same role *(std, verify)*.

## What in the 1989 book no longer applies

| 1989 book says | What's changed *(std, verify)* | Effect on your plan |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer-interest deductions were being phased out under the 1986 Tax Reform Act (10% deductible in 1990, then zero). | The deduction is completely gone. Credit-card interest has no tax benefit at all. | The book's advice to pay cards in full is even stronger today. |
| Emergency fund of "$10,000–$15,000" according to some experts. | In 1989 dollars this doesn't fit most incomes. The usual guide today is 3–6 months of **essential** expenses. | Start with $600, then build toward months of expenses. |
| FDIC/FSLIC insured deposits up to **$100,000**. | FSLIC no longer exists. FDIC (banks) and NCUA (credit unions) insure **$250,000** per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. | Your emergency fund is fully insured at any FDIC or NCUA institution. |
| You could see your credit file (with a fee, waived if you were denied credit in the last 30 days). The book was skeptical of paid "credit credential" services. | Free weekly reports from all three bureaus at **annualcreditreport.com**, FCRA dispute rights and free credit freezes. The skepticism about paid monitoring still holds. | Check your reports for free in month 1. Don't pay for monitoring. |
| Card fraud protections existed but were limited. | Federal law caps liability for unauthorized credit-card charges at $50, and most issuers charge $0. Debit-card liability depends on how quickly you report the loss (EFTA). | Report a lost or stolen debit card immediately. |
| Bankruptcy: Chapter 7 once every 7 years, Chapter 13 plans of about 3 years. | The **2005 BAPCPA** added a means test, mandatory credit counseling, and an 8-year wait between Chapter 7 discharges. Chapter 13 plans run 3–5 years. | With $3,100 of debt, bankruptcy isn't the tool. Use this plan or credit counseling. |
| IRA contributions of up to **$2,000 a year**. No Roth IRA. | The limits are much higher and indexed. Roth IRAs have existed since 1998, and 401(k) matches are common. Check IRS.gov. | Relevant in year 2 when you start retirement saving. |
| Investing via load funds, brokers and dividend reinvestment plans. | Low-cost index funds and ETFs, commission-free trading and much lower expense ratios are now standard. | Applies later, once you are debt-free and have an emergency fund. |
| Balance transfers, 0% promotional APRs and budgeting apps aren't covered. | They are widely available today. | Optional ways to speed up the plan (see above). |
| Financial planners: "Few people need a financial planner." | Still sound. If you do hire one, choose a fee-only **fiduciary** and check them on FINRA BrokerCheck or the SEC's IAPD. | You don't need a paid planner for this plan. |

**What still holds:** the four budgeting steps, budgeting for the unexpected, the 15%-of-gross ceiling on non-mortgage debt, emergency fund before investing, paying cards in full, and reading the fine print. These principles don't depend on 1989 figures.
