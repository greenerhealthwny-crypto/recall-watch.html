# A Four-Step Budget and 12-Month Plan ($2,400/month, $3,100 card debt at 24%)

**A note on the source first.** I don't have the text of the 1989 Consumer Reports money guide in front of me, so I can't quote its worksheets or page numbers. What follows uses the standard four-step budgeting method that guides of that era taught: (1) find out what comes in, (2) track what goes out, (3) set goals and priorities, (4) build the spending plan and check it each month. Where I describe what "the book" says, I mean the general advice those guides gave, not a quotation. Check the details against your copy.

---

## Step 1: What comes in

| Item | Monthly |
|---|---|
| Take-home pay | **$2,400** |

Use **net** (after-tax) pay. If $2,400 is your gross, redo the numbers with your actual deposit amount. If your pay is irregular, budget on your lowest typical month.

## Step 2: What goes out (track it for 30 days)

Before you assign any dollars, write down every expense for one month, or pull the last two or three months of bank and card statements. Sort them into three groups:

- **Fixed:** rent, insurance, phone, minimum debt payments, subscriptions
- **Variable needs:** groceries, utilities, gas or transit, medicine
- **Wants:** eating out, entertainment, shopping

The point of this step is to find out where the money actually goes, not where you think it goes. Most people find $100 to $300 a month they didn't know about.

## Step 3: Set goals, in order

1. **Starter emergency fund: about $600 to $1,000.** With no savings at all, any car repair or medical bill goes straight back on the card at 24%. A small cushion stops that cycle.
2. **Pay off the $3,100 card balance.** At 24% APR this costs you about **$62 a month in interest** right now. Paying it off is a guaranteed 24% return; nothing else you can safely do with the money comes close.
3. **Grow the emergency fund to three months of essential expenses** (roughly $4,500 to $5,500 at your income).
4. **Start retirement saving**, especially if an employer match is available. If there is a match, put in at least enough to get it even while paying down the card.

## Step 4: The spending plan

A sample plan for $2,400 take-home. Your own numbers from Step 2 replace these.

| Category | Amount | % |
|---|---|---|
| Housing (rent + renter's insurance) | $850 | 35% |
| Utilities, phone, internet | $170 | 7% |
| Groceries | $300 | 13% |
| Transportation (gas, insurance, transit) | $230 | 10% |
| Health (copays, prescriptions) | $60 | 3% |
| Personal / household / clothing | $90 | 4% |
| Fun money (eating out, entertainment) | $100 | 4% |
| Irregular costs fund (gifts, car maintenance, annual bills) | $50 | 2% |
| **Debt + savings ("the $400")** | **$400** | **17%** |
| **Total** | **$2,400** | **100%** |

The key line is the **$400 a month** for debt and savings. If your fixed costs are higher than shown (rent is the usual culprit), cut wants and variable categories first to protect that $400. If you truly can't reach $400, even $250 works; it just takes longer (see the end).

Check the plan once a month: compare planned vs. actual, adjust categories, and don't treat one bad month as failure.

## The 12-Month Plan

**Rules for the year:**
- Stop using the card for new purchases. Pay with debit or cash.
- Pay the card on time every month (autopay at least the minimum).
- Call the card issuer and ask for a lower rate. A good payment history sometimes gets one.

**Months 1 to 3: build the starter fund and keep paying down the card**
- $200/month to a separate savings account (ideally at a different bank so it's not easy to spend)
- $200/month to the card

**Months 4 to 10: attack the card**
- $400/month to the card
- Starter fund stays at $600 and is used only for real emergencies (refill it if you dip into it)

**Months 11 to 12: card gone, savings take over**
- The last ~$104 clears the card in month 11
- Everything left goes to the emergency fund

### Month-by-month projection (24% APR = 2% per month)

| Month | Interest | Paid to card | Card balance | Savings |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $62 | $200 | $2,962 | $200 |
| 2 | $59 | $200 | $2,821 | $400 |
| 3 | $56 | $200 | $2,678 | $600 |
| 4 | $54 | $400 | $2,331 | $600 |
| 5 | $47 | $400 | $1,978 | $600 |
| 6 | $40 | $400 | $1,617 | $600 |
| 7 | $32 | $400 | $1,250 | $600 |
| 8 | $25 | $400 | $875 | $600 |
| 9 | $17 | $400 | $492 | $600 |
| 10 | $10 | $400 | $102 | $600 |
| 11 | $2 | $104 | **$0** | $896 |
| 12 | $0 | $0 | $0 | **$1,296** |

**At the end of 12 months:** the card is paid off (in month 11), you've paid about **$344 in total interest**, and you have about **$1,300 in savings**. From month 13 on, the full $400 (plus the $62 you no longer spend on interest) can go to the three-month emergency fund, which you'd reach in roughly another 8 to 10 months, and then to retirement.

**For comparison:** paying only the minimum (often about 2% to 3% of the balance) would take many years and cost well over $2,000 in interest.

**If you can only manage $250 a month total:** use $100 savings / $150 card for three months, then $250 to the card. The card is gone in about 15 to 16 months.

**Consider a balance transfer** if your credit qualifies: a 0% intro card (typically 12 to 21 months, with a 3% to 5% transfer fee, so about $93 to $155 here) would save most of the remaining interest. Only do it if you will stop using the old card and can pay the balance off before the intro rate ends.

## What in a 1989 book no longer applies

I can't check the book line by line, but these are the areas where any personal-finance guide from 1989 is out of date:

1. **Interest-rate numbers.** Late-1980s savings accounts and CDs paid roughly 5% to 9%, and money-market funds were often near 8% to 9%. Any examples that assume your savings grow that fast are wrong for today's planning. Online high-yield savings rates move with the Fed rate; check current rates instead of the book's.
2. **Credit-card interest deductibility.** The Tax Reform Act of 1986 phased out the deduction for consumer interest, ending after 1990. Some 1989 material still mentions it as partly deductible. It isn't anymore.
3. **Credit reports and scores.** In 1989 there was no free annual credit report and FICO scores (introduced 1989) were not yet widely used by lenders or visible to consumers. Today you can get free reports from all three bureaus at AnnualCreditReport.com, and free scores from many banks and card issuers. The CARD Act of 2009 also added rules on rate increases, payment allocation, and the minimum-payment warning on statements.
4. **Retirement accounts.** The book predates the Roth IRA (1997), Roth 401(k) (2006), and today's much higher contribution limits. 401(k) plans existed but were less common, and auto-enrollment and target-date funds did not. Contribution limits from 1989 are far too low.
5. **Index funds and fees.** Low-cost index funds existed but were a niche product. Today, low-cost broad index funds and ETFs with tiny fees are the default advice for most people. Commissions on stock trades, which the book likely treats as a significant cost, are now usually zero.
6. **Tools.** Paper ledgers and checkbook registers are optional now. Banking apps, automatic transfers, and budgeting apps make Step 2 (tracking) much easier. "Pay yourself first" is now best done with an automatic transfer on payday.
7. **Dollar amounts in general.** Any sample budget, rent, food cost, or "how much you need" figure is in 1989 dollars. Prices have roughly tripled since then (about 2.5x to 3x by CPI), so multiply old figures accordingly or ignore them.
8. **Emergency fund size.** Older guides often said three to six months of expenses as a single goal. Many current advisers recommend a small starter fund first when you have high-interest debt, which is the approach used above.
9. **New risks the book couldn't cover.** Identity theft, online scams, phishing, buy-now-pay-later loans, and payday-loan apps all need attention today.

**What still holds up:** the core method. Know your income, track your spending, set priorities, write a plan, and review it. Pay off high-interest debt, keep an emergency cushion, and don't spend more than you earn. That advice hasn't aged.
