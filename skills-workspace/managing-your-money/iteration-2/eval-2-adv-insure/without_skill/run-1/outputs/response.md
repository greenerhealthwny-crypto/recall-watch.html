# Insurance for a 29-Year-Old Single Freelancer Who Rents and Owns a $5k Car

**A note on the source.** You mention "this guide," but I don't have its text, so I can't quote what it says. Below is the standard insurance advice found in consumer money guides (Consumer Reports' money books included): **insure against losses you couldn't afford to pay yourself, and self-insure the small stuff.** Where your guide's numbers differ, especially if it's an older edition, trust current law and current prices over the book.

---

## What you actually need

### 1. Health insurance (top priority)
As a freelancer, nobody gives this to you. A single hospital stay can cost tens of thousands of dollars, so this is the most important policy you can have.
- Shop on the ACA marketplace (HealthCare.gov or your state's exchange). Plans can't refuse you for pre-existing conditions, and freelance income may qualify you for premium tax credits.
- If you're healthy and have some savings, a **high-deductible plan paired with an HSA** is often a good fit. HSA money is tax-deductible, grows tax-free, and comes out tax-free for medical costs.
- Self-employed health premiums are usually deductible on your tax return.
- Avoid "short-term" plans and health-sharing ministries as your main coverage. They can exclude pre-existing conditions and cap benefits.

### 2. Disability insurance (the one most freelancers skip, and shouldn't)
Your ability to earn is your biggest asset. At 29, you're far more likely to be disabled for a long stretch than to die before retirement. Freelancers have no employer group plan and often no workers' comp.
- Look for an **individual long-term disability** policy with an "own occupation" definition if you can afford it, a 90-day waiting period (which keeps the premium down), and benefits to age 65 or 67.
- Aim to replace about 60% of your income. Expect roughly 1% to 3% of your income per year in premiums.
- Some professional associations and freelancer groups offer cheaper group disability plans.
- Your emergency fund needs to cover the waiting period, so keep three to six months of expenses saved.

### 3. Auto liability insurance (required, and worth raising)
Liability is required by law in nearly every state, and it protects you if you injure someone or damage their property. State minimums are often far too low; one serious accident can exceed them many times over.
- Carry at least **100/300/100** (bodily injury per person / per accident / property damage), if the price difference is small.
- Keep **uninsured/underinsured motorist** coverage. Many drivers carry only the minimum or nothing.

### 4. Renter's insurance (cheap and worth it)
Usually about $10 to $25 a month. It covers your belongings (laptop, camera, furniture) against theft and fire, and includes **personal liability** if someone gets hurt in your home or you cause damage to the building.
- Pick **replacement cost**, not "actual cash value."
- **Important for freelancers:** standard renter's policies often limit or exclude **business property** (like $2,500 max for business equipment) and exclude business liability. If your work gear is worth more, add a home-business endorsement or a small business policy.

### 5. Business coverage (depends on your work)
- **Professional liability / errors and omissions** if clients could sue you for mistakes or missed deadlines (consultants, designers, developers, writers). Some clients require it in the contract.
- **General liability** if you meet clients in person or work on their sites.
- Often bought together as a small Business Owner's Policy (BOP). Many freelancers pay a few hundred dollars a year.

## What's likely a waste for you

### Collision and comprehensive on a 2012 car worth $5,000 (probably drop it, or raise the deductible)
These cover damage to **your own** car. The most an insurer will pay is the car's value minus your deductible. With a $1,000 deductible, the maximum payout is about $4,000, and it shrinks every year.
- A common rule: if collision + comprehensive cost more than about **10% of the car's value per year** ($500 here), drop them.
- Drop them only if you could replace the car, or go without one for a while, from savings. If you can't, keep comprehensive (cheaper, covers theft, hail, hitting a deer) and raise deductibles to $1,000.
- If you still have a car loan, the lender requires them.

### Life insurance (probably not needed yet)
Life insurance replaces income for people who depend on you. Single, no kids, and no one relying on your income means there's usually no one to protect.
- Exception: if someone co-signed your private student loans, or you support a parent, a small **term** policy may make sense.
- **Avoid whole life or other "cash value" policies** pitched as investments. They cost many times more than term and are rarely a good deal for someone in your position.
- If you expect a partner or kids soon, cheap 20- or 30-year term at 29 is easy to buy later while you're healthy.

### Other common wastes
- **Credit life / credit disability insurance** sold with loans and credit cards. Expensive for what it pays.
- **Extended warranties and service plans** on electronics and appliances. Most failures happen in the manufacturer's warranty period, and these plans are high-profit for the seller. Self-insure with your emergency fund.
- **Rental car insurance** at the counter, if your auto policy or credit card already covers rentals. Check before you travel.
- **Accidental death / flight insurance.** Narrow coverage, already handled by good life insurance if you need it.
- **Cancer or single-disease policies.** Good health insurance already covers these.
- **Phone insurance** with high deductibles, unless your phone is very expensive and you break things often.
- **Low deductibles everywhere.** Raising deductibles on auto and renter's policies to $500 or $1,000 cuts premiums. Pay the small stuff from savings.
- **Pet, dental, and vision add-ons** only make sense if the premium is less than what you'd expect to spend. Run the numbers.

## Your priority list

| Priority | Coverage | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Health insurance (ACA plan, maybe HDHP + HSA) | Medical bills are the biggest financial risk |
| 2 | Long-term disability | Protects your income; no employer plan |
| 3 | Auto liability (100/300/100) + uninsured motorist | Legally required; lawsuits can be huge |
| 4 | Renter's insurance with liability | Cheap; covers belongings and lawsuits |
| 5 | Professional / general liability (if your work calls for it) | Client lawsuits; renter's won't cover business |
| Skip or reduce | Collision on the $5k car, life insurance, extended warranties, credit insurance | Small or no risk you can't cover yourself |

**Also:** consider an **umbrella policy** (often $150 to $300 a year for $1 million) once you have assets worth protecting. It sits on top of your auto and renter's liability.

**Bottom line:** spend your insurance money on the big, rare disasters (health, disability, liability) and keep an emergency fund for the small ones. Shop policies every year or two and get at least three quotes.

*This is general education, not personalized advice. Rules and prices vary by state.*
