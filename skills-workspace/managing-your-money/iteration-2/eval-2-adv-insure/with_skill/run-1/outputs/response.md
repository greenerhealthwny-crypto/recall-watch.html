# What Insurance You Need: 29, Single, Renting, a $5,000 Car, Freelance

This is based on the Consumer Reports *Complete Guide to Managing Your Money* (1989), Part IV, Insurance, chapters 12–16.

**Labels used below:**
- *(book)* means the advice comes from the 1989 guide.
- *(std, verify)* means it is current general practice that you should confirm with your state insurance department, HealthCare.gov or a licensed agent.

The book's dollar figures and product rules are from 1989. Use its **principles**, not its prices.

## The book's guiding principle

Insure against **catastrophe**, meaning losses you could not pay out of savings. Then **calculate your need** instead of buying what you're sold *(book)*. Before buying any coverage, ask two questions:
1. Could I cover this loss myself?
2. What does my existing coverage already pay?

Your situation changes the usual picture in two ways:

| Your situation | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Freelance** | You have no employer health plan, no group disability coverage and no workers' comp. The coverage most employees get automatically, you have to buy yourself. |
| **Single, no dependents, renting, modest car** | Several common products (life insurance, collision coverage, cash-value policies) don't protect anything you actually have at risk. |

## What you actually need

### 1. Health insurance (top priority)
- The book calls health coverage "protecting your most valuable asset" *(book)*. One hospital stay can cost more than years of premiums.
- **What the book says to look for** *(book)*: the out-of-pocket ceiling. The traditional design is 80/20 coinsurance up to a cap, after which the insurer pays 100%. That cap is what limits your worst case.
- **Where to buy today, as a freelancer** *(std, verify)*:
  - The ACA marketplace (HealthCare.gov or your state's exchange). Premium tax credits are based on your projected income, which matters if your freelance income varies. Report income changes during the year.
  - Plans can't exclude pre-existing conditions and must cover essential health benefits.
  - A **high-deductible plan with an HSA** can suit a healthy 29-year-old. HSA contributions are tax-deductible, and the money carries over from year to year.
  - Self-employed people can generally deduct their health premiums.
- **Avoid as substitutes for real coverage** *(std, verify)*: short-term health plans, "health-sharing ministries" and fixed-indemnity or discount plans. They are not ACA-compliant and can leave you exposed to exactly the catastrophe you're insuring against.

### 2. Disability income insurance (the gap people miss)
- **The book's method** *(book)*: before buying, add up the monthly benefits you'd already receive (group plan, Social Security disability, workers' comp).
- **Your tally:** you have no group plan and no workers' comp. Social Security disability is hard to qualify for and pays little. That means your existing coverage is close to $0.
- **Why it matters:** your ability to earn is your biggest asset. At 29 you have decades of earnings ahead, and an illness or injury is statistically more likely than an early death.
- **What to look for** *(std, verify)*:
  - An individual long-term disability policy replacing roughly 60% of income.
  - "Own-occupation" or a strong definition of disability.
  - A 90-day elimination period, which keeps premiums down. Your emergency fund covers the wait.
  - Benefits to age 65 or 67.
  - Non-cancelable and guaranteed renewable terms.
  - Premiums are lower while you're young and healthy.
- Freelancer associations sometimes offer group rates *(std, verify)*.

### 3. Renter's insurance
- **Separate policies exist for renters** *(book)*. Your landlord's policy covers the building, not your belongings or your liability.
- **Liability** *(book)*: this covers you if someone is hurt in your home or you accidentally cause damage, and the book's example is your dog biting someone. For a single renter, this is often the most valuable part of the policy.
- **Replacement-cost contents:** in the book's era it cost renters about 20–40% more than actual-cash-value coverage *(book)*. It is usually worth it, because actual cash value pays for your used laptop and couch after depreciation.
- **Make a household inventory** *(book Close-Up)*: a phone video of each room, saved to the cloud *(std)*.
- **Freelancer warning** *(std, verify)*:
  - Standard renter's policies often limit or exclude **business property** (computers, cameras, equipment used for paid work).
  - They also exclude **business liability**, such as a client tripping in your home office.
  - Ask for a home-business endorsement or a separate small business policy.
- **Flood and earthquake** are excluded from standard policies. Buy them separately (flood through NFIP or private insurers) if your area is at risk *(std, verify)*.
- Renter's insurance is typically inexpensive relative to what it protects *(std, verify)*.

### 4. Auto insurance: carry strong liability, and think about dropping collision
The book lists these coverages: liability (bodily injury and property damage), medical payments, no-fault/PIP, uninsured motorist, collision and comprehensive *(book)*.

**Keep and strengthen:**
- **Liability.** This protects your future income from a lawsuit. State minimums are often low. Many advisers suggest about 100/300/100 (bodily injury per person / per accident / property damage, in $ thousands) or more *(std, verify)*.
- **Uninsured / underinsured motorist.** This protects you when the other driver can't pay *(book)*.
- **PIP / medical payments**, as your state requires *(book)*.

**Probably drop: collision, and maybe comprehensive.**
- Collision pays for damage to *your* car *(book)*. On a 2012 car worth about $5,000, the most it will ever pay is the car's value minus your deductible, for example $5,000 − $1,000 = $4,000.
- **Rule of thumb** *(std, verify)*: if the yearly premium for collision and comprehensive is more than about 10% of the car's value (here about $500), or if you could replace the car from savings, drop it or raise the deductible to $1,000.
- **Exceptions:**
  - A lender or lease requires collision.
  - You have no savings to replace the car and depend on it to earn income. In that case keep it with a high deductible until your emergency fund grows.
- **Comprehensive** (theft, hail, flood, hitting a deer) is usually cheaper. Keep it if your area has high theft or weather risk.

**Freelancer check:**
- If you drive for work (deliveries, rideshare, carrying client equipment), tell your insurer. A personal policy may deny a claim that happened during business use *(std, verify)*.

**Shop around** *(book)*. Premiums for identical coverage vary widely between insurers. Get at least three quotes, and ask about raising deductibles, bundling with renter's insurance, and safe-driver or low-mileage discounts.

**Watch out for packaged policies** *(book Close-Up)*. Check each coverage line separately, so you aren't paying for extras you don't need.

### 5. Professional liability / E&O (depending on your field)
- This isn't in the 1989 book. It's current practice *(std, verify)*.
- If your work involves advice, design, code, health or wellness services, or a client's property, a mistake can lead to a lawsuit. Renter's and auto policies won't cover it.
- Many client contracts require general liability or E&O (errors and omissions) insurance. Read your contracts. A single-member LLC does not by itself protect you from your own professional errors.

### 6. Later, as your assets grow: an umbrella policy
- **The book** *(book)*: an umbrella adds $1 million or more of liability on top of auto and renter's coverage, and also covers libel and slander. It cost about $100 a year in 1989.
- **Today** *(std, verify)*: prices are higher but still modest. Insurers usually require you to carry certain underlying auto and renter's liability limits first.
- **When to buy it:** once you have real savings or a high income to protect. It isn't urgent at your stage.

## What's likely a waste of money for you

| Product | Why it's poor value here |
|---|---|
| **Life insurance (most kinds)** | The book says the people who need it are those with **dependents who rely on their income** *(book)*. Run its three-step method: (1) your death would leave survivors with only funeral and final costs; (2) subtract your assets; (3) the gap is small or zero. **Exceptions:** someone depends on you financially (a parent or partner), or someone cosigned a private student loan. In that case buy cheap **term** insurance for exactly that amount. |
| **Cash-value / whole life sold as "an investment" or "lock in rates while young"** | It combines insurance with a high-fee savings plan. The book notes how whole life keeps the cash-value buildup "in the dark" *(book)*. If you later need coverage, buy term and invest the difference separately *(std)*. |
| **Credit-life / credit-disability insurance** (offered with car loans and credit cards) | "Usually not a good buy" *(book)*. It pays the lender, not you, and costs a lot for the coverage you get. Proper term or disability coverage does the job better. |
| **Dread-disease (cancer) policies** | Poor value for the dollar, narrow coverage, and often sold with "promotional fear tactics" *(book)*. A good health plan with an out-of-pocket maximum already covers cancer. |
| **Accident-only, hospital-indemnity and similar narrow policies** *(std, verify)* | Same logic as dread-disease policies: they pay small fixed amounts for specific events. Your emergency fund and real health and disability coverage do this better. |
| **Collision coverage on the $5,000 car** (in most cases) | It pays out at most about $4,000 and costs hundreds a year. See the rule of thumb above. |
| **Rental-car counter coverage** (CDW/LDW) | Often duplicates your own auto policy or the coverage on your credit card *(std, verify)*. Check both before you travel. Note: if you drop collision, your own policy may no longer cover damage to a rental car. |
| **Travel, title and legal insurance** | Buy only in special circumstances, after checking what your existing coverage already does *(book)*. A large non-refundable trip may justify travel insurance. A one-off flight doesn't. |
| **Extended warranties, phone or device protection plans** *(std)* | Small losses you could cover yourself. They are profit centers for the seller. Paying for small repairs out of savings is cheaper over time. |
| **Several small policies instead of one adequate one** | The book's point is that one large policy beats several small ones *(book)*. Overlapping coverage means you pay twice for the same risk. |
| **Low deductibles everywhere** | Low deductibles mean you're paying to insure small, affordable losses. Build an emergency fund and choose higher deductibles on auto, renter's and health (if you have an HSA) *(book heuristic: raise deductibles to cut premiums; std)*. |

## Priority checklist

1. **Health insurance:** compare ACA marketplace plans with your income estimate, and consider an HDHP with an HSA *(std, verify)*.
2. **Disability insurance:** get quotes for an individual long-term policy *(std, verify)*.
3. **Auto insurance:** raise liability limits, keep uninsured motorist coverage, price the policy with and without collision, and get three quotes *(book)*.
4. **Renter's insurance:** choose replacement-cost contents coverage, add a home-business endorsement for your work equipment, and make a video inventory *(book; std)*.
5. **Professional liability:** check whether your contracts or your field call for it *(std, verify)*.
6. **Skip** life, cash-value, credit-life, dread-disease and narrow indemnity policies unless someone depends on your income *(book)*.
7. **Reinforce your insurance with savings:**
   - An emergency fund of 3–6 months of expenses *(std)*. Freelancers with uneven income should lean toward 6 months or more.
   - That fund is what lets you choose high deductibles and a 90-day disability waiting period.
   - For retirement, the book points self-employed people to Keogh and SEP plans *(book)*. Today a SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k) does this job, with much higher limits; check IRS.gov *(std, verify)*.
8. **Review once a year**, and whenever something changes: a partner or child, buying a home, a new car loan, or a big jump in income.
