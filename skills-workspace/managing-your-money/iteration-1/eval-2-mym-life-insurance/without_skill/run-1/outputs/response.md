# Life Insurance at 34, Married, Two Young Kids

*Note: This answer comes from general knowledge of Consumer Reports' long-standing consumer-finance advice. I'm not quoting the guide's exact text, so check specific figures against your copy.*

## 1. Figuring out how much you need

Consumer Reports' advice is to work out what your family would actually need. Rules of thumb like "7–10 times your salary" are a rough starting point at best. The question is: **if your income stopped tomorrow, how much money would your spouse and kids need, and for how long?**

A needs-based worksheet looks like this:

**Add up what your family would need:**
- **Income replacement:** the yearly after-tax income your household would lose, times the number of years it's needed. With young kids, that usually means until the youngest is through school, about 18–22 years. You don't have to replace 100%, because one less person means somewhat lower expenses. A common target is 60–80% of take-home pay.
- **Debts to pay off:** mortgage (or enough to keep making payments), car loans, credit cards, student loans.
- **Future big costs:** college for two children, and child care if a stay-at-home parent dies or if the surviving parent must pay for care they now provide.
- **Final expenses:** funeral, burial and estate settlement, often $10,000–$20,000.
- **An emergency cushion** for the adjustment period.

**Subtract what's already there:**
- Savings and investments (not counting retirement money you want kept for retirement).
- Existing life insurance, including group coverage through work. Don't over-rely on it, since it usually ends when you leave the job.
- **Social Security survivor benefits.** Your spouse and minor children may be eligible for monthly benefits, which can be substantial for a young family. Check your estimate at ssa.gov.

**The gap is roughly how much coverage to buy.** For a 34-year-old with two small children, this often comes out in the high six figures to over $1 million.

**Don't forget a stay-at-home or lower-earning spouse.** Their unpaid work, such as child care and running the household, would cost a lot to replace. They need coverage too.

**Revisit the number** after major life events: a new child, a new home, a big raise, or debts paid off.

## 2. What kind to buy: term life insurance

Consumer Reports' consistent advice for most families: **buy term life insurance.**

- **Term insurance** covers you for a set period, such as 20 or 30 years. It pays only if you die during that time and builds no cash value. That makes it far cheaper, often 10–15 times less than cash-value policies for the same death benefit. A healthy 34-year-old can typically get $1 million of 20- or 30-year coverage for a modest monthly premium.
- **Choose level-premium term** so your premium stays fixed for the whole term.
- **Match the term to the need.** A 20- to 30-year term gets your youngest child to adulthood and covers most of the mortgage. Many people would pick a 25- or 30-year term here.
- **Look for a policy that's guaranteed renewable and convertible**, which lets you switch to permanent coverage later without a medical exam if your situation changes.

**Why not whole life, universal life or variable life (cash-value policies)?**
- They cost much more, so many families end up **underinsured** because they can only afford a small death benefit.
- High commissions and fees, especially in the early years, eat into the cash value. Surrendering early often means losing money.
- The "investment" part usually returns less than you could get on your own. That's the logic behind Consumer Reports' "**buy term and invest the difference**" advice: put the premium savings into a 401(k), IRA or 529 college plan.
- Permanent insurance can make sense in narrow cases, such as estate-tax planning, a lifelong dependent or certain business needs. It rarely fits a typical young family.

**Shopping tips:**
- Compare quotes from several insurers, using online quote services or an independent agent. Prices vary a lot for the same coverage.
- Check insurers' financial-strength ratings (A.M. Best, Standard & Poor's, Moody's).
- Apply while you're young and healthy. Rates depend on age and health, and buying now locks in a low price.
- Answer the application honestly. Misstatements can lead to a denied claim.
- Don't rely only on your employer's group policy. Buy your own so coverage doesn't depend on your job.

## 3. Credit life insurance on your car loan: generally not worth it

Consumer Reports has long called credit life insurance one of the **worst deals in insurance**. It's the coverage sold with a car loan, mortgage or credit card that pays off the debt if you die.

- **It's overpriced** compared with regular term life insurance, often by a wide margin. Insurers typically pay out only a small share of the premiums in claims.
- **The payout shrinks while the price doesn't.** The benefit falls as you pay down the loan, but the premium usually stays the same.
- **The lender is the beneficiary, not your family.** The money goes only to pay off the loan. Your spouse gets no choice about how to use it.
- **Premiums are often rolled into the loan**, so you pay interest on the insurance too.
- **It's optional.** A lender can't require credit life insurance as a condition of the loan. If a dealer suggests otherwise, push back, and check your contract for add-ons you didn't ask for.
- **The same applies to credit disability and "debt protection" add-ons:** high cost, narrow benefits.

**The better approach:** include your car loan and your other debts in your needs calculation, then buy enough term life insurance to cover them. Your family gets a bigger, cheaper and more flexible benefit they control.

Possible exception: credit life can be worth considering if your health makes you uninsurable through regular underwriting, since it often requires no medical exam. Even then, compare it with guaranteed-issue or group coverage first.

---

### Bottom line
1. Estimate your family's needs (income replacement until the kids are grown, debts, college, final expenses), then subtract savings, existing coverage and Social Security survivor benefits.
2. Cover both spouses, including a stay-at-home parent.
3. Buy level-premium **term** insurance for about 20–30 years from a highly rated insurer, after comparing quotes. Invest the money you save instead of buying cash-value insurance.
4. **Say no to credit life insurance** on the car loan. Make your term policy big enough to cover that debt instead.
