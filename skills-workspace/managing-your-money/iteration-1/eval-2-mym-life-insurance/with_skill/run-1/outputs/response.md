# Life insurance for a 34-year-old parent of two: what the Consumer Reports guide says

*Source: Consumer Reports Complete Guide to Managing Your Money (Consumers Union, 1989), Chapter 13. The book's principles still hold up. Its prices, products and tax rules come from 1989, so check any numbers against current sources.*

## 1. Do you need it? Yes.

The guide says life insurance is for people whose **dependents rely on their income**. A married parent of two young children fits that exactly. If both you and your spouse earn income or do unpaid work the family depends on, like childcare, **work out a need for each of you**.

## 2. How much: the book's three-step method

The guide doesn't use a rule of thumb like "10× salary." It has you work out the actual gap.

**Step 1: Add up what your family would need if you died.**
- *Immediate costs:* funeral, probate and estate administration, any estate taxes, and any debts you'd want paid off at death.
- *Future costs:* the family's living expenses for as many years as they'd need support. With young kids, that's at least until the youngest is self-supporting, often 18 to 20 years. Include **children's education** too.

**Step 2: Subtract the resources they would already have.**
- Savings and investments
- **Social Security survivor benefits** for your spouse and the children
- **Your spouse's income**
- **Existing life insurance**, including any group policy through work

**Step 3: Buy coverage for the gap.** Whatever is left from Step 1 after Step 2 is the amount of coverage to buy.

**Worked shape (fill in your own numbers):**

| Item | Your figure |
|---|---|
| Final and immediate costs (funeral, debts, estate costs) | $ |
| Yearly living costs the family would need you to replace × years until the youngest is independent | $ |
| College or education fund for 2 children | $ |
| **Total need** | $ |
| − Savings and investments | $ |
| − Social Security survivor benefits over that period | $ |
| − Spouse's earnings available for these costs | $ |
| − Existing coverage (group plan, other policies) | $ |
| **= Coverage to buy** | $ |

Tips for filling it in:
- **Survivor benefits:** get your estimate from your *my Social Security* account at ssa.gov. Don't use 1989 figures.
- **Employer group life:** it usually ends when you leave the job, so don't count all of it as permanent coverage *(current practice, verify)*.
- **Redo the numbers** when your life changes: a new child, a new home, a big raise, or the kids growing up. The guide's year-end review habit is a good time to do it.

## 3. What kind to buy

The guide splits policies into two families:

- **Term insurance** is "pure protection." You pay for a death benefit for a set period and nothing else. It's simple and gives the most coverage per premium dollar.
- **Cash-value insurance** combines protection with a savings feature. It includes:
  - **Whole life**
  - **Universal life** (introduced in 1979). The book notes it shows you how the cash value builds up, which traditional whole life keeps "in the dark." Its charges can be front-loaded or back-loaded, so read the fine print.

The book also covers **converting** between the two types.

**What this means for you:** your need is large and temporary. It peaks now, while the kids are small, and shrinks as they grow and your savings build. That matches **low-cost term insurance** well. A level term policy that runs until your youngest is independent, often 20 to 25 years at your age, fits that window. Save and invest separately, for example for retirement, rather than through the policy. This is the common approach for families like yours, and it follows from the book's framework. The guide's own wording on how strongly to prefer term is only partly captured in my source notes, so treat the specific recommendation as standard advice, not a direct quote.

When you shop:
- Compare quotes from several insurers for the same amount and term length.
- Check that the policy is **convertible** to cash-value coverage without a new medical exam, in case your health changes.
- Ask how premiums are guaranteed.
- Check the insurer's financial strength rating, such as AM Best *(current, verify)*.

The guide also says **one large policy is better than several small ones**, since buying many small policies usually costs more per dollar of coverage.

## 4. Credit life insurance on your car loan: usually not worth it

The guide is direct: credit-life insurance **"is usually not a good buy."**

Why, briefly:
- **It protects the lender, not your family.** The payout goes straight to the lender to pay off the loan.
- **The coverage shrinks** as the loan balance goes down, but the cost is often fixed or built into the loan, so you pay interest on the premium too.
- **It's poor value per dollar** next to ordinary term life.
- **It's another small policy**, which goes against the book's advice to carry one large policy.

**Better approach:** include the car loan balance in Step 1 of your needs calculation, under debts to pay off at death, and let your term policy cover it. If the dealer or lender added credit life, it's normally optional. You can usually decline it or cancel it and get a refund of the unused premium. Ask the lender, and check your state's rules *(verify)*.

## 5. Related gaps the guide would have you check

- **Disability insurance.** At 34, you're more likely to become disabled than to die before retirement *(standard actuarial point, verify)*. The guide says to add up the benefits you already have first: an employer group plan, Social Security disability and workers' comp. Then buy only to fill the gap.
- **A will.** With minor children, this is as important as the insurance. The guide warns that dying without a will leaves your assets to be divided under state law. A will is also where you **name a guardian** for the kids.
- **Beneficiary designations.** Make sure your policies name the right people. For minor children, it's usually better to leave the money to a trust or a custodian than to name the kids directly. Ask an estate attorney *(current practice, verify)*.
- **Avoid "dread disease" policies.** The guide calls cancer-only and similar policies poor value. They're often sold with "promotional fear tactics."
