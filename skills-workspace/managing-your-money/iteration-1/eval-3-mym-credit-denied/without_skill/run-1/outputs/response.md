# Turned Down for a Credit Card: Credit Reports, Your Rights, and Marriage

> **A note on sources:** I don't have the text of the *Consumer Reports Complete Guide to Managing Your Money* in front of me, so I can't quote it or give page numbers. What follows is the standard consumer-credit guidance that Consumer Reports and similar personal-finance guides teach. It rests on federal law: the **Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)** and the **Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA)**. If you have the book, check its chapter on credit, and check current rules at consumerfinance.gov, because some details (such as how many free reports you get) have changed since older editions came out.

---

## 1. What a credit report is

A credit report is a file kept by a credit bureau. The three national bureaus are **Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion**. A report usually contains:

- **Identifying information:** your name and past names, current and past addresses, Social Security number, date of birth, and sometimes employers.
- **Your credit accounts:** each credit card, loan, and mortgage, with the date it was opened, the credit limit or loan amount, the balance, and your payment history (on time, 30/60/90 days late, charged off).
- **Public records and collections:** bankruptcies, and accounts sent to collection agencies.
- **Inquiries:** who has pulled your report. "Hard" inquiries come from credit applications. "Soft" inquiries come from things like promotional offers or checking your own report.

Lenders use the report, and usually a **credit score** calculated from it, to decide whether to approve you and on what terms. The bureaus don't approve or deny anyone. The lender does.

Errors are common. Accounts get mixed up with someone who has a similar name, debts you paid still show as unpaid, accounts are reported twice, or the account was opened by an identity thief. That's why the guides stress checking your report, and denial is the best time to do it.

## 2. What to do now that you've been denied

### Step 1: Read the "adverse action" notice
When a creditor turns you down, the law requires it to tell you:
- the **specific reasons** for the denial, or that you can ask for them within 60 days (ECOA), and
- if a credit report played a part, the **name, address, and phone number of the bureau** that supplied it, plus a statement that the bureau didn't make the decision (FCRA).

Common reasons include too little credit history, late payments, high balances compared with your limits, too many recent applications, or income that's too low or can't be verified.

### Step 2: Get your free credit report
- If a report was used against you, you can get a **free copy from that bureau within 60 days** of the denial notice.
- You can also get free reports from all three bureaus at **AnnualCreditReport.com**, the only official site for this. Free weekly reports are now available permanently.
- Older editions of personal-finance guides may say "30 days" or "once a year." The rules have become more generous since then.

### Step 3: Review it line by line
Look for:
- accounts you don't recognize (possible identity theft)
- late payments that weren't late
- debts you paid off that still show a balance
- closed accounts still shown as open, or accounts closed by the creditor that you actually closed
- negative items older than the reporting limit: most negative information drops off after **7 years**, and Chapter 7 bankruptcy after **10 years**
- wrong personal information, which can be a sign of a mixed file

### Step 4: Dispute any errors
- File a dispute with the **bureau** online, or in writing by certified mail with a return receipt. Include copies of your supporting documents, not originals.
- Also notify the **creditor** that supplied the wrong information.
- The bureau generally has **30 days** to investigate. If it can't verify the item, the item must be corrected or removed.
- If the dispute doesn't resolve the problem, you have the right to add a short **statement of explanation** (up to 100 words) to your file. You can also complain to the **Consumer Financial Protection Bureau**.
- Once corrected, you can ask the bureau to send the corrected report to anyone who received it recently.

### Step 5: If the report is accurate, fix the underlying problem
- **Pay every bill on time.** Payment history matters most.
- **Pay down revolving balances.** Try to keep balances well below your credit limits; under about 30% is the common rule of thumb.
- **Don't apply for several cards right away.** Each application adds a hard inquiry and can look like financial distress.
- **Build a thin file carefully.** A secured credit card, a credit-builder loan, or becoming an authorized user on a responsible person's account can help.
- **Ask the creditor to reconsider.** If you can explain the problem (for example, income they didn't count), call and ask, or ask whether a different card would fit you better.
- **Beware of "credit repair" companies** that promise to erase accurate negative information. They can't legally do that, and you can do everything legitimate yourself for free.

## 3. Does being married affect your credit file?

The short answer is that **your credit file stays your own.** Marriage doesn't merge your file with your spouse's, and you don't have a joint credit report or joint score. What matters is how each account is held.

| Type of account | Appears on whose report? |
|---|---|
| Account in your name only | Yours only |
| Account in your spouse's name only | Your spouse's only (unless you're an authorized user) |
| **Joint account** (both of you applied and are liable) | **Both** reports. Good and bad payment history affects you both |
| You're an **authorized user** on your spouse's card | Often appears on your report too, though you aren't legally liable |

Key points, often stressed in older guides because historically it mostly affected married women:

- **Under the ECOA, creditors can't discriminate based on marital status.** They can't deny you credit because you're married, single, divorced, or widowed. They also can't require your spouse to co-sign if you qualify on your own.
- **Make sure joint accounts are reported in both names.** Since the ECOA rules took effect in the 1970s, accounts that both spouses use or are liable for must be reportable in both names. In the past, many married women ended up with no credit history of their own because every account was in the husband's name. If that happened to you, ask the creditors to report the shared accounts under your name as well.
- **Keep some credit in your own name.** Credit in your own name gives you an independent history. That protects you if you divorce or your spouse dies, and it helps if your spouse's credit is poor.
- **Your spouse's past credit problems don't transfer to you** just because you married. Debts you take on together, though, affect both of you. In **community-property states** (such as California, Texas, and Arizona), debts taken on during the marriage may be treated as shared, even when they don't appear on your credit report.
- **If you changed your name,** check that your report correctly shows both your current and former names, so your history isn't split up or lost.

### What this means for you
1. Check whether the denial was based on **your** file. It should be, unless you applied jointly.
2. If most of your household credit is in your spouse's name, you may simply have a **thin file**. Ask to be added to joint accounts, or open a starter or secured card in your own name.
3. If you share accounts with your spouse, check that they appear on **your** report, and dispute them if they don't.
4. If your spouse's poor credit appears on your report only because of joint accounts, those accounts are affecting you. Work together to bring them up to date.

## Quick checklist
- [ ] Read the denial letter and note the reasons and the bureau named
- [ ] Get your free report from that bureau, and from all three at AnnualCreditReport.com
- [ ] Dispute any errors with both the bureau and the creditor, in writing
- [ ] Check that joint accounts appear on your report, and that your name history is correct
- [ ] Fix real problems: pay on time, lower balances, limit new applications
- [ ] Build credit in your own name if your file is thin
- [ ] Avoid paid "credit repair" schemes. Disputing errors yourself is free
