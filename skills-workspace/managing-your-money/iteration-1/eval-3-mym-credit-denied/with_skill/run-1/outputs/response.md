# Turned Down for a Credit Card: What the Book Says and What to Do Now

The *Consumer Reports Complete Guide to Managing Your Money* covers this in Chapter 8, "Credit rating." Keep in mind that the book came out in **1989**. Its advice on how to approach your credit file still holds up. The specific rights, fees and deadlines have changed since then, so I've flagged the parts you should check against current rules.

Labels used below:
- *(book)* means it comes from the 1989 text.
- *(current, verify)* means it's today's general rule. Confirm it with the CFPB, FTC or the credit bureaus.

---

## 1. What the book says about credit reports

**Your credit file doesn't show everything you pay.** The book points out that many routine bills are **never reported** to credit bureaus. That includes phone, utilities, rent and medical bills *(book)*. So you can pay every bill on time for years and still have a "thin" file that tells a lender very little. Being turned down doesn't always mean the lender saw something bad. Sometimes it saw almost nothing.

**Lenders have to answer you.** A lender must respond to a credit application within **30 days** *(book)*.

**If you're denied credit, the book says you have rights** *(book)*:
- You can **see your credit bureau file**. In 1989 the bureau waived its fee if you'd been denied credit within the previous 30 days.
- You can **review the file in person** and bring one other person with you.
- You can **dispute errors** you find.

**The book doesn't trust paid "fixes."** It's skeptical of paid "credit credential" and monitoring services and asks whether they're worth what they cost *(book)*. That advice still applies to "credit repair" companies today. They can't legally do anything you can't do yourself for free.

**The book's tools.** Chapter 8 ends with a sample consumer credit report and a **credit report checklist** *(book)*. You use them to go through every entry: personal details, accounts, payment history and inquiries.

---

## 2. What to do now: a step-by-step plan

These steps follow the book's approach, with current procedures added.

1. **Read the denial notice.** Under today's law, a lender that turns you down must send an "adverse action" notice. It says which credit bureau it used and the main reasons for the denial, and it often shows the credit score it used *(current, verify)*. Those reasons tell you what to fix.

2. **Get your credit reports from all three bureaus for free.** The book's fee waiver has been replaced by something better. You can get free reports from Equifax, Experian and TransUnion at **annualcreditreport.com**, the only official site *(current, verify)*. A denial also entitles you to a free report from the bureau the lender used if you ask within 60 days *(current, verify)*.

3. **Check each report against the book's checklist.** Look for:
   - accounts you don't recognize (a possible sign of identity theft or a mixed-up file);
   - late payments you didn't make;
   - debts shown as unpaid that you paid off;
   - wrong personal details (name, address, Social Security number);
   - old negative items that should have dropped off. Most negative items drop off after 7 years, and Chapter 7 bankruptcy after 10 *(current, verify)*.

4. **Dispute errors in writing.** Dispute with the credit bureau and with the company that reported the item. The Fair Credit Reporting Act requires the bureau to investigate, usually within about 30 days *(current, verify)*. Keep copies of everything. The book stresses keeping good records throughout.

5. **If the report is accurate, work on what it shows.**
   - **Debt load:** the book says your monthly payments on all debts except the mortgage should stay **at or under 15% of your gross income** *(book)*. If you're above that, lenders may see you as overextended. Pay down high-interest balances first.
   - **Thin file:** if you have little credit history, build some. Use one card lightly and pay it in full every month. The book also advises paying credit cards in full instead of carrying a balance *(book)*.
   - **Recent applications:** don't apply for several more cards right away. Each application can add a hard inquiry.

6. **Consider a credit freeze if anything looks like fraud.** Freezes are free at all three bureaus *(current, verify)*.

7. **Skip paid credit-repair services.** That's the book's view *(book)*, and you can do all of the above yourself for free.

## 3. You're married: does that affect your credit file?

Yes, it can. The book covers this directly.

**Each person has their own credit file.** Marriage doesn't merge your file with your spouse's *(current, verify)*. What matters is how each account is set up and reported.

**The "user" designation.** The book explains that the **Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA)** created a "user" designation. It lets a married person get credit history for accounts in the spouse's name or held jointly *(book)*. The book says this mainly protected **women**. Many of them had paid household accounts for years, but the history built up only in the husband's file. That left them with no credit record of their own after divorce or widowhood.

**What to check:**
- **Look at every account you and your spouse share, or that you use, and make sure it appears on *your* report.** The book specifically advises checking that these accounts are reported under your name *(book)*. If a joint card or loan shows up only on your spouse's file, ask the lender to report it in your name too.
- **Joint accounts work in both directions.** On a joint account, both of you are legally responsible. Late payments hurt both files.
- **An authorized user on your spouse's card** may get the account's history, depending on the issuer's reporting. But you aren't responsible for the debt *(current, verify)*.
- **Your spouse's own accounts** (in their name only) shouldn't appear on your file, and their problems shouldn't hurt your score.
- **If you changed your name when you married,** make sure your report shows all your accounts under both names and nothing is split across two files.

**Why this might matter for your denial.** If most of your household's credit is in your spouse's name, your own file may be thin. That could be why you were turned down. Getting shared accounts reported in your name, as the book advises, is one of the quickest legitimate ways to strengthen your file.

**Your rights.** Under the ECOA, a lender can't deny you credit because of your sex or marital status *(book; current, verify)*. If you think that happened, you can file a complaint with the **CFPB** (consumerfinance.gov).

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Read the denial notice for the bureau used and the reasons
- [ ] Get free reports from all three bureaus at annualcreditreport.com
- [ ] Go through each report entry by entry (the book's credit-report checklist)
- [ ] Dispute errors in writing and keep copies
- [ ] Confirm joint or spouse accounts appear on *your* report (ECOA)
- [ ] Check that non-mortgage debt payments are at or under 15% of gross income
- [ ] Hold off on new applications while you fix things
- [ ] Skip paid credit-repair services

---

*This is general educational information based on a 1989 book, not personal financial or legal advice. Credit-reporting law (FCRA, ECOA) has been amended many times since 1989. Check current rules with the CFPB (consumerfinance.gov), the FTC (consumer.ftc.gov) or the credit bureaus.*
