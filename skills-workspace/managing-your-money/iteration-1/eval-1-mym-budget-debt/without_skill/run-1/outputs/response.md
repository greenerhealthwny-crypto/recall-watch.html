# Budget, Debt, Then Investing: A Step-by-Step Order

**A caveat first:** I don't have the text of the *Consumer Reports Complete Guide to Managing Your Money* in front of me. I can't quote its chapters, page numbers or exact figures, and I won't pretend to. What follows is the standard order of operations that consumer-finance guides like Consumer Reports generally recommend, applied to your situation. Before you rely on any specific number below, check it against your copy of the book.

---

## The short answer: the order

1. **Track your spending and build a written budget** so you know where the money goes.
2. **Build a small starter emergency fund** (around $1,000, or one month of bare-bones expenses). This keeps a car repair from going back on the credit card.
3. **Pay down the high-interest credit card debt aggressively** while making the minimum payments on the car loan.
4. **Grab any employer 401(k) match** if you have one, even while you're paying off debt. A match is an immediate, guaranteed return.
5. **Finish building a full emergency fund** of 3 to 6 months of essential expenses, kept in a high-yield savings or money market account.
6. **Decide what to do about the car loan.** Pay it off early if the rate is high. If it's low, keep paying it on schedule.
7. **Then start investing in stocks**, preferably through tax-advantaged accounts and low-cost, diversified index funds rather than individual stocks.

---

## Step 1: Set up the household budget

- **Gather 2 to 3 months of statements** (bank, credit card, pay stubs) and record your actual take-home income and every expense.
- **Sort expenses into categories:**
  - *Fixed:* rent or mortgage, car payment, insurance, phone, minimum debt payments
  - *Variable necessities:* groceries, utilities, gas, medical
  - *Discretionary:* dining out, entertainment, subscriptions, shopping
  - *Irregular or annual:* car registration, gifts, holidays. Divide each by 12 and budget monthly.
- **Pick a framework.** A common starting point is the 50/30/20 guideline: 50% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings and extra debt payments. While you're in debt, aim to shift more than 20% toward debt and savings by trimming "wants."
- **Find cuts.** Unused subscriptions, insurance re-shopping (Consumer Reports often stresses comparison shopping for insurance, phone and cable), cooking at home, and avoiding new credit purchases.
- **Automate.** Schedule transfers to savings and debt payments right after payday so the budget runs itself.
- **Review monthly** and adjust. A budget is a living document.

## Step 2: Starter emergency fund

With no savings, any surprise expense goes straight onto a credit card and undoes your progress. Park $500 to $1,000 in a separate savings account before attacking debt hard.

## Step 3: Attack the debt (about $9,000)

- **List every debt** with its balance, interest rate (APR) and minimum payment.
- **Pay minimums on everything, then put every extra dollar toward one target:**
  - *Avalanche method* (highest APR first). This saves the most money and is usually the credit card.
  - *Snowball method* (smallest balance first). This gives quick psychological wins.
- **Lower the rate on the credit card debt:**
  - Call the issuer and ask for a lower APR.
  - Consider a 0% balance-transfer card. Watch the 3 to 5% transfer fee and pay the balance off before the promo period ends.
  - Or consider a lower-rate personal or credit-union consolidation loan.
- **Stop adding new charges.** Use debit or cash until the card balance is gone.
- **Beware of debt-settlement companies.** If you need help, a nonprofit credit counselor (NFCC-affiliated) is the safer route.
- **The car loan:** car loans usually carry lower rates than credit cards, so they typically come after the cards. If the rate is high, look into refinancing through a credit union.

## Step 4: Employer retirement match

If your employer matches 401(k) contributions, contribute enough to get the full match even during debt payoff. Skipping it means turning down free money.

## Step 5: Full emergency fund

Once the credit cards are paid off, redirect those payments into savings until you have **3 to 6 months of essential expenses**. Keep it safe and easy to reach in an FDIC-insured high-yield savings account or a money market account. It should not be in stocks.

## Step 6: Start investing in stocks

When high-interest debt is gone and the emergency fund is in place, here's how to begin:

- **Use tax-advantaged accounts first:** 401(k) or 403(b) beyond the match, then a Roth or traditional IRA.
- **Favor low-cost, broadly diversified index funds or ETFs** (for example, a total-market or S&P 500 fund) or a target-date fund over picking individual stocks. Fees compound against you, so compare expense ratios.
- **Invest regularly** through automatic monthly contributions (dollar-cost averaging) rather than trying to time the market.
- **Match your mix of investments to your time horizon and risk tolerance.** Money you'll need within about 5 years shouldn't be in stocks.
- **Avoid high-commission products and hot tips.** Be cautious with advisers who earn commissions. A fee-only fiduciary is the safer choice if you want help.

---

## Why this order?

- **Paying off credit card debt is a guaranteed return equal to its interest rate**, often 20% or more. That beats the long-run average stock market return of roughly 7 to 10%, which isn't guaranteed.
- **Investing without an emergency fund is risky.** A job loss or big bill could force you to sell stocks at a loss or borrow on cards again.
- **The budget comes first because it creates the surplus** that pays for every later step.
