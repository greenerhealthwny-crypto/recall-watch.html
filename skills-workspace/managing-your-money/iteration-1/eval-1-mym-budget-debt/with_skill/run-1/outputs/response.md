# Your plan, in the book's order: budget, cushion, debt, insurance, then stocks

The *Consumer Reports Complete Guide to Managing Your Money* (Consumers Union, 1989) answers your question directly. You **should not start buying stocks yet.** The book lists three things to have before you invest in stocks: your debts under control, adequate insurance, and an emergency fund. You have $9,000 of consumer debt and no savings, so you're at the start of that sequence. Starting there isn't a failure. It's simply where the plan begins.

> **A note on dates:** the book was published in 1989. Its principles still hold. Its tax rules, interest rates and dollar figures are out of date. Below, **(book)** marks what the book says and **(today, verify)** marks current context you should check with official sources such as the CFPB, FDIC, IRS or annualcreditreport.com.

---

## Step 1: Build the budget with the book's four-step method (book)

The book's budgeting chapter uses four steps:

1. **Cash-flow analysis: see where your money actually went.** This step isn't a budget yet. You're just recording what happened. Go through the last 3–12 months of bank and card statements and total your income and your spending by category, for example:
   - Housing (rent or mortgage, utilities)
   - Car loan payment, gas, car insurance, repairs
   - Credit card payments
   - Groceries and eating out
   - Insurance (health, renters or homeowner's, life)
   - Phone, internet, subscriptions
   - Clothing, personal, entertainment
   - Irregular items: gifts, medical, annual fees
2. **Set budget goals.** For you the goals are already clear: (a) build a starter emergency cushion, (b) pay off the $9,000, (c) then save and invest.
3. **Allocate resources.** Give each category a monthly dollar limit. Put the debt payments and the emergency-fund deposit into the budget as fixed lines, not as whatever is left over at the end of the month.
4. **Year-end review.** Total each category and compare it with your budget and with the year before. Confirm that your income (not counting what you saved) was **more than your spending**. The book suggests charting progress, and your falling debt balance is the obvious thing to chart. Then set next year's goals.

The book's supporting advice for this chapter:
- **Budget for the unexpected.** Car repairs, medical bills and annual premiums will come up, so set money aside for them.
- **Keep good records.**
- **Make the budget work.** Revise it when reality doesn't match the plan.

### Run the book's debt test while you're doing this (book)
The book's rule is that monthly principal and interest on all debts **except a mortgage** shouldn't exceed **15% of your gross (pre-tax) income**.

- *Example:* on a gross income of $4,000 a month, your car loan plus card payments should total **$600 or less**.
- If you're over 15%, your debt is not "under control" by the book's standard, and paying it down is the top priority.
- Lenders use a related **28%/36%** guideline: housing at about 28% of gross income, and all debt including housing at about 36%. That matters if you plan to buy a home later.

## Step 2: Start an emergency fund (book)

The book puts the emergency fund ahead of investing. It calls the right size "a personal matter":
- Some experts quoted in the book suggest $10,000–$15,000 (in 1989 dollars).
- Others suggest a set number of months of expenses.
- **(today, verify):** a common current guideline is **3–6 months of essential expenses**.

**How to do this alongside the debt.** The book doesn't give a split between saving and paying down debt. A practical approach that fits its logic is to **build a small starter cushion first**, for example one month of essential expenses or $1,000–$2,000. That way a flat tire doesn't go back on the credit card. Then send most of your extra money to the debt (Step 3). Once the high-interest debt is gone, finish the full emergency fund.

**Where to keep it (book):** keep it liquid and safe. The book discusses savings accounts, money market deposit accounts, credit unions (which often pay better savings rates) and short-term CDs. Shop rates, and don't accept a poor return just out of habit. As the book puts it, "recognize your value to the bank and expect adequate compensation in return."
- **(today, verify):** an FDIC- or NCUA-insured high-yield savings account works well. Coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category.

## Step 3: Get the debt under control, credit cards first (book)

- **Pay off the credit cards first.** The book's advice on charge cards is to pay them in full each month. It also noted that the 1986 tax law phased out the deduction for consumer interest, so card interest is pure cost.
  - **(today, verify):** card interest is still not deductible, and card rates are usually far higher than car-loan rates. Each card dollar you pay off earns you that card's rate, risk-free, which is a better return than stocks can promise.
- **Order of attack (practical application):** make the minimum payment on everything, then put every extra dollar on the **highest-interest card**. When that card is paid off, move its payment to the next one. The car loan, which usually has a lower rate, comes last.
- **Stop adding to the balance.** While you're paying down the cards, use cash or a debit card for everyday spending.
- **The car loan:** keep paying it as scheduled. If it has a high rate, check whether it allows prepayment without penalty, and compare refinancing offers by **APR** (the book's standard for comparing loans).
- **Check your credit report (book).** The book's credit chapter suggests reviewing your file and disputing errors. **(today, verify):** free reports from all three bureaus are available at **annualcreditreport.com**.
- **The book is wary of paying for help.** It questions paid "credit credential" and monitoring services. If you look for help with debt, **(today, verify)** use a nonprofit credit counselor and avoid debt-settlement companies that charge large fees.
- **Bankruptcy is not your situation.** The book covers Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 for debt that can't be managed. $9,000 can be handled with a budget. (Bankruptcy law was rewritten in 2005, so the book's details are out of date anyway.)

## Step 4: Close any insurance gaps (book)

"Adequate insurance" is one of the book's conditions for investing in stocks. Its principle is to insure against catastrophe and to calculate how much you need instead of buying whatever is pitched. Check these:
- **Health insurance:** one big medical bill can undo everything above.
- **Auto:** since you have a car loan, the lender almost certainly requires collision and comprehensive. Carry solid **liability** limits, and shop around, because premiums vary a lot.
- **Renter's or homeowner's**, including liability.
- **Disability:** first add up what you already have (an employer plan, Social Security disability) before buying more.
- **Life insurance, only if someone depends on your income.** Use the book's three-step method: (1) estimate what your family would need, (2) subtract your assets and other income sources, (3) insure the gap. Most people are best served by low-cost **term** insurance.
- **Skip** credit-life insurance on your loans, which the book says "is usually not a good buy", and dread-disease policies.

## Step 5: Start retirement saving (book + today)

The book tells readers to start retirement planning with their first job, even while career and family take most of their attention.
- **(today, verify):** if your employer matches 401(k) contributions, many people contribute enough to get the full match even while paying off debt, since the match is an immediate return on your money. After the cards are paid off, increase your contributions. IRA and 401(k) limits are much higher than the book's 1989 figure of $2,000. Check IRS.gov for this year's limits.

## Step 6: Then invest in stocks, starting carefully (book)

Once the cards are paid, your non-mortgage debt is under 15% of gross income, your insurance is in place and your emergency fund is full, the book's advice for a new investor is:

1. **Understand the trade-off.** Investments offer **liquidity, safety and total return.** "Liquidity and safety usually go hand-in-hand, but for a higher return you generally have to sacrifice some liquidity or safety, or both."
2. **Start in stages.** Until your surplus beyond your emergency and retirement funds reaches about **$10,000–$20,000**, the book suggests short-term (6–12 month) CDs, Treasury bills, or a **low-minimum stock or bond mutual fund**, with staggered maturities.
3. **Diversify.** Don't put your money into a single company. A fund gives you more diversification than most individuals can buy on their own.
4. **Keep costs low.** Avoid **load** (sales-commission) funds and 12b-1 fees, which the book says "do nothing" for existing shareholders. Use discount brokers.
5. **Invest regularly.** **Dollar-cost averaging** (investing a fixed amount on a schedule) and **dividend reinvestment** suit small investors.
6. **Avoid speculation.** The book lists high-risk assets such as fledgling companies, commodity options, precious metals and collectibles.
7. **Be skeptical of advisers.** "Few people need a financial planner." The book warns about product sellers with "misleading titles".

**(today, verify):** today the low-cost way to follow this advice is usually a broad **index fund or ETF**. Expense ratios are far below 1989 levels, and trading is often commission-free. If you hire an adviser, ask whether they are a **fee-only fiduciary** and look them up on FINRA BrokerCheck.

## Summary: your order of operations

| # | Step | Done when… |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cash-flow analysis and budget (four steps) | You know where every dollar goes and have a monthly plan |
| 2 | Starter emergency cushion | About one month of essentials (or $1,000–$2,000) is saved |
| 3 | Pay off credit cards (highest rate first), keep paying the car loan | Cards at $0, and non-mortgage debt payments ≤ 15% of gross income |
| 4 | Insurance check-up | Health, auto liability, renter's or home, disability; life if you have dependents |
| 5 | Full emergency fund and retirement saving | 3–6 months of expenses saved *(today)*; at least the employer match captured |
| 6 | Begin investing | Diversified, low-cost funds, dollar-cost averaging; CDs, T-bills or a low-minimum fund until the surplus reaches about $10k–$20k |
| — | Every year | Year-end budget review: income above spending, debt chart, new goals |
