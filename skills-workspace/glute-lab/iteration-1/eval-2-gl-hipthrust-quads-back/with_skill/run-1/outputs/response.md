You're probably not doing much "wrong." These are two of the most common hip thrust complaints, and they usually have fairly mechanical causes. Most likely your **bench is too high for your height**, and you're **arching your lower back at the top** instead of finishing the movement with your hips. Here's how to fix both, based on Bret Contreras's *Glute Lab* (he's the coach who popularized the barbell hip thrust).

## 1. Your bench is probably too tall (the main reason you feel your quads)

Contreras's guidelines for bench height:

- **Most people:** about 14 in (35 cm).
- **Lifters 5'2" or shorter** (or anyone with long legs and a short torso): **about 12 in (30 cm).**
- **Too high → more quads.** Too low → you end up hinging over the bench.

Gym flat benches are often around 16–18 inches, so at 5'2" you could easily be 4–6 inches too high. That shifts the work toward your quads, and it also makes it easier to arch your back to get the bar up.

**Ways to get a lower bench:**
- An **aerobic step with risers**, braced against a wall so it can't slide. This is the setup Contreras suggests, and you can set it right around 12 in.
- An **adjustable decline bench**, a lower padded box, or a hip thrust machine if your gym has one.
- **Check your setup:** the bench edge should sit just **below your shoulder blades**. If it sits higher on your back, the bench is too tall or you're too far onto it.

## 2. Other ways to move the work from your quads to your glutes

Try these one at a time and see which ones help:

| Fix | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Move your feet farther out** | At the top, your shins should be about vertical. If your knees end up well past your toes, your feet are too close. |
| **Push through your heels** | It's fine if your toes lift a little. |
| **Use a mini band around your knees** | Pushing your knees out against the band brings in more glute. Keep your knees tracking over your middle toes. |
| **Lock out fully and squeeze** | Finish with shoulders, hips and knees roughly in a line, and hold a hard squeeze for about 1 second. Stop the set when you can't reach full lockout. |
| **Try the "American hip thrust"** | Put the bench edge at mid-back with your feet a bit closer, and focus on tilting your pelvis under. It uses less quad and more hamstring, and Contreras specifically recommends it when regular hip thrusts bother your back. |

Also, **feeling some quad doesn't mean your glutes aren't working.** How strongly you feel a muscle varies a lot between people, and it improves with practice. Do a few sets of bodyweight or banded glute bridges as a warm-up and focus on the squeeze.

## 3. Lower back soreness: stop finishing the lift with your spine

The usual cause is **spinal hyperextension at lockout**. When your hips stop rising, you keep going by arching your lower back and flaring your ribs. That loads your back instead of your glutes, and a bench that's too tall makes it worse.

Fixes from the book:
- **"Move from your sternum down."** Keep your ribs down and your torso stiff. Your upper body should rise as one piece, pivoting on the bench.
- **Tuck your chin and look forward**, not up at the ceiling. Your gaze should follow your torso up, ending on the wall in front of you or your knees.
- **Tilt your pelvis slightly under at the top** (posterior pelvic tilt, like tucking your tailbone). Many lifters, Contreras included, like a slight arch at the bottom that turns into a tuck at the top.
- **At the top, your torso should be about parallel to the floor**, not arched above it.
- **Brace your core at the bottom** of each rep before you drive up.
- **Don't slide up and down the bench.** Your shoulder blades should stay in the same spot the whole set.

**Lighten the load while you learn this.** If you can't reach full lockout without arching, the weight is too heavy for now. Hip thrusts are meant to be easy on the lower back when done well, so lower back soreness means something needs to change.

## A quick plan for your next session

1. Set up on a lower surface (about 12 in), with the edge just below your shoulder blades. Use a bar pad.
2. Warm up with 2 sets of 15–20 banded glute bridges, holding the squeeze at the top.
3. Hip thrust **3 sets of 10–12** with about **60–70% of your usual weight**. Use a knee band, keep your shins vertical at the top, push through your heels, keep your chin tucked and ribs down, and hold a 1-second squeeze with your pelvis tucked.
4. Film one set from the side. Check for vertical shins, a flat (not arched) torso at the top, and full hip extension.
5. Add weight only once every rep looks like that.

If one setup still doesn't feel right, try the American hip thrust variation. Contreras's general rule is to keep what you feel in your glutes and what doesn't hurt.

**One caution:** normal muscle soreness that fades in a day or two is one thing. If your back pain is sharp, lasts, spreads into your leg, or happens even with good form and lighter weights, stop the exercise and see a physical therapist or doctor. Until then, you can work around it with knee-banded bridges, frog pumps, and band abduction work, which are easy on the back.
