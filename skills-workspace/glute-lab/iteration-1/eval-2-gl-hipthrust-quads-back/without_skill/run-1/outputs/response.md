Feeling hip thrusts in your quads and lower back is one of the most common complaints, and your height plus the flat bench probably explain a lot of it. Here's what's likely going on and how to fix it.

## 1. The bench is probably too tall for you

Most standard flat benches are about 16–17" (40–45 cm) high. That height suits people around 5'6" and taller. At 5'2", a standard bench puts your torso too high relative to your feet, which tends to:

- Make you push up and back with your legs (more quads) instead of pivoting your hips up
- Make it hard to keep your shoulders and ribcage stable, so you finish the rep by arching your lower back

**Fixes:**
- **Raise your feet.** Put a low aerobic step or a couple of stacked bumper plates (about 2–4") under your feet. This is the easiest fix and it effectively "lowers" the bench.
- **Use a lower surface.** An aerobic step with risers, a plyo box set lower, or a lower bench if your gym has one. You want something around 13–15" (33–38 cm).
- **Position your upper back correctly.** The edge of the bench should sit just below your shoulder blades (around the bottom tips), not up at your neck or down mid-back.

## 2. Your feet are probably too close to your butt

Foot position decides which muscles take most of the load:

- **Feet too close:** knees travel forward, so more quads (this is probably you)
- **Feet too far out:** more hamstrings
- **Just right:** at the **top** of the rep your **shins are vertical**, with knees roughly over your ankles and about a 90° bend at the knee

**Fix:** Do a rep with an empty bar or no weight, pause at the top, and look at your shins. If your knees are past your toes, move your feet out an inch or two. Also:
- Feet about shoulder-width or slightly wider, toes turned out a little (around 15–30°)
- Push through your **heels and midfoot**. Some people can lift their toes slightly to feel this. If your heels are coming up, you're pushing through the balls of your feet, which brings in more quads.
- Let your knees track out a little, in line with your toes

## 3. Your lower back is finishing the rep instead of your glutes

Lower back soreness almost always means you're **hyperextending your lumbar spine at the top**. Your ribs flare up and your back arches to get "extra height" once your hips have stopped extending.

**Fixes:**
- **Tuck your chin and look forward, not at the ceiling.** As you rise, keep your eyes on the wall in front of you or toward your knees. Throwing your head back pulls your spine into an arch.
- **Ribs down.** Keep your ribcage "stacked" over your pelvis through the whole rep.
- **Posterior pelvic tilt at the top.** Think "tuck your tailbone under" or "zip your pubic bone toward your ribs" as you lock out. This is the single biggest cue for getting the work into your glutes instead of your back.
- **Pivot around the bench.** Your upper back should rotate on the bench edge like a hinge. Your torso and hips move together as one unit, and you shouldn't be sliding up the bench.
- **Stop at full hip extension.** At lockout your body should form a straight line from your knees to your shoulders. Going higher than that is your back, not your glutes.

## 4. The weight may be too heavy for now

If load has outrun technique, your body will find the easiest path, which is quads and lower back. Drop the weight by 30–50% for a couple of weeks and:
- Hold a **2–3 second squeeze at the top** of every rep
- Use a controlled lowering (about 2 seconds)
- Add a light **mini band just above your knees** so you have something to push out against. This tends to light up the glutes.

You should feel a strong glute burn and squeeze. If you can't feel your glutes at that weight, more weight won't fix it.

## Quick checklist for your next session

1. Raise your feet on a step or plates (or use a lower bench)
2. Bench edge just below your shoulder blades
3. Feet placed so your shins are vertical at the top, pushing through your heels
4. Chin tucked, eyes forward, ribs down
5. Tuck your pelvis at lockout and stop when your hips are straight
6. Lighter weight with a 2-second pause at the top

## Helpful warm-ups and alternatives

- **Glute bridges on the floor** (bodyweight or banded): the floor is a "perfect height" for everyone, so these are great for learning the posterior tilt and lockout.
- **Single-leg hip thrusts** (bodyweight) for 1–2 sets before your barbell work to "wake up" your glutes.
- If the bench setup still feels off, **barbell glute bridges from the floor** are a great substitute while you sort out your equipment.

**One note:** normal muscle soreness in your back that fades in a day or two usually goes away once your form is fixed. If you have sharp pain, pain that lasts, or pain that travels down your leg, stop doing the exercise and get it checked by a physical therapist or doctor.
