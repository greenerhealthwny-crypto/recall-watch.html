# Ranking Three Coaching Markets with Hormozi's Starving-Crowd Criteria

In *$100M Offers*, Hormozi's order of importance is **Starving Crowd (market) > Offer Strength > Persuasion Skills**. A great market can save a weak offer, and a bad market sinks even a brilliant one. So this is the most important decision you'll make for the offer.

He judges a market on **four indicators**:
1. **Massive pain.** They *desperately need* it, not merely want it. "The pain is the pitch," and price is proportional to pain.
2. **Purchasing power.** They can afford to pay, ideally a premium. (His cautionary example: a resume service for the unemployed failed because the buyers had no money.)
3. **Easy to target.** They gather somewhere you can reach them: associations, lists, groups, channels.
4. **Growing.** Growth is a tailwind and decline is a headwind. Newspapers had three of the four but were shrinking.

He also says the evergreen markets sit in **health, wealth or relationships**.

## The ranking

### 1st: (b) Dentists wanting more patients
**This is the strongest starving crowd by Hormozi's criteria.** It matches all four indicators.
- **Pain:** more patients means more revenue, so the pain is measurable in dollars. You can show the ROI directly ("one new patient is worth $X in lifetime value").
- **Purchasing power:** this is Hormozi's own example. "Rich doctors" are easy to find, and they can afford premium prices. With a high price, the **virtuous cycle of price** kicks in: more margin, better delivery, better results, more justification for the price.
- **Targeting:** they're licensed, listed and clustered in associations and conferences.
- **Why it's a "wealth" offer:** you can sell it against the money it produces. That's the easiest kind of value to prove, and it supports guarantees tied to results (for example, "X new patients in 90 days or we keep working for free").
- **Watch-outs:** sophisticated buyers who are pitched constantly by dental marketing agencies, and some professional advertising rules (state dental boards). You'll need a differentiated **Grand Slam Offer**, not "marketing services", to avoid being a commodity.
- **Niche down further:** for example, "solo general dentists in their first 5 years of practice ownership", or "practices that want more implant/Invisalign cases". Hormozi: "Riches are in the niches." The same product can command many times the price for a specific avatar.

### 2nd: (a) Busy moms wanting to lose weight
**A good "normal" market, and worth doing with a strong offer, but weaker on purchasing power and targeting.**
- **Pain:** high and emotional (health, confidence, energy for the kids). This is the classic health market, and Hormozi's own gym clients sold exactly this outcome.
- **Purchasing power:** mixed. Many busy moms control household spending, but discretionary budgets are tight and price sensitivity is high. You'll likely need a lower price point or careful avatar selection (for example, "working professional moms aged 35–45", with higher household incomes).
- **Targeting:** reachable but crowded. Every fitness coach, app and program targets this group, so commoditization pressure is intense.
- **Growing:** yes. But GLP-1 medications and cheap apps are changing what "losing weight" means for buyers. A coaching offer would need to address that (for example, "for moms who want results without medication", or support *alongside* it, within your scope).
- **How to win here:** attack the **bottom of the Value Equation**. "Busy" means time delay and effort are the real obstacles, so lower them: done-for-you meal plans, 20-minute home workouts, prebuilt grocery carts (one of Hormozi's own delivery-vehicle examples), and text support.
- **Compliance note:** weight-loss outcome-plus-time claims ("lose 20 lbs in 6 weeks") are heavily regulated (FTC and platform ad policies). Use action goals in the name and keep testimonials typical and truthful.

### 3rd: (c) Teenagers wanting to learn guitar
**A weak starving crowd. It fails at least three of the four indicators.**
- **Pain:** a want, not a need. Nobody "desperately needs" to play guitar, and it sits outside health, wealth and relationships.
- **Purchasing power:** teenagers don't have money. This is the same trap as Hormozi's resume service for the unemployed. The real buyer is the parent, who is comparing you with cheap local lessons and free YouTube videos.
- **Targeting:** advertising to minors is restricted, and the content space is saturated with free alternatives.
- **Growing:** flat at best.
- **If you love this space anyway:** reposition toward a buyer with pain and money. For example, *parents* who want a screen-free, confidence-building activity for their child; or adults (often 35–55) who always wanted to learn guitar and have disposable income. That's a different, stronger market.

---

## Bottom line

**Rank: (b) Dentists > (a) Busy moms > (c) Teen guitarists.**

- **Dentists** are the closest to Hormozi's ideal starving crowd: massive financial pain, strong purchasing power, easy to target and stable-to-growing. Start here if you have, or can acquire, credible expertise in practice growth.
- **Busy moms** are a solid health market, but you'll be fighting commoditization and price sensitivity. Win with a Grand Slam Offer that removes time and effort.
- **Teen guitar** is the market you'd have to out-persuade, and Hormozi's whole point is that persuasion can't fix a bad market.

**One more filter:** pick the market whose avatar you can serve credibly. Hormozi committed to one narrow avatar (microgym owners) and turned everyone else away. Whichever you choose, **niche down hard**, then build the offer with the Value Equation and the five-step process.

*The scores are judgment calls meant to illustrate the framework, not market data. Validate them with real conversations and test offers before committing. As Hormozi says, try many offers. A failed offer "doesn't mean you suck. It means your offer sucks."*
