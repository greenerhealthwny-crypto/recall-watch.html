# Bly on Structure, USP, and Features vs. Benefits

*(From memory of Robert W. Bly's* The Copywriter's Handbook*. The ideas are summarized here, not quoted.)*

## 1. The "Motivating Sequence"

Bly gives a five-step sequence for copy that has to make a sale or get a response. It works for sales letters, ads, brochures and landing pages.

1. **Get attention.** This is mostly the headline's job, along with the opening lines and the visual. Speak to the reader's self-interest, usually with a strong benefit, news, or a provocative question.
2. **Show a need.** Point out a problem, want or pain the reader already has, or make them aware of one. Many prospects don't know they need the product until the copy shows them why.
3. **Position your product as the solution.** Once the need is established, show how the product meets it. Explain how it removes the problem or delivers the want.
4. **Prove it.** Back up your claims so the reader believes them. Proof can be testimonials, case studies, test results, statistics, guarantees, track record, demonstrations, or the credentials of the company. Skeptical readers need this step most.
5. **Ask for action.** Tell the reader exactly what to do next: call, order, mail the coupon, request a brochure or click. Give them a reason to do it now, such as an offer, a deadline, a bonus or a risk-free trial.

Bly treats this as a flexible checklist, not a rigid formula. Short ads may compress or merge steps. A reader who already knows they need the product may need little time on step 2. The logic holds either way: attention, need, solution, proof, action.

### How it differs from AIDA

AIDA (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action) is the classic older model. Both models start with attention and end with action. Bly's version differs in a few ways:

| | AIDA | Bly's motivating sequence |
|---|---|---|
| Steps | 4 | 5 |
| Middle steps | "Interest" and "Desire" are broad psychological states | Concrete jobs the copy must do: show the need, present the solution, prove it |
| Problem/need | Implied, not named | A step of its own: establish the need *before* pitching the product |
| Proof/credibility | Not a separate step | A step of its own. Persuasion requires belief, not just desire |
| Usefulness to the writer | Describes the reader's mental journey | A working outline you can write and check copy against |

In short, AIDA says *what the reader should feel* at each stage. Bly's sequence says *what the copy must do*. Above all, it adds an explicit **proof** step. Bly argues that readers are skeptical, so claims without support fail, and AIDA leaves this out. It also makes the **need/problem** step explicit, so the product is presented as the answer to something the reader cares about.

## 2. The Unique Selling Proposition (USP)

Bly takes the USP from Rosser Reeves (*Reality in Advertising*). He explains its three parts:

1. **A proposition.** Each ad must say to the reader: "Buy this product and you get this specific benefit."
2. **Unique.** The benefit must be one competitors don't or can't offer, or at least one they don't claim.
3. **Selling.** The proposition must be strong enough to move people to buy. A unique but trivial point does not count.

Bly's practical take:

- **Uniqueness can come from what you claim, not only from what the product is.** Many products are parity products, much the same as their rivals. You can still differentiate by being the first to stress a benefit the whole category shares. This is the "preemptive claim." The classic example is Claude Hopkins's Schlitz beer campaign, which explained that the bottles were steam-cleaned. Every brewer did that, but Schlitz said it first.
- **The USP must be important to the buyer.** Being different only counts if the difference matters to the reader. Find the product's most important benefit to the customer and build the copy around it.
- **Do your homework first.** Study the product, the market and the competition before you write. That study is where you find the USP. The headline and lead should usually be built on it.
- **Stay focused.** A clear USP gives the whole piece one central selling idea, so it doesn't scatter across dozens of minor points.

## 3. Features vs. Benefits

Bly's rule: **people buy benefits, not features.**

- A **feature** is a fact about the product: what it is, what it has, how it's built. Examples: "12-hour battery," "stainless-steel blade," "256-bit encryption."
- A **benefit** is what the feature *does for the customer*, meaning how it makes their life better, easier, cheaper, safer or more pleasant. Examples: "work a full day without hunting for an outlet," "stays sharp for years and never rusts," "your customers' data stays safe from hackers."

**Technique:** List every feature of the product. Next to each one, write the benefit it delivers. To find it, ask "So what?" or "What does this mean to the buyer?" until you reach something the reader actually cares about. Lead with the benefits and use the features as support and proof.

**Bly's qualifications** (he was trained as an engineer and wrote a great deal of technical and business-to-business copy):

- **Features still matter, especially to technical and business buyers.** Engineers, IT staff and purchasing agents often want specifications, performance data and technical details. A copy of nothing but vague benefits looks like fluff to them and loses credibility. For these readers, give benefits *and* the hard features or specs behind them.
- **Know your audience.** How you balance features and benefits depends on who is reading. Consumers respond mainly to benefits and emotion. Specialists want to see the substance that makes the benefits believable.
- **Features serve as proof.** A specific feature makes a benefit claim credible. "Lasts longer" becomes believable when you explain *why*. So features support step 4, the proof step, of the motivating sequence.

## How the three ideas fit together

The **USP** is the main benefit the whole piece is built around. The **features-to-benefits translation** gives you the material. The **motivating sequence** puts that material in persuasive order: grab attention with the key benefit, show the need, present the product as the solution, prove it with features, evidence and testimonials, and ask for the order.
