# Bly on the Motivating Sequence, USP, and Features vs Benefits

*Source: Robert W. Bly, *The Copywriter's Handbook*, 3rd ed. (2005), Chapter 4 "Writing to Sell". Points marked (book) come from the book. Points marked (interpretation) are my own reading or general copywriting knowledge, not Bly's wording.*

---

## 1. The motivating sequence

Bly gives five steps for organizing a piece of sales copy (book):

| # | Step | What it means in practice |
|---|------|---------------------------|
| 1 | **Get attention** | Open with your strongest benefit, usually in the headline. Don't open with a pun or a gimmick. Bly says wordplay "does not sell products." |
| 2 | **Show a need** | Show the reader that they have a problem or a want your product deals with. Many readers don't know they have the need until you point it out. |
| 3 | **Satisfy the need, and position your product as the solution** | Show that your product meets that need, and why it does so better than the other options. |
| 4 | **Prove it** | Back up your claims with testimonials, results, case histories, your track record, test data and guarantees. |
| 5 | **Ask for action** | Tell the reader exactly what to do next (call, order, send for the free booklet) and make it easy to do. |

This is also step 2 of Bly's 11 tips for clear copy: "carefully organize your selling points", which means following the motivating sequence (book).

### How it differs from AIDA

AIDA (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action) is the classic formula, and the motivating sequence came after it (book). The two start and end the same way. The middle is different:

| AIDA | Motivating sequence | What changes (interpretation) |
|------|---------------------|-------------------------------|
| Attention | Get attention | Same step, but Bly is specific about how: lead with the strongest benefit. |
| Interest | Show a need | AIDA's "interest" is a vague state of mind. Bly gives the writer a concrete job: name the reader's problem. |
| Desire | Satisfy the need and position the product | "Desire" turns into a link between the problem and *your* product as its answer, which puts competitive positioning inside the copy. |
| *(no equivalent step)* | **Prove it** | This is the real addition. AIDA has no step for proof. Bly makes evidence (testimonials, data, guarantees) a required part of the copy. |
| Action | Ask for action | Same step, with Bly's stress on stating exactly what to do and making the response easy. |

In short (interpretation):
- AIDA describes **what the reader feels** at each stage.
- The motivating sequence tells the writer **what the copy has to do**.
- It is built around a problem and its solution, and it adds proof.

For skeptical, business-to-business or high-ticket buyers, the proof step is often what closes the sale.

## 2. USP (Unique Selling Proposition)

Bly uses Rosser Reeves's definition, which has three requirements (book):

1. **Every ad must make a proposition to the reader:** "Buy this product and you will get this specific benefit."
2. **The proposition must be one the competition either cannot or does not offer.** That is the "unique" part.
3. **The proposition must be strong enough to move the mass millions,** meaning strong enough to pull in new customers.

**Bly's practical point (book):**
- Many products have no truly unique feature.
- In that case, look for an important benefit that **competitors haven't publicized** and claim it first.
- His example is a brewery that advertised that its bottles were washed in live steam. Every brewery did this, but this one was first to say so, and the claim came to belong to it. (The book doesn't name the brewery.)

**How USP fits with other tools (book):**
- Bly treats positioning (Trout and Ries) as a complement to features, benefits and sales arguments, not a replacement.
- In his headline method, you ask:
  1. Who is my customer?
  2. What are the product's important features?
  3. Why will the customer buy?
- The answer to question 3 is the key selling proposition, and it goes in the headline.

## 3. Features vs benefits

**The principle (book):**
- A **feature** is what the product *is* or *has*.
- A **benefit** is what the product *does for the buyer*.
- Customers buy benefits, not features.

**Bly's method (book):**
1. Write a **features/benefits list**.
2. List every feature of the product.
3. Next to each one, write the benefit it gives the buyer.

Bly works through this using an ordinary No. 2 pencil.

Illustrative pairs in the spirit of that example (interpretation, not quoted from the book):

| Feature | Benefit |
|---------|---------|
| Eraser on the end | Fix mistakes right away, with no separate eraser to find |
| Hexagonal barrel | It won't roll off your desk |
| Graphite "lead" | Writes smoothly and erases cleanly |
| Inexpensive wood | Cheap enough to keep a dozen around |

**A useful test is "So what?"** Read each feature and ask "So what?" until you reach something the reader cares about. That answer is the benefit (interpretation, based on Bly's approach).

**Tie benefits to motivators (book).** Bly lists 22 copy motivators, including:
- making money, saving money, saving time
- making work easier
- being secure, healthy, liked or important
- fear, greed and guilt

A benefit is strongest when it connects to one of these. He also recommends Masterson's **BFD formula**: write down your market's **Beliefs, Feelings and Desires** before you write any copy.

**Features still matter (interpretation):**
- Features are the proof behind the benefits.
- Technical buyers want specifications.
- Bly's advice is to lead with benefits and back them up with features, not to drop features.

---

## Putting it together

A practical way to use all three ideas in one piece of copy (book workflow, condensed):

1. Research the product, the customer (BFD, motivators) and what competitors claim.
2. Build the features/benefits list.
3. Pick your USP. If nothing is unique, pick the strongest benefit competitors haven't claimed.
4. Put that USP or benefit in the headline (step 1 of the sequence). Check it against the 4 U's: urgent, unique, ultra-specific, useful.
5. Write the body in the motivating sequence: need, then solution, then proof, then a specific call to action.

*Caveat: make only truthful, substantiated claims. FTC rules apply to claims, testimonials and endorsements.*
