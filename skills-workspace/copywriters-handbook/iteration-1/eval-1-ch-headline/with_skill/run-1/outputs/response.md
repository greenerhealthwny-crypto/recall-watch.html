# Critique and Rewrite: "Quality Bookkeeping Solutions For Your Business"

*Method: Robert W. Bly, The Copywriter's Handbook, 3rd ed. (2005), Chapter 2 (Headlines) and Chapter 4 (Writing to Sell).*

---

## 1. The diagnosis in one line

The headline names a category (bookkeeping), adds two words every competitor also uses ("quality", "solutions"), and offers no benefit, no news, no specifics and no reason to read on. Change "bookkeeping" to "plumbing" or "IT" and it works just as well. That is the problem.

---

## 2. Bly's four functions of a headline

Bly says a headline has four jobs. Here is how this one does:

| Function | How the original does | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Get attention** | Fails | No benefit, news, promise or curiosity. It reads like a sign on an office door. |
| **2. Select the audience** | Weak | "Your business" covers everyone. It doesn't say *which* business owner: a sole trader, a contractor, a restaurant, a 20-person agency. |
| **3. Deliver a complete message** | Fails | The reader only learns "we do bookkeeping." There is no proposition along the lines of "buy this, get this benefit." |
| **4. Draw the reader into the body copy** | Fails | Nothing in it is unresolved, promised or intriguing, so there is no reason to read the next line. |

## 3. Word-by-word critique

- **"Quality"** is an empty claim. Every bookkeeper says it, and nobody advertises "mediocre bookkeeping." Bly's clarity rule applies: *be specific, because facts, figures and details sell and generalities don't.* Quality has to be shown with proof (accuracy rate, credentials, turnaround), not announced.
- **"Bookkeeping"** describes the **feature** (the service), not the **benefit**. Customers don't want bookkeeping. They want what it gets them: their evenings back, no tax-time panic, knowing where the cash is, a clean set of books for the accountant or the bank. Ask Bly's "so what?" question.
- **"Solutions"** is jargon and filler. It adds length but no meaning. (Clarity tips 5–7: use simple words, avoid jargon, be concise.)
- **"For Your Business"** is a gesture toward you-orientation, but it's generic. It doesn't *target a particular reader* (technique 20).
- **Title Case On Every Word** makes it look like a brochure label rather than a message. This is a small point, but it adds to the "corporate filler" feel.

## 4. 4 U's score (Masterson, as taught by Bly)

Score each 1 to 4. Bly's rule: if a headline doesn't score 3 or 4 on at least three of the four U's, rewrite it.

| U | Score | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Urgent | 1 | No reason to act now. |
| Unique | 1 | Any bookkeeper could run it word for word. |
| Ultra-specific | 1 | No numbers, no niche, no outcome. |
| Useful | 2 | It implies a useful service but states no benefit. |

**Total: 5 out of 16. It doesn't reach 3 on any U, so it must be rewritten.**

## 5. The method: how Bly would rebuild it

### Step 1: Ask Bly's three headline questions
1. **Who is my customer?** For example, owners of small businesses (1–25 staff) who do their own books at night, or have fallen behind, and dread tax season.
2. **What are the product's important features?** For example, monthly reconciliation, a named bookkeeper, monthly P&L and balance sheet, catch-up/clean-up of past months, flat monthly fee, accountant-ready year-end files, cloud software setup.
3. **Why will the customer buy (which feature matters most)?** The answer is the **key selling proposition** that goes in the headline. For most owners it is one of these: *time back*, *no tax-time stress / no penalties*, or *knowing their numbers so they can make decisions*.

### Step 2: Turn features into benefits (the "so what?" test)

| Feature | So what? (benefit) |
|---|---|
| Monthly reconciliation | Books are always current, so no year-end scramble |
| Dedicated bookkeeper | One person who knows your business answers your questions |
| Monthly P&L report | You see profit and cash flow while you can still act on them |
| Catch-up service | Months of backlog cleared without you touching a receipt |
| Flat monthly fee | No surprise invoices, so you know the cost upfront |
| Accountant-ready year-end | Lower accountant bill and faster tax filing |

### Step 3: Find the USP (Rosser Reeves)
A USP has to be a real proposition, one competitors don't or can't offer, and strong enough to pull customers. If nothing about the service is truly unique, Bly's fallback is to **stress an under-publicized benefit** that competitors offer but don't talk about. In bookkeeping, plausible candidates are a guaranteed close date ("books closed by the 10th of every month"), a fixed flat fee, a named bookkeeper rather than a pool, or a clean-up guarantee. **Pick one you can actually deliver and prove.**

### Step 4: Check BFD and the motivators
- **Beliefs:** "Bookkeepers are expensive." "I can do it myself." "They'll make it more complicated."
- **Feelings:** guilt about the shoebox of receipts, dread of tax season, anxiety about the IRS or tax authority.
- **Desires:** time with family, confidence about cash, a business that feels under control.
- **Copy motivators that apply:** save time, make work easier, be secure, fear, guilt, save money.

### Step 5: Write many headlines with the 38 techniques, then score them
Below, the rewrites are grouped by Bly's headline types and techniques.

> **Note on facts:** bracketed items like **[10th]**, **[$X]** or **[12 hours]** are placeholders. Replace them with *your* real, provable figures. Bly's insistence on specifics only works if the specifics are true and substantiated (FTC truth-in-advertising rules apply to claims and testimonials).

## 8. Recommended headline

**Primary (general small-business audience):**
> **Books Closed by the [10th] of Every Month, or That Month Is Free**
> *Subhead:* Accurate monthly bookkeeping for small businesses at a flat fee, with a dedicated bookkeeper and reports you'll actually read.

Why it wins on Bly's terms:
- **Attention:** a specific, unusual promise backed by a guarantee (technique 26).
- **Audience:** the subhead selects small-business owners.
- **Complete message:** a reader who only sees the headline still gets the proposition: on-time books, guaranteed.
- **Pulls into the body:** it raises the questions "How do they do it?" and "What does it cost?"
- **USP:** a guaranteed close date is an under-publicized benefit most bookkeepers don't state.

**Alternative (if your best leads are behind on their books):**
> **Behind on Your Books? We'll Catch You Up in [30] Days, Even If It's Been a Year.**

Only use the guarantee if you can honor it. If you can't, use #9 or #4, which make claims you can support.

## 9. What comes after the headline (motivating sequence)

The headline is step 1 of Bly's five-step sequence. The body copy should follow:
1. **Get attention:** the headline above.
2. **Show a need:** the late-night receipts, the tax-time scramble, not knowing whether last month made money.
3. **Satisfy the need:** monthly reconciliation, a named bookkeeper, a one-page report by the [10th], flat fee.
4. **Prove it:** client testimonials, years in business, credentials (for example certified bookkeeper or software certifications, if you hold them), number of businesses served, the guarantee.
5. **Ask for action:** one specific, easy step, such as "Book a free 20-minute books review," with a button or phone number.

## 10. Quick checklist to reuse

- [ ] Does it state a **benefit**, not just the service?
- [ ] Does it **select a reader** (who is this for)?
- [ ] Is there a **specific** number, deadline, price or result?
- [ ] Would it make sense if it were the **only thing read** (no blind headline)?
- [ ] Have you cut **filler words** like quality, solutions, best-in-class or leading?
- [ ] Does it score **3+ on at least three of the 4 U's**?
- [ ] Is every claim **true and provable**?
- [ ] Did you write **10 or more** versions before choosing?
