# Is a supplier self-audit plus a COA enough for pasteurized liquid egg in an RTE, no-cook product?

## Short answer: No

Under the Preventive Controls for Human Food (PCHF) rule, 21 CFR Part 117, Subpart G (Supply-Chain Program, §§117.405–117.475), your QA lead's plan does not meet the requirement, for two reasons:

1. **A supplier's audit of itself is not an acceptable verification activity.** The rule says the receiving facility (you) cannot accept a supplier's own audit, or the supplier's own choice of how it should be verified, as supplier verification. An audit has to be done by a **qualified auditor** who is independent of the supplier. That means you, your own qualified auditor, or a third party with no financial conflict of interest.
2. **A certificate of analysis on its own is not enough for this hazard.** *Salmonella* in egg products is a hazard where exposure can cause serious adverse health consequences or death (a "SAHCODHA" hazard). Your product has no kill step after the egg arrives, so the supplier's pasteurization is the only control. For a SAHCODHA hazard controlled by the supplier, the default verification activity is an **onsite audit by a qualified auditor before you first approve the supplier, and at least once a year after that** (§117.430(b)). A COA is useful supporting evidence, but it does not replace the audit unless you have a documented, defensible written determination (see below).

## Why a supply-chain-applied control is required here

- Your hazard analysis (§117.130) should identify *Salmonella*, the main biological hazard in shell egg and raw egg product, as a hazard requiring a preventive control.
- You do not control that hazard in your own facility. There is no cook step after the egg arrives, and your product is ready-to-eat.
- The supplier controls it, through pasteurization, before you receive the egg.
- Under §117.410, when the supplier controls a hazard that requires a preventive control, you need a **supply-chain-applied control** and a written supply-chain program.
- The exemptions in §117.405 do not apply. They cover cases where a **customer** further down the chain controls the hazard, or where you control it yourself. Neither is true here.

## What the written supply-chain program must include (§117.410–§117.475)

The program is part of your food safety plan and must be written. It must cover the following elements.

### 1. Approved suppliers (§117.410(d), §117.420)
- **You** (the receiving facility) must approve the supplier. This step cannot be handed to anyone else.
- Approve the supplier **before** you receive the raw material, and document the approval.
- Base the approval on the evaluation described in item 3.

### 2. Written procedures for receiving raw materials (§117.420)
- Written procedures that ensure you receive liquid egg **only from approved suppliers**. If you ever use an unapproved supplier, the procedures must cover temporary use, with verification before use.
- Document that you follow these procedures. Examples are checking the supplier against the approved list at receiving, checking lot identification and paperwork, and checking temperature on arrival.

### 3. Supplier evaluation: what to consider when approving the supplier and choosing verification activities (§117.410(d))
Document an evaluation that considers:
- Your hazard analysis for this raw material: *Salmonella*, plus *Listeria monocytogenes* from contamination after pasteurization, because the product is RTE.
- The entity that applies the control: the supplier's pasteurization process.
- The supplier's **procedures, processes and practices**, including its pasteurization validation and its controls against contamination after pasteurization, such as packaging and environmental monitoring.
- **Applicable FDA (and here, USDA FSIS) food safety regulations and the supplier's compliance history.** Check for warning letters, import alerts, recalls, and FSIS noncompliance records.
- The supplier's **food safety performance history**: your own test results, earlier audit results, and how the supplier responded to problems.
- Other relevant factors, such as storage and transport practices. Liquid egg is temperature-sensitive.
- Re-evaluate the supplier periodically, and right away if you learn of new information such as a recall or a positive result.

### 4. Appropriate supplier verification activities and frequency (§117.410(b), §117.430)
The allowed verification activities are:
- **Onsite audits** (required by default for SAHCODHA hazards: before first use and at least annually)
- Sampling and testing of the raw material (by you, or by the supplier if results are documented and reviewed)
- Review of the supplier's relevant food safety records
- Other appropriate activities based on supplier performance and the risk

**For this case:**
- **Annual onsite audit by a qualified auditor** (§117.430(b)(1)). It is not a self-audit.
- **Alternative to the onsite audit:** instead of the audit, you may rely on the written results of an **inspection of the supplier by FDA, another federal agency such as USDA, or a state, local, tribal or territorial agency** (or a comparable foreign authority). The inspection must have been done within one year before the date the onsite audit would have been due (§117.430(e)). Egg products plants are under continuous USDA FSIS inspection, so this may be a workable route. You must actually **obtain and keep documentation** of that inspection and its results. Simply knowing that FSIS is present is not enough.
- **Departing from the annual-audit default:** you may use other verification activities or a lower audit frequency **only if you make a written determination** that they give adequate assurance the hazard is controlled (§117.430(b)(2)). For a single-sourced SAHCODHA ingredient going into an RTE, no-cook product, that justification will be hard to defend. Expect inspectors to scrutinize it.
- **Recommended additions to the audit:** lot-by-lot COAs with methods stated (for example, *Salmonella* negative, plus *Listeria* where relevant), periodic verification testing by you or an independent lab, and an annual review of the supplier's pasteurization and HACCP records.

### 5. Who does what (§117.415)
- **Only you** may approve suppliers.
- You may let another entity, such as a broker, a consultant, or a corporate QA function that is not the supplier, determine or conduct verification activities. You must then **review and assess** that entity's documentation.
- The **supplier may not** determine its own verification activities or audit itself for you.
- The supplier **may** do sampling and testing of specific lots, and you must review the results.

### 6. Onsite audit requirements (§117.435)
- Conducted by a **qualified auditor** (§117.3; trained and experienced in auditing).
- Considers the applicable FDA (and, for an FSIS-regulated egg products plant, the relevant FSIS) food safety regulations, and includes review of the supplier's written food safety plan (HACCP plan for an FSIS plant) and how it is carried out.
- A third-party audit, such as a GFSI-benchmarked scheme, can be used **if** the auditor is qualified, independent, and the scope covers the hazard. You still have to review the report and any corrective actions.
- **No financial conflicts of interest** that could influence the results (§117.430(f)). Payment by the supplier is not on its own a conflict, but the auditor must be independent of the supplier's operation.

### 7. Corrective actions when the supplier does not control the hazard (§117.410(e))
- Written procedures for what you will do if you learn the supplier is not controlling the hazard. Examples are an adverse audit, a positive *Salmonella* or *Listeria* result, or an FSIS action.
- Possible actions include stopping use, putting affected product on hold, testing, recall evaluation, and suspending the supplier until it shows it has fixed the problem.
- Document the actions you take.

### 8. Records (§117.475)
Keep these records, reviewed and maintained under Subpart F:
- The written supply-chain program
- Documentation of supplier approval
- Written receiving procedures and evidence that they were followed
- Documentation of the verification activities you determined, and the written justification for any alternative frequency or activity
- **Onsite audit records**: the auditor's name and qualifications, audit date, procedures used, conclusions, and corrective actions. Or, if you use the alternative, the documentation of the regulatory inspection.
- Sampling and testing records, such as COAs and your own test results, with the product, lot, method, test date, results, and corrective actions
- Records of your review of the supplier's food safety records
- Corrective action records
- Records of any reliance on another entity, and your review of its work

## Related controls in your own facility

Your plant still needs controls beyond the supply-chain program:
- **Receiving and storage temperature control** for the liquid egg, typically as a process or other preventive control, together with the CGMPs.
- **Post-pasteurization contamination in your own plant.** Once the egg is opened and mixed into an RTE product with no kill step, your own **sanitation controls and environmental monitoring for *Listeria*** (§117.135, §117.165) are critical.
- **Allergen controls.** Egg is a major allergen, so you need allergen labeling and cross-contact controls (§117.135(c)(2)).

## Bottom line for your QA lead

| Proposed element | Acceptable? |
|---|---|
| Supplier's own annual self-audit | **No.** A supplier's self-audit cannot be used as verification. |
| Certificate of analysis alone | **Not on its own** for a SAHCODHA hazard with no further kill step. Use it as a receiving and supporting check. |
| Annual onsite audit by an independent qualified auditor (or documented recent FDA, USDA or state inspection results) | **Yes.** This is the default requirement. |
| Lot COAs plus periodic verification testing plus supplier record review | **Strongly recommended** in addition to the audit. |
| Written program, approval, receiving procedures, corrective actions, records | **Required** |

*Note: this is a general regulatory overview based on 21 CFR Part 117, Subpart G. Check the current text of the regulation and FDA's supply-chain program guidance, and confirm with your PCQI or regulatory counsel, especially before relying on FSIS inspection results or making a written determination that departs from the annual onsite audit.*
