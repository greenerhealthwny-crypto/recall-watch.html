# Cook-step deviation: 152 °F against a ≥ 158 °F critical limit

**Short answer:** this needs a **corrective action**, not a correction. The product missed a critical limit at a process preventive control, and it has already moved to the next step. Under 21 CFR 117.150 you must find and fix the cause and reduce the chance it happens again. You must also evaluate every affected unit for safety, keep any food whose safety you can't ensure out of commerce, and document all of it. A PCQI (or someone working under PCQI oversight) then reviews that record within 7 working days.

---

## 1. What to do now (in order)

### Step 1: Stop and contain (within minutes)
- **Hold the affected batch where it is.** Tag it "HOLD – cook deviation", with the date, time and batch or lot code. Don't let it go on to freezing, packing or shipping until someone makes a documented disposition decision.
- **Track down everything the batch touched in assembly:** partial assemblies, combined product, rework bins. If under-cooked product was mixed with other batches, the hold covers those batches too.
- **Treat the assembly table as possibly contaminated.** Product that hasn't been cooked enough (e.g. egg that may carry *Salmonella*) can contaminate food-contact surfaces in the RTE assembly area. Clean and sanitize the assembly surfaces and utensils it touched before the line restarts, and record it.

### Step 2: Establish the scope of the deviation
- **Re-check the thermometer.** Do an accuracy check right away (ice point or a comparison against a reference). Two outcomes:
  - The thermometer reads correctly: the 152 °F reading is real.
  - The thermometer reads wrong: the problem may be larger. Every batch measured since the last good accuracy check is now suspect, including batches that "passed".
- **Look back through the cook log.** Find the last batch that clearly met ≥ 158 °F. Every batch after it is in scope until you rule it out.
- **Check the cook equipment.** Look at the burner or griddle setpoint, the cook time, the batch size and fill, the starting temperature of the batter, and any recent maintenance.

### Step 3: Fix the cause and restart under control
- **Fix the immediate cause.** Adjust the setpoint, repair the equipment, or retrain the operator.
- **Before restarting, confirm the next batches meet ≥ 158 °F.** Consider measuring more often for a while (e.g. every batch plus a supervisor check measurement).
- **Run a root-cause analysis** so it doesn't happen again. Ask:
  - Was there an **operating limit** (e.g. 163 to 165 °F) that would have triggered an adjustment before the critical limit was missed?
  - Was the measuring point the coldest spot or the thickest part?
  - Was the batch size within the validated range?
  - Was the operator trained?
  - Should the plan say that product can't move to assembly until the cook-log reading is recorded? That last question matters most here: the batch shouldn't have reached assembly before the temperature reading was confirmed.

### Step 4: Evaluate the product's safety and decide what happens to it
Evaluate all affected food. Your options are listed from most to least protective:

| Option | When it fits |
|---|---|
| **Re-cook** to the validated critical limit | Only if re-cooking assembled product is validated and practical, and doesn't create new hazards (e.g. scorched or uneven product, allergen cross-contact from mixed components). |
| **Divert** to a use with a validated kill step | If such an outlet exists, with documentation. |
| **Destroy** | When neither of the above is possible. This is usually the simplest and safest choice for a single batch. |
| **Release** | Only with a scientific basis that the batch is safe. See the caution below. |

> **Caution about releasing on test results.** The critical limit is the validated value for lethality against the target vegetative pathogens. 152 °F instantaneous is below it. You would need a process authority or validation data to show the process actually delivered enough lethality, e.g. that time at temperature was enough. A negative *Salmonella* test on a sample is **not** enough on its own to show a batch is safe, because pathogens are spread unevenly and sampling has limited statistical power. If the safety of the food can't be ensured, it can't go into commerce.

- **If any affected product has already shipped**, activate your **recall plan**. Evaluate the health risk and decide whether to notify consignees. Also check whether a **Reportable Food Registry** report is required. It is required where there's a reasonable probability of serious adverse health consequences or death.

### Step 5: Document it
Write a **corrective action record**. It should include:
- product identity and lot or batch codes, and the quantity affected;
- date and time, and who found the deviation;
- the measured value (152 °F) against the critical limit (≥ 158 °F);
- the results of the thermometer check;
- the actions taken to contain the product and fix the cause;
- the root cause found and the steps taken to prevent it happening again;
- the safety evaluation and disposition of the product, with its basis (e.g. destroyed, with quantity and method);
- the signature or initials of the person who did each step.

### Step 6: Decide whether reanalysis is needed
- **If this deviation is a one-off and the corrective action procedure already in your plan covered it, a full reanalysis isn't automatically required.** Still, record that the PCQI considered the question.
- **Reanalysis is required in these cases:**
  - the problem was **unanticipated**, meaning your plan had no written corrective action procedure for it;
  - deviations keep happening;
  - the root cause shows the **control itself is ineffective**, e.g. the validated parameters don't work at your real batch sizes.

  In those cases, apply the corrective action to the product **and** reanalyze the Food Safety Plan to decide whether you need to change it, e.g. new parameters, an operating limit, or a hold point before assembly (117.150(b)).

## 2. Correction vs. corrective action

| | **Correction** | **Corrective action** |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | A quick action to find and fix a **minor, isolated** problem, without the full corrective action procedure. | The full, written procedure applied when a preventive control isn't properly implemented, e.g. a **critical limit is missed**. |
| **Product impact** | **No direct impact on product safety.** Usually it's caught before the product is affected. | Product safety **may be affected**, so the affected food must be evaluated. |
| **What it includes** | Fixing the condition (re-clean, re-gown, put the guard back). | (1) Find and fix the problem; (2) **reduce the likelihood of recurrence** (root cause); (3) **evaluate all affected food** for safety; (4) **prevent affected food from entering commerce** if its safety can't be ensured; (5) **document**. |
| **Where it applies** | Mainly **sanitation and food allergen controls**, e.g. a food-contact surface found dirty at the pre-op inspection and re-cleaned **before production**; an employee told to gown properly. | Any preventive control where product may be affected, and **always for a missed process-control critical limit**. |
| **Records** | Lighter. Document it when appropriate (117.150(d)). Many sanitation corrections need little or no documentation. | A **corrective action record** is required, and it's subject to verification and records review. |

**How it applies to your case:** 152 °F against ≥ 158 °F at a validated cook step is a deviation from a critical limit, and the product has already moved downstream. That makes it a **corrective action** under 117.150(a). It isn't a correction. The correction exemption in 117.150(c) covers timely fixes to **sanitation and food allergen** conditions and practices, and minor, isolated problems that don't directly affect product safety. It doesn't cover an under-processed batch.

> Tip: an **operating limit** set tighter than the critical limit (e.g. a 163 °F target against the 158 °F CL) lets the operator adjust the process (a correction, at most) before a critical-limit deviation happens. That keeps you out of corrective-action territory.

## 3. Verification and record timelines under Part 117

| Activity | Requirement | Applied to this event |
|---|---|---|
| **Records review of monitoring and corrective action records** (117.165(a)(4)) | By, or under the oversight of, a **PCQI**, within **7 working days** of the record being created (or a reasonable timeframe justified in writing). | Review and sign the cook log for today and the corrective action record within 7 working days. Check that the actions were appropriate and complete. |
| **Verification that corrective actions were taken** (117.165(a)(2)) | Required. | The PCQI confirms the disposition happened, e.g. destruction was witnessed or documented. Also confirm the root-cause fixes were put in place. |
| **Calibration and accuracy checks** (117.165(a)(3)) | As your plan specifies. The FSPCA model uses a **daily accuracy check and annual calibration** of the cook thermometer. | Record today's extra accuracy check. If the thermometer was off, take it out of service, recalibrate or replace it, and widen the product evaluation. |
| **Review of calibration, product testing and EM records** (117.165(a)(4)(ii)) | Within a **reasonable time** after the records are created. | Include any product or environmental test results from this event, e.g. extra *Listeria*/EM swabs of the assembly area if your EM program calls for them. |
| **Validation** (117.160) | Required for process controls. Done before implementation, or within **90 days** of production starting. | If the root cause raises doubt about the validated parameters, revalidate as part of reanalysis. |
| **Reanalysis** (117.170) | At least every **3 years**. Also required after an unanticipated problem, when a control is found ineffective, or after a significant change. Complete it before the change takes effect, or within **90 days** of production with written justification. | Required if this was unanticipated or the control proved ineffective; otherwise the PCQI documents the decision. |
| **Record content** (117.305) | Original records made at the time of observation. Each must include the facility name and location, the date (and time where appropriate), the signature or initials of the person doing the activity, and product identity and lot code where applicable. Records must be accurate and indelible. | Don't backfill or rewrite the cook log. Correct errors with a single line through them, plus initials and date. |
| **Signature on records review** | The reviewer signs or initials and dates the review. | Required on today's cook log and the corrective action record. |
| **Retention** (117.315) | At least **2 years** after the date the record was created. Validation and scientific studies are kept for 2 years after their use is discontinued. | Keep the corrective action record, cook log and verification records for at least 2 years. |
| **Availability** (117.320) | The Food Safety Plan stays **onsite**. Other records can be offsite if they can be retrieved within **24 hours** of an official request. Records must be promptly available to FDA. | Keep the records ready for inspection, since FDA may ask about deviations. |

## 4. Quick checklist for today

- [ ] Batch on hold, tagged, and quantity confirmed
- [ ] Commingled or downstream product identified and held
- [ ] Assembly surfaces cleaned and sanitized, and recorded
- [ ] Thermometer accuracy check done and recorded
- [ ] Cook log reviewed back to the last good reading
- [ ] Equipment or process fixed, and the next batches confirmed ≥ 158 °F
- [ ] Root cause identified, and recurrence prevention assigned
- [ ] Product evaluated and disposition documented (destroy, divert, or validated re-cook)
- [ ] Shipments checked. Recall plan or Reportable Food Registry triggered if needed
- [ ] Corrective action record completed
- [ ] PCQI review of the records within 7 working days
- [ ] Reanalysis decision documented

---

**Caveats**
- This is based on the FSPCA *Preventive Controls for Human Food* curriculum (2016) and 21 CFR Part 117. Your own written corrective action procedures and validation study govern the specifics.
- Release decisions for under-processed product should be made by your PCQI with process authority or validation support. This guidance isn't legal advice.
- If the product contains meat or poultry above USDA-FSIS thresholds, or is an egg product under FSIS jurisdiction, FSIS HACCP rules (9 CFR 417) apply instead.
