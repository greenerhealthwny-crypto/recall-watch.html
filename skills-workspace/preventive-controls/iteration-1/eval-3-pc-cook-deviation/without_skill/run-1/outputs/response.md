# Cook-Step Critical Limit Deviation (152 F vs. ≥158 F) — What 21 CFR Part 117 Requires

## Bottom line

A monitoring reading of 152 F against a critical limit of ≥158 F means the process preventive control was **not properly implemented** for that batch. Under 21 CFR 117.150(a) this calls for a **corrective action**. A simple "correction" is not enough, because the deviation hit a process control and product has already moved downstream. You must:

1. Identify and correct the problem.
2. Take steps to reduce the chance it happens again.
3. Evaluate all affected food for safety.
4. Keep affected food out of commerce unless you can show it is not adulterated.
5. Document all of it, and have the records verified (reviewed) within **7 working days**.

---

## 1. Immediate steps (today)

**a. Stop and contain**
- Place the under-cooked batch on **hold**. Do the same for **everything it touched in assembly**: assembled product, partially assembled components, rework, and anything packed since the batch arrived.
- Use tags or segregate the product physically, and lock it in your inventory system if you can.
- Set boundaries using lot codes, timestamps, and line records. Ask what else went through assembly after this batch arrived. If under-cooked product could have contaminated food-contact surfaces, product from later batches on that line may also be implicated.
- Check whether any affected product has **shipped**. If it has, go to step 1e.

**b. Fix the process**
- Find out why the cook fell short. Common causes are oven or fryer setpoint or belt speed, a loading or batch-size change, product going in colder or thicker than usual, burner or steam faults, a door left open, or a mis-set recipe.
- Before the next batch, confirm the cook step consistently meets ≥158 F, and keep monitoring frequency at or above normal until it is stable.
- **Check the thermometer.** Do a calibration or accuracy check on the probe (ice point or against a reference). Also confirm the operator measured **surface** temperature the way your validated procedure says, since surface readings depend heavily on technique and timing. If the thermometer reads accurately, the reading stands. If it reads inaccurately, every recent reading taken with it is suspect, which can widen the scope rather than narrow it.

**c. Clean and sanitize**
- If under-cooked product went onto RTE or post-lethality assembly surfaces, clean and sanitize the assembly line and tools before you restart RTE production.
- Consider extra environmental monitoring (e.g., *Listeria* spp. swabs on zones 1–3) if your hazard analysis identifies an environmental pathogen for this area.

**d. Evaluate the affected food for safety (117.150(a)(2)(ii))**
A Preventive Controls Qualified Individual (PCQI), or someone under their oversight, should make the disposition decision based on your validation and the hazard (e.g., *Salmonella*, *Listeria monocytogenes*, pathogenic *E. coli*). Options, roughly in order of preference:
- **Reprocess**: re-cook to meet the critical limit, if the product form allows. Re-cooking a separated component may be possible. Assembled product often cannot be fully reprocessed.
- **Divert** to a use where a later validated lethality step will occur. This must be documented and under control.
- **Destroy or reject.**
- **Release after a scientific evaluation**: only if a qualified person can show the food is not adulterated. Examples are a validated time-temperature equivalency showing the actual thermal history still gave the required log reduction, or input from a process authority. **Product testing alone is a weak basis for release.** Pathogens are unevenly distributed and a negative sample does not prove the lot is safe. If you use testing, use a statistically sound sampling plan, treat it as supporting evidence only, and hold product until results are in.

Do not release product based only on "it was close" (152 vs. 158). The critical limit is the validated boundary. Being below it means you cannot rely on the validation.

**e. If affected product has left your control**
- Start your **recall plan** (21 CFR 117.139). Trace the product, notify consignees, and contact FDA as appropriate.
- If there is a reasonable probability the food could cause serious adverse health consequences or death, you must submit a **Reportable Food Registry** report within **24 hours** (FD&C Act section 417).

## 2. Correction vs. corrective action

| | **Correction** | **Corrective action** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition (117.3) | An action to identify and correct a problem that occurred during the production of food, **without** the other actions associated with a corrective action procedure. | The full set of actions in 117.150(a): identify and correct the problem, **reduce the likelihood of recurrence**, **evaluate all affected food for safety**, and **prevent affected food from entering commerce**. |
| When it is enough | Only in the limited situations in 117.150(c): timely fixes of conditions or practices that don't match **food allergen controls** (e.g., product-contact surfaces not cleaned) or **sanitation controls** (food-contact surface cleanliness, cross-contact or cross-contamination prevention), and minor, isolated problems that do not directly affect product safety. | Required for a **process control** deviation, including any failure to meet a critical limit. |
| Records | Document when appropriate (117.150(d)). | **Always** documented (117.150(d)). |

**What this means for your case:** Re-cooking the next batch correctly is a correction. It is not the whole answer. Because the critical limit on a process control was missed and product moved on, you need the **full corrective action**: root cause, a fix that prevents recurrence, safety evaluation and disposition of all affected food (including assembled product), and documentation.

**If the cause was unanticipated** (117.150(b)): suppose the deviation is not covered by your written corrective action procedures, or the problem shows a control is ineffective. Then you must also:
- take corrective action to identify and correct the problem, reduce recurrence, evaluate affected food, and keep it out of commerce;
- **decide whether the food safety plan needs reanalysis** (117.170) and, if it does, reanalyze and modify the plan. Examples include a new raw material size, a new oven profile, or a monitoring method that cannot reliably catch a low surface temperature.

## 3. Verification requirements (117.155 / 117.165)

| Activity | Requirement | Timeline |
|---|---|---|
| **Records review: monitoring and corrective action records** | A PCQI, or someone under their oversight, reviews the cook monitoring records and the corrective action records for this event. The review confirms they are complete, the activities followed the written procedures, appropriate decisions were made about corrective actions, and the record is signed or initialed and dated. | **Within 7 working days** after the records are created. A longer interval is allowed only with a written justification in the food safety plan (117.165(a)(4)(i)). |
| **Calibration / accuracy checks** of the thermometer used for monitoring | Calibrate or check accuracy, and record it (117.165(a)(1)). Do an extra check now because of the deviation. | At the frequency in your plan. Calibration records are reviewed **within a reasonable time** after creation (117.165(a)(4)(ii)). |
| **Product testing / environmental monitoring** | As appropriate to the facility, food, and preventive control (117.165(a)(2)–(3)). Relevant here if you test held product or swab assembly after a possible contamination event. | Per your written procedures. Review results within a reasonable time. |
| **Validation** | If the root cause shows the cook step or critical limit is not adequate, or you change the process, revalidate (117.160). | Before the change is implemented, or within 90 calendar days of first production (or a justified longer period). |
| **Reanalysis** | Required when a preventive control or combination of controls proves ineffective, when an unanticipated problem occurs, or when there is new information about hazards (117.170). | Complete **within 90 calendar days** after production of the food first begins (or with written justification), and before any change is implemented if practical. |

The verification review is not a rubber stamp. The reviewer should confirm that the disposition of the affected food was scientifically sound, that the root cause and recurrence prevention are reasonable, and that the scope (which lots and which product) was set correctly.

## 4. Records (117.190, 117.301–117.335)

**Records to create for this event:**
- The **monitoring record** showing the 152 F reading, with date, time, product, lot, and operator initials. Never change or overwrite this entry.
- The **corrective action record**: the problem, root cause, actions taken to fix it and prevent recurrence, affected lots and quantities, the safety evaluation and its basis, the final disposition (reprocess, divert, destroy, or release), and who decided.
- **Calibration or accuracy check** records for the thermometer.
- Any **product testing or environmental monitoring** results.
- The **verification record**: the PCQI or designee's review, signed or initialed and dated.
- If applicable, **reanalysis** documentation, an updated food safety plan signed and dated by the owner, operator, or agent in charge (117.310), any **validation** records, and **recall** records.
- **Training** records showing the operator and the PCQI are qualified (117.4, 117.180).

**Record requirements (117.305):**
- Kept as original records, true copies, or electronic records (Part 11 does not apply to records required by Part 117; see 117.305(g)).
- Contain **actual values and observations** from monitoring. Enter "152 F", not "fail" or "OK".
- Accurate, indelible, and legible.
- **Created at the time the activity is performed.**
- As detailed as needed to show the history of the work.
- Include the plant name and location, the date and time of the activity, the signature or initials of the person doing it, and, where appropriate, the product identity and lot code.

**Retention and access (117.315, 117.320):**
- Keep records for **at least 2 years** after the date they were prepared.
- Records can be stored **offsite after 6 months** from preparation if they can be retrieved within **24 hours** of a request. The **food safety plan must stay onsite**. Electronic records accessible from an onsite location count as onsite.
- Records must be available to FDA for **official review and copying on oral or written request**.

## 5. Quick checklist

- [ ] Hold the batch plus all assembled or co-mingled product. Determine lot scope.
- [ ] Confirm whether anything shipped. Recall plan and RFR (24 h) if needed.
- [ ] Check thermometer calibration and the operator's measurement technique.
- [ ] Root cause and fix. Confirm the cook meets ≥158 F before restart.
- [ ] Clean and sanitize assembly. Consider environmental monitoring.
- [ ] PCQI safety evaluation and documented disposition (reprocess, divert, destroy, or justified release).
- [ ] Measures to prevent recurrence (training, equipment, procedure, alarms).
- [ ] Decide whether reanalysis or revalidation is needed (117.150(b), 117.170, 117.160).
- [ ] Complete the corrective action record. Monitoring record shows the actual value.
- [ ] Verification review of monitoring and corrective action records **within 7 working days**.
- [ ] Retain records **2 years**. Keep them retrievable within 24 h.

*This walkthrough reflects the general requirements of 21 CFR Part 117, Subpart C. Your written food safety plan's specific corrective action procedures, and your PCQI's judgment about the hazards you identified, should control the details. Consult FDA guidance or a process authority if you are unsure whether the product can be released.*
