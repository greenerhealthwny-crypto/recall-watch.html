# Is Your 1998 Case Still Good Law, and Does It Bind Your Court?

*Based on the general approach taught in Nolo's* Legal Research: How to Find & Understand the Law *(Elias & Levinkind), written from memory. Check the current edition for exact wording and page references.*

Finding a case that supports you is only the first step. Before you rely on it, answer two separate questions:

1. **Is it still good law?** Has it been overruled, reversed, or weakened since 1998?
2. **Is it binding authority in your court, or only persuasive?**

---

## Part 1: Is the case still "good law"?

A case from 1998 has had more than 25 years for things to happen to it. Any of these can take away its value:

- **Reversed:** a higher court in the *same* case changed the result on appeal.
- **Overruled:** a later case in a court with authority over it (or the same court) rejected its rule.
- **Superseded by statute:** the legislature changed the law the case interpreted.
- **Depublished or review granted:** in some states, such as California, an appellate opinion can be depublished, or review can be granted, which may leave it uncitable.
- **Criticized, limited, or distinguished:** the case still stands, but later courts have narrowed it.

### How to check: "Shepardize" or cite-check the case

Nolo's book teaches the traditional method, **Shepardizing**: you look up your case in *Shepard's Citations* and get a list of every later case (and some secondary sources) that cited it, with a code showing what each one did to it.

**Ways to do it today:**

- **Shepard's on Lexis** (Lexis+ or Lexis Advance). This is the modern Shepard's. Lexis also puts colored signals on cases (see below).
- **KeyCite on Westlaw.** This is Westlaw's equivalent, with red and yellow flags.
- **A law library.** Many county, court, and law school libraries give the public free access to Lexis or Westlaw on library terminals, and some still keep the print Shepard's volumes. Ask a law librarian. Nolo strongly recommends this.
- **Free tools** such as Google Scholar's "How cited," CourtListener, and Casetext-style citators. These help you find later cases, but they are **not a substitute** for Shepard's or KeyCite because they do not reliably flag negative treatment.

### Steps (Nolo-style)

1. Get the case's **full citation**: volume, reporter, page, court, and year (for example, `123 Cal. App. 4th 456 (1998)`). Use the official reporter citation, not just a website URL.
2. Run it through Shepard's or KeyCite, or look it up in the print Shepard's volumes and **every later supplement** (the paper pamphlets update the bound books, so you must check them all).
3. **Check the case history first.** Was this decision appealed, reversed, or modified?
4. **Look for negative treatment** by later courts: overruled, questioned, criticized, limited.
5. **Read the citing cases yourself.** A code is only a hint. Pull any case marked negative and read what it actually says. Pay attention to whether it dealt with the **same point of law** you are relying on. Many cases have several holdings, and a case can be overruled on one issue and still be good law on another. Shepard's headnote numbers help you match citing cases to the specific point.
6. **Look for newer, stronger authority.** The citing cases often include more recent decisions that state the same rule. Citing those as well makes your argument stronger.
7. **Check statutes.** If the case interprets a statute, confirm the statute has not been amended or repealed since 1998, using the annotated code and its pocket parts.

## Part 2: What the Shepard's codes mean

### Case history codes (the same case, going up or down on appeal)

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **a** (affirmed) | A higher court affirmed the decision in the same case. |
| **r** (reversed) | A higher court reversed the decision in the same case. **Danger.** |
| **m** (modified) | The decision was changed in some way on appeal. |
| **s** (same case) | The same case at another stage of the litigation. |
| **S** (superseded) | A later opinion in the same case replaced this one. |
| **v** (vacated) | The decision was vacated (set aside). **Danger.** |
| **D** (dismissed) | The appeal was dismissed. |
| **cert den / US cert den** | The U.S. Supreme Court declined review. This is generally *not* an endorsement of the decision. |
| **cert gran** | The Supreme Court agreed to review the case. Find out what happened next. |
| **reh den / reh gran** | Rehearing was denied or granted. |

### Treatment codes (how *other* later cases treated it)

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **o** (overruled) | A later court said the ruling is no longer law. **Do not rely on it**, at least on that point. |
| **q** (questioned) | A later court questioned whether the ruling is valid. **Warning.** |
| **c** (criticized) | A later court disagreed with the reasoning. **Warning.** |
| **L** (limited) | A later court refused to extend the ruling beyond its exact facts. |
| **d** (distinguished) | A later court said its facts were different, so the rule did not apply. This often does no harm, but read it to see if your facts look more like the later case. |
| **e** (explained) | A later court interpreted or clarified the ruling. |
| **f** (followed) | A later court relied on it as controlling. **Good sign.** |
| **h** (harmonized) | A later court reconciled an apparent conflict with another case. |
| **j** (dissenting opinion) | Cited in a dissent. |
| **~** (concurring opinion) | Cited in a concurrence. |
| **ψ** (psi) | The citing case was decided on other grounds, so the citation may matter less. |

A citing case that has **no letter** just cited your case without a specific treatment. Most citations look like this. Shepard's also gives **small superscript numbers**: these are headnote numbers that tie each citing reference to a particular point of law in your case.

### Signals in Shepard's online (Lexis)

| Signal | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Red stop sign** | Strong negative treatment (overruled or reversed). |
| **Red "Pr"** | Strong negative treatment of a statute. |
| **Orange "Q"** | Validity questioned. |
| **Yellow triangle** | Possible negative treatment (criticized, limited, distinguished). |
| **Green diamond with a "+"** | Positive treatment (followed, affirmed). |
| **Blue "A"** | Analyzed or cited, neutral. |
| **Blue "I"** | Citing references exist, but no history or treatment analysis. |

**KeyCite (Westlaw) equivalents:** a **red flag** means no longer good law on at least one point, a **yellow flag** means some negative history, and a **blue-striped flag** means an appeal is pending.

A red or yellow signal does **not** always mean you can't use the case. It could be negative on a different issue. That is why you have to read the citing cases.

## Part 3: Does the case bind your court?

**Mandatory (binding) authority** means your court *must* follow it. **Persuasive authority** means your court *may* consider it but is not required to follow it.

### The basic rules

Two things decide whether a case binds you: **which court decided it** and **which court you are in**.

1. **Same court system.** State law generally binds only courts in that state. A California appellate case does not bind a Texas court, although the Texas court may find it persuasive.
2. **Higher court in your line.** Decisions of courts *above* yours bind you. Your state supreme court binds every court in your state. A court is generally not bound by courts at the same level or below.
3. **Intermediate appellate courts vary by state:**
   - In some states (California is the usual example), a published decision of **any** court of appeal binds **all** trial courts in the state. If appellate districts conflict, the trial court may choose between them.
   - In other states, a trial court is bound only by the appellate district or division it sits in. Decisions from other districts are persuasive.
   - One appellate panel usually does not bind another panel or district in the same state. Practice varies, so check your state.
4. **Federal courts:**
   - U.S. Supreme Court decisions bind all federal courts, and they bind state courts on questions of federal law.
   - A federal **circuit court** binds the district courts *within that circuit*. A 9th Circuit case does not bind a court in the 5th Circuit.
   - State courts generally are **not** bound by lower federal courts, even on federal questions, though they often find them persuasive.
   - When a federal court applies **state** law (in a diversity case), the state's highest court is the final word.
5. **Published vs. unpublished.** Many courts treat unpublished opinions as non-precedential, and some court rules limit or forbid citing them. Cases "found online" are sometimes unpublished, so check this.
6. **Holding vs. dicta.** Only the **holding**, meaning the part of the decision needed to resolve the issues before the court, is binding. Comments that weren't needed (dicta), concurrences, and dissents are at most persuasive.
7. **Subject matter.** A case binds you on the legal issue it actually decided. Make sure your facts and issue are close enough.

### Quick checklist for your 1998 case

- [ ] What court decided it: state or federal, which state or circuit, which district?
- [ ] What court are you in? Is the deciding court above yours in the same system?
- [ ] Was it **published**, and can it be cited under your court's rules?
- [ ] Does the part you rely on come from the **holding**, not dicta?
- [ ] Has it been **Shepardized or KeyCited** with no reversal or overruling on your point?
- [ ] Has a **higher court**, or a statute change, since 1998 changed the rule?
- [ ] Are there **newer cases** stating the same rule that you can cite alongside it?

If the case is persuasive only (for example, from another state), you can still cite it. Present it as persuasive and look for binding authority from your own jurisdiction that agrees with it.

## Practical tips from Nolo's approach

- **Don't trust the website copy alone.** Get the official citation and confirm the text in an official reporter or a reliable database.
- **Ask a law librarian.** They can show you how to Shepardize in minutes, and many public law libraries offer free Lexis or Westlaw access.
- **Check your court's local rules** on citing cases, especially unpublished ones.
- **Citing bad law hurts your credibility** with the judge, and opposing counsel will check. Always cite-check right before you file.
- If the stakes are significant, consider a limited-scope consultation with a lawyer to confirm the case still works for your situation.

*This is general legal-research information, not legal advice for your situation.*
