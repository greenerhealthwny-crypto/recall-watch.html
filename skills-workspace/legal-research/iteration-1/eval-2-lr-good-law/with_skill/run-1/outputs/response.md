# Is your 1998 appellate case still good law, and does it bind your court?

Finding a case that supports you is a good start. Nolo's *Legal Research* (19th ed., 2021) warns that **"just because a source is online doesn't mean it holds legal weight."** A 1998 case has had more than 25 years to be reversed, overruled, limited by later cases, or replaced by a new statute. Before you rely on it, answer two separate questions:

1. **Does it bind my court?** (This is about the court hierarchy.)
2. **Is it still good law?** (This is about what has happened to the case since 1998.)

---

## Part 1: Does the case bind your court?

### Step 1. Identify which court decided it

The citation tells you. Look at the reporter abbreviation and the parenthetical at the end. For example, "(9th Cir. 1998)" is a federal circuit court, and "Cal. App. 4th" is a California intermediate appellate court. Then work out whether it's a **state** or **federal** court, and **which** appellate court or circuit.

### Step 2. Apply the book's rules on binding versus persuasive authority

| Situation | Binding or persuasive? |
|---|---|
| A published opinion from an appellate court **above your court in the same system** (for example, your state's court of appeal and you're in a trial court in that state) | **Binding.** A published appellate decision binds the courts below it in that system. |
| A state with **only one intermediate appellate ruling** on the issue | That ruling is generally followed statewide until other appellate courts rule on it. Check how your state handles conflicts between appellate districts. |
| A **federal circuit** case | Binds only the district courts **in that circuit**. For example, a Fifth Circuit case binds federal courts in Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi. In the Eleventh Circuit it is only persuasive. |
| A **different state's** court | **Persuasive only.** Courts sometimes rely on out-of-state cases (the book's example is a Colorado court citing cases from Hawaii, Texas and Virginia), but they don't have to follow them. |
| **State and federal courts** | They generally **don't bind each other**. One exception: a state court deciding a *federal* claim must follow federal law. |
| A **trial court** decision | Trial courts generally don't issue binding opinions. |

### Step 3. Make sure the part you rely on is the holding

Only some of an opinion is binding:
- **The holding** of the **majority** (or a plurality) is the law.
- **Dicta**, meaning statements that weren't needed to decide the case, are **not binding**. The book's one exception is the U.S. Supreme Court, where every word is weighed.
- **Concurring and dissenting opinions** are not binding, although they can be persuasive.
- **Headnotes and the synopsis** at the top of the case were added by the publisher. They are **not law** and "tend to overly simplify issues", so quote the opinion itself, never a headnote.

Also check whether the opinion was **published**. Unpublished opinions often carry limited or no precedential weight. Your court's rules will say whether you can cite them *(std, verify your court's rules)*.

### Step 4. Check that the case really fits your issue

The book's method for deciding whether a case helps you:
1. Identify the **precise issues** the court decided and compare them with yours. If none match, the case probably won't help.
2. Check whether its ruling on those issues **logically applies to your facts**.
3. Check whether the court is **binding on the court hearing your matter**. If it isn't, treat the case as persuasive.
4. Check whether it is **still good law** (Part 2).

Expect your opponent to try to **distinguish** the case, meaning they'll argue that your facts or the law involved are different. The book notes that it is "much easier to get a court to 'distinguish' an old case than openly overrule it." Be ready to explain why the differences don't matter.

## Part 2: Is it still good law? Shepardize it (or KeyCite it)

The book says **"Shepardizing is every bit as important as finding a case or statute."** Shepardizing does two jobs:
- It confirms the case **hasn't been overruled, reversed or otherwise changed**.
- It **finds later cases** that cite yours. Those cases are leads to more, and more recent, law on your issue.

### How to do it

**Online (fastest).** Use **Shepard's on Lexis** or **KeyCite on Westlaw**. Both update almost immediately and link to every citing case. Westlaw also flags cases that are at risk of being overruled.

**Free.** Free sites like Google Scholar, Justia and FindLaw let you read cases, but they don't reliably tell you whether a case has been overruled. (VersusLaw lists citing cases but gives no analysis of how they treated the case.) For a free Shepard's or KeyCite check, **go to a local law library**: law school, state or county libraries often give the public free access to Lexis or Westlaw. Call first to confirm public access. Librarians can show you how to run the check.

**In print (law library).** Following the book's steps:
1. Write down the case's full citation (volume, reporter, page).
2. Find the Shepard's volumes for that reporter.
3. Use the right section and the right time period.
4. Find the volume number, then the page number in bold.
5. Read the list of citing references and their letter codes.
6. **Check every supplement**, so nothing after 1998 is missed.

### What to look for: two kinds of information

**A. The case's own history** (what happened to *this* case on further appeal):
- **Affirmed**: a higher court agreed. Good.
- **Reversed** or **vacated**: a higher court undid it. Don't rely on the decision you found.
- **Modified**: part of it changed. Check that *your* point survived.
- **Supreme Court action**: for example, **review denied** means the higher court declined to hear the case, so the decision stands as good law.

**B. Treatment by later, unrelated cases**:

| Code | Meaning | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| **o** (overruled) | A later court overruled it | **No longer valid.** Don't rely on it. |
| **su** (superseded by statute) | A statute replaced the rule | The rule is gone. Find and use the new statute. |
| **q** (questioned) | Its validity is doubted | Risky. Read the questioning case. |
| **c** (criticized) | Another court disagreed | Shows the arguments your opponent may use. |
| **d** (distinguished) | A similar case was decided differently because its facts or law differed | Read it. It may be closer to your facts than your case is. |
| **e** (explained) | The case is clarified | Read it for how courts understand the rule. |
| **f** (followed) | A later court applied the rule | **Helpful.** Cite the later cases too, especially recent ones. |
| **j** (dissenting opinion) | Cited in a dissent | Not binding. |
| **~** (concurring opinion) | Cited in a concurrence | Not binding. |

### Online signals (Lexis Shepard's)

If you use Lexis, the codes appear as colored signals:
- **Red**: strong negative treatment, such as overruled or reversed.
- **Orange**: validity questioned.
- **Yellow**: possible negative treatment, such as distinguished or criticized.
- **Green**: positive treatment, such as affirmed or followed.
- **Blue "A"**: neutral analysis available.
- **Blue "I"**: citation information only, for example law review articles.

*(The book lists these meanings. Confirm the exact colors on the current Lexis screen.)* A signal is only a summary, so **always read the negative cases yourself**. A case can be overruled on one point and still be good law on another. A yellow flag may only mean another court distinguished it on different facts.

### Don't forget the statute angle

The book points out that a legislature can **undo case law with a new statute** (unless the case interpreted the Constitution). Its example is the Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act, passed after a Supreme Court ruling. If your 1998 case interprets a statute:
- Shepardize the **statute** too, to find amendments and repeals.
- Check the **pocket part** (or the online update) of the annotated code.
- Check the **official code website**.

Look for "su" (superseded by statute) in the case's Shepard's report.

## Part 3: Make your argument stronger

- **Find newer cases.** The book advises preferring recent cases. Look at the citing cases marked "f" (followed), especially ones from the court that binds yours. A 2020s case from your own appellate court that follows the 1998 case is far more persuasive than the 1998 case alone.
- **Use the headnote key number** (West reporters) and a **digest** to find more cases on the same point. Always check the digest's pocket part.
- **Stick with existing law.** The book notes that trial judges almost never disregard precedent (*stare decisis*). A binding, still-good case is your best tool. Arguing for a change in the law usually has to happen on appeal.

---

## Quick checklist

- [ ] What court decided the case (state or federal, which appellate court or circuit)?
- [ ] Is that court above my court in the same system? If so, it's **binding**. If not, it's **persuasive** only.
- [ ] Was the opinion published? Is my point in the **holding**, not dicta, a dissent or a headnote?
- [ ] Do the issues and facts really match mine?
- [ ] Shepardized or KeyCited: no reversal, no "o", no "su"? Did I read every "q", "c" and "d" case?
- [ ] If it interprets a statute: is the statute still in the same form (pocket part, official code site)?
- [ ] Did I find recent cases that **follow** it?
