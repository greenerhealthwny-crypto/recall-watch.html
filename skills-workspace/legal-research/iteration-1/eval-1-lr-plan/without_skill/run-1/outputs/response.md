# Researching Your Ohio Security Deposit Claim, the Nolo *Legal Research* Way

Nolo's *Legal Research: How to Find & Understand the Law* (Elias & Levinkind) teaches a step-by-step method. You start broad with background reading, then find the primary law (statutes, then cases), read it closely, and check that it is still current. Here is that method applied to your situation.

> This is a research roadmap, not legal advice. Check every citation below against a current official source before you rely on it in court.

---

## Step 1: Define your issue and put it in a category

Nolo suggests writing your question down in plain words, then sorting it into a legal category so you know where to look.

- **Plain question:** "My landlord kept my entire security deposit after I moved out. Did they have the right to, and what can I recover?"
- **Category:** Civil law, then landlord-tenant law, then security deposits. This is **state law**, plus possibly local ordinances. It is not federal law.
- **Facts you need to pin down** (these decide which rules apply):
  - Your move-out date, and the date you handed back the keys (gave up possession)
  - Whether and when you gave the landlord your **forwarding address in writing**
  - Whether you got **an itemized list of deductions**, and when
  - The deposit amount, your monthly rent, and how long you lived there
  - What your lease says about the deposit, cleaning and damage
  - Your evidence: move-in and move-out photos, checklists, receipts, texts and emails

**Build a word list** for searching indexes and databases: *security deposit, rental deposit, return of deposit, wrongful withholding, itemized deductions, landlord and tenant, damages, attorney fees, forwarding address, normal wear and tear.*

---

## Step 2: Get background from secondary sources

Nolo's main advice is to **start with secondary sources** (plain-English guides, practice manuals, encyclopedias) before you read statutes and cases. They tell you what the law is in general, use the vocabulary you need, and usually cite the controlling statute.

Good starting points:
- **Nolo's *Every Tenant's Legal Guide*** and Nolo's free online articles on security deposits. Nolo also keeps a state-by-state chart of deposit rules.
- **Ohio Legal Help** (ohiolegalhelp.org): plain-language guides and forms for Ohio tenants, including sample deposit demand letters.
- **The Ohio Attorney General's** consumer and tenant publications, and the **Ohio State Bar Association's** "Law You Can Use" pieces.
- **Your local legal aid society** and **court self-help centers**. Many municipal courts publish small claims guides.
- **Legal encyclopedias and treatises** at a public or county law library, such as *Ohio Jurisprudence 3d* (the "Landlord and Tenant" title) and Ohio landlord-tenant practice manuals. A **law librarian** can point you to the right volume. Nolo strongly recommends asking librarians for help.

**What to look for:** the name and citation of the statute, the deadline for returning the deposit, what counts as a proper deduction, and what penalties a landlord faces.

---

## Step 3: Find the primary law, starting with the statute

The background sources should lead you to the **Ohio Landlord-Tenant Act, Ohio Revised Code Chapter 5321**. The key section is:

- **R.C. 5321.16, Security deposits.** In summary (check this against the current text):
  - **(A)** Any deposit over $50 or one month's rent, whichever is greater, earns **interest at 5% a year** if you stayed **six months or more**. Interest is computed and paid yearly.
  - **(B)** Within **30 days** after the tenancy ends and you give up possession, the landlord must **return the deposit** or give you an **itemized written list of deductions** (past-due rent and damages caused by your failure to meet your duties, for example under R.C. 5321.05), together with whatever money is left. The notice goes to the forwarding address you provided in writing.
  - **(C)** If the landlord wrongfully withholds any part of the deposit, you can recover **the money wrongfully withheld, plus damages equal to that amount** (which effectively doubles it), **plus reasonable attorney fees**. One catch: if you **did not give a written forwarding address**, you may not get the double damages and fees. Read this subsection carefully.
- Related sections to read:
  - **R.C. 5321.01** (definitions)
  - **R.C. 5321.04 and 5321.05** (landlord and tenant duties, which bear on what counts as legitimate "damage")
  - **R.C. 5321.13** (lease terms that can't waive your rights under the chapter)

**Where to find it:**
- Free: **codes.ohio.gov**, the official online Ohio Revised Code.
- Better, at a law library: an **annotated code**, either *Page's Ohio Revised Code Annotated* or *Baldwin's Ohio Revised Code Annotated*. Annotations list summaries of cases that interpret each section, which leads you straight into Step 5. Nolo emphasizes using annotated codes for exactly this reason.

---

## Step 4: Read the statute closely

Nolo advises reading the statute several times, word by word, and breaking it into parts:
- **Who** must act (the landlord), **when** (within 30 days of termination *and* delivery of possession), **what** (return the money or provide an itemized notice), and **how** (by mail or delivery to your written forwarding address).
- Note the defined terms and look them up in R.C. 5321.01.
- Pay attention to words like "shall" (mandatory) and "may."
- Check **cross-references** (for example, to R.C. 5321.05) and read those sections too.
- Make a checklist of each **element** you'll have to prove, and note which facts and evidence support each one.

---

## Step 5: Find cases that interpret the statute

Statutes rarely answer every question. Cases tell you how Ohio courts actually apply R.C. 5321.16, for example: what "wrongful withholding" means, what counts as normal wear and tear, whether a vague notice counts as "itemized," and how double damages are calculated.

**How to find them:**
- Use the **case annotations** under R.C. 5321.16 in the annotated code.
- Use free databases: **Google Scholar** (choose "Case law" and select Ohio courts), **CourtListener**, **Casetext/vLex alternatives**, and the **Supreme Court of Ohio's website** (supremecourt.ohio.gov), which posts opinions from the Ohio Supreme Court and the courts of appeals. Search terms: `"5321.16" wrongful withholding`, `"5321.16" itemized`, `"5321.16" forwarding address`.
- Many law libraries offer free public access to **Westlaw or Lexis**.

**A leading case to start with:** *Smith v. Padgett*, 32 Ohio St.3d 344 (1987). The Ohio Supreme Court discussed how R.C. 5321.16 works, including that the landlord's liability for double damages and attorney fees covers the *wrongfully withheld* amount, and that a tenant who wins is entitled to attorney fees. Read the full opinion, not just a summary. After that:
- Prefer **Ohio Supreme Court** decisions, then decisions from **your own appellate district**. Ohio has 12 district courts of appeals, and your district's rulings carry the most weight in your local court.
- Read for the **holding**: what the court decided on facts like yours.

---

## Step 6: Update and validate everything ("Shepardize")

Nolo stresses that you must confirm the law is still good law:
- **Statutes:** check that you are reading the current version (codes.ohio.gov shows effective dates), and look for recent amendments or pending bills on the Ohio Legislature's site (legislature.ohio.gov).
- **Cases:** use a citator, either **Shepard's** (Lexis) or **KeyCite** (Westlaw), usually free at a law library, to make sure no case you rely on has been overruled or limited. On Google Scholar, the "How cited" link is a rough free substitute.

---

## Step 7: Check local law, court rules and the procedure for your court

- **Local ordinances:** some Ohio cities have extra tenant protections. Check your city's codified ordinances, which are often on American Legal Publishing or Municode.
- **Small claims procedure:**
  - **R.C. Chapter 1925** governs small claims divisions of municipal and county courts. The current limit is **$6,000**; check whether that has changed. Your claim, including double damages, has to fit within the limit, or you can waive any excess.
  - Read **your court's local rules** and small claims guide. They cover filing fees, service on the landlord, and hearing procedure.
  - Note that attorney fees under R.C. 5321.16(C) matter mostly if you hire a lawyer.
- **Your lease:** read the deposit, cleaning and notice clauses. Remember that a lease can't waive your statutory rights (R.C. 5321.13).

---

## Step 8: Organize your findings and apply them to your facts

Nolo recommends writing a short **summary or memo** for yourself:
1. **Issue:** Did the landlord wrongfully withhold the deposit under R.C. 5321.16?
2. **Rule:** Quote the statute and the key cases.
3. **Application:** Go through each element with your facts and evidence (dates, forwarding address, missing or inadequate itemization, photos showing no damage).
4. **Remedy:** List what you'll ask for: the wrongfully withheld amount, an equal amount in damages, any interest owed under 5321.16(A), and court costs.

Then:
- Send the landlord a **written demand letter** that cites R.C. 5321.16, states the amount owed and sets a deadline. Keep proof that you sent it.
- If they don't pay, **file in small claims**. Bring copies of the statute, the key cases (highlighted), your lease, your forwarding-address letter, any deduction notice, photos and receipts.

## Quick checklist
- [ ] Wrote down the issue, the key facts and the search terms
- [ ] Read background in Nolo, Ohio Legal Help or a law library treatise
- [ ] Read the current R.C. 5321.16, plus 5321.01, .04, .05 and .13
- [ ] Used an annotated code to find interpreting cases
- [ ] Read *Smith v. Padgett* and cases from my appellate district
- [ ] Shepardized or KeyCited every case, and confirmed the statute is current
- [ ] Checked local ordinances and my small claims court's rules and limit
- [ ] Organized evidence and sent a demand letter
- [ ] Filed in small claims if the landlord doesn't pay

If your situation has complications, such as a large amount, counterclaims for damage or an unclear tenancy end date, think about a free consultation with **local legal aid** or a **lawyer referral service**. Many offer low-cost initial meetings.
