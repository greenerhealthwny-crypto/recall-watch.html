# Writing a Legal Memo: Nolo's *Legal Research* (19th ed., 2021)

Chapter 9 of the book ("Organizing and putting your research to use") covers the legal memo.

## First: a memo is not a brief

- A **legal memorandum** is an internal document. You write it for yourself or your employer, and it lays out the facts and the law **neutrally**, including the weak points.
- A **brief** (also called a "memorandum of points and authorities") is filed in court and **argues one side**.

If you are writing the memo for someone else, such as a supervising lawyer or an employer, use their format and be objective about the weaknesses in the case.

## The structure

The book says a memo's parts follow the parts of a court opinion:

1. **Statement of facts.** Include only the **legally relevant** facts, the ones that affect the outcome. Leave out background that makes no legal difference.
2. **Question presented (the issue).** One short question, usually one you can answer "yes" or "no".
3. **Decision (conclusion or brief answer).** Your prediction of how a court would rule, for example "Probably not", followed by a one-line reason.
4. **Discussion (analysis).** The book calls this "really the most important part." For each issue, apply the law to your facts using **IRAC**:
   - **I: Issue.** The specific question the authority answers.
   - **R: Rule.** What the statute or case holds, with a citation.
   - **A: Analysis.** How your facts match the case, or differ from it.
   - **C: Conclusion.** What that authority means for your situation.
5. **Citations throughout.** The book calls citations "the roadmap" for the reader. Every rule you state should point to its source so the reader can check it.

The book's IRAC illustration uses a Louisiana appellate case, *Ellender v. Neff Rental, Inc.* An employee who was on a work-related call, on a phone the employer provided, was held to be acting in the course and scope of employment. That made the employer **vicariously liable** when the employee drove negligently and hit a pedestrian. The **Issue** is whether the employer is liable. The **Rule** is the case's holding. The **Analysis** compares your facts with the case's facts (Was it a work call? Whose phone was it?). The **Conclusion** follows from that comparison. Look up the full citation yourself before you rely on the case.

## Template

```
TO:
FROM:
DATE:
RE:

FACTS
(legally relevant facts only)

QUESTION PRESENTED
(one question, answerable yes or no)

BRIEF ANSWER
(probably yes / probably no, plus a one-line reason)

DISCUSSION
Issue 1:
  Issue:      ...
  Rule:       ... (citation)
  Analysis:   ... (how our facts compare)
  Conclusion: ...
(repeat for each issue)

NEXT STEPS / OPEN QUESTIONS
(what still needs research or verification)
```

## Short example

This uses the book's own landlord-tenant scenario. The book gives the question and the answer. I filled in the facts, and I put bracketed placeholders where the real citations would go, so the example doesn't present an authority I haven't checked.

> **TO:** File
> **FROM:** [Your name]
> **DATE:** [Date]
> **RE:** Whether the new building owner can evict Tenant over her dog
>
> **FACTS**
> Tenant has rented a California apartment since 2019 under a written one-year lease that later became month to month. The lease says nothing about pets. In 2020 the then-owner gave Tenant written permission to keep one dog. The building was sold in March 2024. In May 2024 the new owner served Tenant a notice to remove the dog or face eviction.
>
> **QUESTION PRESENTED**
> Can a new owner of an apartment house in California evict a tenant for having pets even though a prior landlord allowed them?
>
> **BRIEF ANSWER**
> Probably not. The new owner takes the property subject to the agreements the prior owner negotiated, including the permission to keep the dog.
>
> **DISCUSSION**
> *Issue:* Is the new owner bound by the prior owner's written permission for the dog?
> *Rule:* [State the California statute or case on a buyer's obligations under existing tenancies, with its citation.]
> *Analysis:* The permission was in writing and was given while the earlier owner held the property. Nothing suggests Tenant agreed to change it when the building was sold. [Compare these facts with the facts of the authority. Note any difference, such as an oral versus written permission or a lease clause that allows changes.]
> *Conclusion:* If the rule holds as stated, the new owner probably cannot evict on this ground without first changing the terms lawfully. For a month-to-month tenancy, that would mean proper notice of the change and compliance with any local just-cause or rent-control ordinance.
>
> **NEXT STEPS**
> - Check the city and county ordinances (for example rent control or just-cause eviction rules).
> - Shepardize or KeyCite every case cited, and check the pocket part or the official code site for each statute.

## Tips for the research behind the memo

- **Organize as you go.** Keep one folder for each research question. On each case, mark where the issue, facts, decision and rationale appear, so you can write the IRAC sections quickly.
- **Cite primary law, not secondary sources.** Encyclopedias, treatises and practice guides help you find the law, but the Rule section should cite the actual statute, regulation or case.
- **Make sure the law is still good** before you rely on it: Shepard's or KeyCite, the pocket parts, and the date on the official code website.
- **Keep to existing law.** Predict what a court would do under current precedent, not what you think the law should be.

*This is legal information drawn from the Nolo book, not legal advice. For a real dispute, especially one with a close deadline or a lot of money at stake, check with a local lawyer.*
