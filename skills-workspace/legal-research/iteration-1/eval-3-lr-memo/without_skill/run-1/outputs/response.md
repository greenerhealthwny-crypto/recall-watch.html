# Writing a Legal Memo, Following Nolo's *Legal Research*

**A note on sources:** This answer is written from memory. I don't have the book in front of me. Nolo's *Legal Research: How to Find & Understand the Law* (Stephen Elias and Susan Levinkind; later editions revised by the Nolo editors) has a chapter on writing up your research as a legal memorandum. The structure below is the standard one the book teaches. Section names and their order can differ a little between editions, so check your copy for the exact headings and its sample memo.

## Why the book says to write a memo

- **It tests your research.** If you can't explain the law clearly in writing, you probably don't understand it yet, or you haven't finished researching.
- **It keeps a record.** You can come back to it later, or hand it to a lawyer, a court clerk or the other side without starting over.
- **It's a step toward court papers.** Much of a memo can be reused in a letter, a motion or a brief.
- **It's objective, not one-sided.** A memo is for you to understand where you stand. Lay out the law that hurts you as well as the law that helps you.

## The structure

1. **Heading.** Who the memo is to and from, the date and the subject (for example, "Tenant's right to withhold rent for repairs").
2. **Statement of Facts.** The facts that matter to the legal question, stated neutrally and briefly. Leave out facts that don't affect the outcome, but include the unhelpful ones.
3. **Question(s) Presented.** The legal issue or issues, each written as one specific question that combines the law and the key facts. Number them if there is more than one.
4. **Brief Answer / Conclusion.** A short, direct answer to each question ("Probably yes, because...") so the reader knows the bottom line right away.
5. **Discussion.** The main part of the memo. For each issue:
   - State the rule from the statutes, regulations and cases you found, with citations.
   - Explain how courts have read and applied the rule. Compare the facts of key cases to yours.
   - Apply the rule to your facts.
   - Deal with counterarguments and law that goes against you.
   - Note any gaps or uncertainty, and say whether you Shepardized or KeyCited your authorities to confirm they are still good law.

   This order is essentially IRAC: Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion.
6. **Conclusion.** A short summary of where things stand, along with practical next steps or recommendations. Some short memos combine this with the Brief Answer.

**Writing tips the book stresses:** use plain English, short sentences and paragraphs, and a headed section for each issue. Cite every legal statement so it can be checked, and quote statutory language exactly when it matters.

## A short example

> **MEMORANDUM**
>
> **To:** File
> **From:** J. Researcher
> **Date:** November 21, 2026
> **Re:** Can a tenant deduct repair costs from rent? (California)
>
> **Statement of Facts**
> The tenant rents an apartment in Los Angeles for $1,500 a month. On March 1 the heater stopped working, and on March 2 the tenant gave the landlord written notice. As of April 5 the landlord had not made the repair. The tenant paid a repair service $300 and wants to subtract that amount from April's rent. The tenant has not used repair-and-deduct in the past 12 months.
>
> **Question Presented**
> Under California law, can a tenant whose landlord failed for more than 30 days after written notice to fix a broken heater pay for the repair and deduct the $300 from one month's rent?
>
> **Brief Answer**
> Probably yes. California Civil Code § 1942 lets a tenant deduct repair costs of up to one month's rent if the landlord fails to make a needed repair within a reasonable time after notice. The statute presumes that 30 days is reasonable. Heating is a basic habitability requirement, and the tenant meets the once-in-12-months limit.
>
> **Discussion**
> *Rule.* Civil Code § 1942(a) allows a tenant to make repairs to conditions that make the premises untenantable and deduct the cost from rent. The landlord must have had notice and failed to repair within a reasonable time, which the statute presumes is 30 days. The deduction can't exceed one month's rent, and the remedy may be used no more than twice in any 12-month period. Civil Code § 1941.1 lists adequate heating among the conditions required for a home to be tenantable.
>
> *Application.* The broken heater is an untenantable condition under § 1941.1. The tenant gave written notice, and the landlord did nothing for more than 30 days, which meets the presumptively reasonable period. The $300 is well under one month's rent, and this is the tenant's first use of the remedy this year.
>
> *Counterarguments.* The landlord might argue that the tenant caused the damage. Under § 1929, a tenant cannot use the remedy for damage the tenant caused. Nothing in the facts suggests that. The landlord might also try to evict in retaliation. Civil Code § 1942.5 presumes retaliation if the landlord acts within 180 days of the tenant using this remedy. *(Cases interpreting § 1942 still to be checked, and all authorities to be Shepardized.)*
>
> **Conclusion**
> The tenant can most likely deduct the $300. Next steps: keep copies of the notice, the repair invoice and the receipt; send the landlord a letter explaining the deduction with the April rent; and confirm that the statutes haven't been amended since this memo was written.

The example's statutes are given for illustration. Check current law before relying on them for a real situation.
