## Part 1: Frame the issue before you read anything (Nolo Ch. 3)

- **Civil or criminal?** Small claims is **civil**: one party asks for money from another.
- **Substantive or procedural?**
  - *Substantive:* what the law says about your dispute, such as breach of contract, a security deposit, property damage, or a defective product.
  - *Procedural:* how small claims works, such as the dollar limit, which court, how to serve the other side, the hearing, and collecting the judgment.
  - You need both.
- **State or federal?** Almost always **state law** in a state small claims court.
- **Area of law:** for example contracts, landlord-tenant, consumer, or tort (property damage or negligence).
- **Write the research question:** "My goal is to recover $___ from ___ because ___. What law gives me that right, what do I have to prove, and how do I present it in ___ County small claims court?"

To find the statute, start with a **secondary source**: a Nolo article or *Everybody's Guide to Small Claims Court*, your state court's small claims self-help pages, or a state legal encyclopedia or practice guide at a law library. These give you the vocabulary and point you to the statute and the leading cases.

## Part 2: How to read a statute

### Find the right version
- Use your state's **official code website**, or an **annotated code** at a law library. The annotated version adds case summaries, history and cross-references.
- **Check the pocket part** (the supplement at the back of a print volume) or the online update. It shows amendments and repeals.
- Note the **effective date**. You need the version that was in force when your dispute arose.

### Nolo's seven rules for reading a statute
1. **Read it at least three times, then read it again.** Slowly, clause by clause.
2. **Pay close attention to "and" and "or".** "And" means every element is required. "Or" means any one is enough. A single word can decide your case.
3. **Assume every word and every punctuation mark means something.** Watch for "shall" (required) versus "may" (optional), "within 30 days", and lists separated by commas.
4. **Read it consistently with related statutes.** Look at the sections around it, and the **definitions section** of that chapter, which often defines key words.
5. **Criminal statutes are read strictly,** with ambiguities resolved for the defendant. This rarely applies in small claims, but know it.
6. **Read ambiguous language to further the law's purpose,** especially consumer- and tenant-protection laws, which courts tend to read in favor of the people they protect.
7. **Read it so that it makes sense.** Courts avoid absurd results the legislature couldn't have intended. In Nolo's example, a California court read a "$100 a day" penalty as "up to $100".

### Then go beyond the text
- **Follow every cross-reference.**
- **Read the cases that interpret it.** Nolo says court interpretations are part of the statute. Use the annotations to find them.
- Other aids: **attorney general opinions** (persuasive, not binding), *Words and Phrases* (one-line definitions from cases), and **legislative history** if the language is truly unclear.

| Question | Your notes |
|---|---|
| Citation and effective date | |
| Who does it apply to? (definitions) | |
| **Elements**: what must be true for it to apply? List each one, and mark the "and"s and "or"s | |

Turn the elements into a checklist of **facts you must prove** at the hearing, and the **evidence** for each one.

## Part 3: How to read a case (Nolo Ch. 6–7)

### Read the citation
Case name (*Plaintiff v. Defendant*; the appealing party may be listed first) → **volume** → **reporter** → **first page** → **(court and year)**. The court and year tell you whether the case binds your court.

### Know which parts are law
- **Headnotes and synopsis** at the top are written by the publisher. They are **not law** and "tend to overly simplify issues". Use them to find the relevant pages, then read the opinion itself.
- The opinion itself usually contains: **facts → issue(s) → holding → reasoning**.
- **Only the majority (or plurality) decision is law.** Concurrences and dissents aren't binding.
- **Holding versus dicta:** the holding is the ruling needed to decide the case. **Dicta** are side comments that weren't necessary to the decision, and they aren't binding (except, in practice, for every word of the US Supreme Court).

### Is it binding on *your* small claims court?
- Small claims and other trial-level decisions generally **don't create binding precedent**, and most aren't published.
- What binds your small claims court: your state's **appellate courts** (the intermediate court of appeals in your region, and the state supreme court) and, on federal questions, federal law.
- **Out-of-state cases** are only persuasive. They're useful if your state has nothing on point.

### Does it actually help you? (Nolo's analysis steps)
1. **Identify the precise issue** the court decided and compare it with yours. If they don't match, the case probably won't help.
2. **Compare the facts.** Are they close enough that the ruling logically applies? Note the differences the other side could use to **distinguish** it.
3. **Check the court:** binding or only persuasive for your court?
4. **Check whether it's still good law** (Part 4).
5. **Prefer recent cases.** Read the newest ones first.

### Where to find cases free
Google Scholar ("Case law", then select your state's courts), Justia and FindLaw. Search by issue words plus your statute's citation. Also use the annotations in your state's annotated code. Once you find **one good case,** follow the cases it cites and the cases that cite it.

## Part 4: Confirm it's still good law (Nolo Ch. 8)

- **Shepardize or KeyCite** every case and statute you plan to rely on. This confirms it hasn't been reversed, overruled, amended or repealed, and it finds more cases.
- Key treatment flags: **overruled** (no longer valid); **questioned**, **criticized** or **distinguished** (be careful); **followed** (good).
- Free option: most **county law libraries** and law school libraries give public access to Lexis or Westlaw. Ask the librarian.
- For statutes, also check the pocket part and the official code site.

## Part 5: IRAC memo outline (Nolo Ch. 9)

A **memo** is a neutral, internal analysis for you, or for a lawyer you might hire. It isn't a brief and it isn't filed. Writing one forces you to face the weak points before the other side raises them. For the hearing itself, turn it into a one-page outline and an organized evidence packet.

```
MEMORANDUM
TO:   File (or: Attorney ___)
FROM: ___
DATE: ___
RE:   [Your name] v. [Other party], ___ County Small Claims, amount $___

I. STATEMENT OF FACTS
   Only the legally relevant facts, in date order, each tied to an exhibit.
   - [Date]: agreement made (Exhibit 1: contract / texts)
   - [Date]: what you did (paid, delivered, moved out...)
   - [Date]: what they did or failed to do
   - [Date]: your demand for payment (Exhibit __: demand letter)
   - Amount owed and how you calculated it

II. QUESTION PRESENTED
   One question, answerable yes or no. Example form:
   "Under [State statute § ___ / contract law], is [other party] liable to
   [you] for $___ when [key facts]?"

III. BRIEF ANSWER
   "Probably yes/no, because ___." (one or two sentences)

IV. DISCUSSION (one IRAC block per issue: liability, damages, any defense)

   Issue 1: [e.g., Did the other party breach the agreement?]
     I - Issue: state it precisely.
     R - Rule: the statute (quote the key language, cite it) and/or the
         binding case (citation; one-sentence holding). Note whether the
         authority is binding or persuasive.
     A - Analysis: go element by element. For each one, the fact and the
         evidence that proves it. Compare your facts with the case's facts.
         State the other side's best argument and your answer to it.
     C - Conclusion: what the rule means for this issue.

   Issue 2: [e.g., Amount of damages / is a statutory penalty available?]
     I / R / A / C

   Issue 3: [e.g., Defense the other side may raise: statute of
            limitations, they already paid, you caused the problem...]
     I / R / A / C

V. PROCEDURAL CHECKLIST
   - Is the claim within the court's dollar limit? (verify for your court)
   - Correct court and county (venue): where the defendant lives or does
     business, or where the transaction happened (verify local rules)
   - Within the statute of limitations? (verify for this kind of claim)
   - Defendant's correct legal name; service completed and proof filed
   - Hearing date; exhibits copied (one set for you, the judge and the
     other side); witnesses lined up

VI. CONCLUSION AND NEXT STEPS
   - Overall prediction and the weakest point
   - Open questions / research still needed
   - Validation done: [ ] pocket part  [ ] official code site
     [ ] Shepard's/KeyCite for each case
```

**Tips:**
- Cite properly (case name, volume, reporter, page, court and year). Citations are "the roadmap" for the reader.
- Be **objective**. A memo that hides weaknesses doesn't help you prepare.
- Stick to **existing law**. Trial judges, including small claims judges, follow precedent. They don't create new law.

## Part 6: Small claims practicalities to verify for your state

- The **dollar limit** and whether you can split or reduce your claim to fit under it.
- **Filing fees,** and how the court serves the defendant.
- Whether **lawyers are allowed** (some states bar them in small claims) and whether the clerk will help you. Clerks are often required to.
- **Appeal rights.** Some states let only the defendant appeal, or give a new trial (*de novo*) on appeal.
- How to **collect** if you win.

Your court's website and self-help center, and Nolo's *Everybody's Guide to Small Claims Court*, cover these. Always confirm against your court's current rules.
