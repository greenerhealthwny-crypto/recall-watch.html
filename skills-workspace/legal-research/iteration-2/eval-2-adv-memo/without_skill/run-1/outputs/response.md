## Part 1: How to read a statute

1. **Get the official, current text.** Use your state legislature's website or the official code. Check the "current through" date. An outdated version, or a summary from a blog, is the most common research mistake.
2. **Read the whole section, then the parts around it.** Read the section itself and also:
   - the **definitions** section for the chapter or article (words like "consumer," "goods," "merchant," or "landlord" often have special meanings),
   - neighboring sections that build on it, give exceptions, or set penalties,
   - any **effective date** and amendment history, so you know which version applied on the date your dispute arose.
3. **Break it into elements.** Turn the statute into a checklist of everything that must be true. For example: "A [landlord] who [fails to return a security deposit] within [X days] after [the tenant vacates] is liable for [damages]." Each bracketed item is an element you have to prove.
4. **Watch the small words.**
   - "shall" or "must" means mandatory. "May" means optional.
   - "and" means all elements are required. "or" means any one of them is enough.
   - "unless," "except," and "provided that" signal exceptions. Find out who has to prove the exception.
   - Time limits: "within 14 days," "no later than," and whether the count is calendar days or business days.
5. **Identify the remedy.** What do you get if you win: actual damages, double or treble damages, attorney's fees, statutory penalties? Is there a cap? Is there a pre-suit notice or demand requirement?
6. **Check the procedural fit.** Is there a statute of limitations? Does the remedy fit within the small-claims dollar limit? Does the statute require something before you sue, such as written notice?
7. **See how courts have read it.** An annotated code lists cases interpreting each section. Courts' readings control over your own reading of an ambiguous phrase.

## Part 2: How to read a case

1. **Check whether the case matters for you.** Note:
   - **Court and level:** a decision of your state's highest court binds every lower court in the state. An intermediate appellate decision usually binds the trial courts under it. A decision from another state, or a trial-level decision, is only *persuasive*.
   - **Date:** is it still good law? Check that it has not been overruled or that the statute has not been amended since. Use Shepard's or KeyCite at a law library, or "cited by" lists in free tools.
2. **Read it in passes.**
   - First pass: the syllabus or headnotes (written by editors, *not* law) and the conclusion, so you know who won.
   - Second pass: the full opinion.
3. **Brief it.** Write down:
   - **Facts:** what happened (only the facts that mattered to the result)
   - **Procedural history:** who sued, what the lower court did, why it is on appeal
   - **Issue:** the legal question the court answered
   - **Holding:** the court's answer to that question. This is the binding part.
   - **Reasoning:** why the court decided that way (which statute or rule, and how it applied)
   - **Disposition:** affirmed, reversed, or remanded
   - **Dicta:** side comments that were not needed for the result. These can persuade a judge but do not bind one.
   - Any **concurrence or dissent**, which can show the weak points of the majority's reasoning
4. **Compare it to your facts.** List the ways your facts are *like* the case (analogize) and *unlike* it (distinguish). Expect the other side to rely on the differences.
5. **Pull a short quote** stating the rule, with a pinpoint page cite, to show the judge.

**Small-claims tip:** small-claims judges usually want a short, clear explanation rather than a long legal argument. One or two on-point cases, and the statute's text, are often enough. Bring printed copies for the judge and the other side.

## Part 3: IRAC memo outline

**MEMORANDUM**
**To:** [File / self / small-claims judge prep]
**From:** [You]
**Date:** [ ]
**Re:** [Plaintiff] v. [Defendant], [court and county], claim for $[amount]

### Question presented
One sentence combining the law and the key facts. *Example:* "Under [statute § ___], is [defendant] liable for [remedy] where [key fact 1] and [key fact 2]?"

### Brief answer
"Probably yes / probably no, because ..." in two or three sentences.

### Statement of facts
- A neutral chronology with dates
- Note the evidence for each fact (contract, texts, receipts, photos, witnesses)
- Include harmful facts too. You need to answer them.

### Discussion (IRAC for each issue)

**Issue 1: [e.g., breach of contract / statutory violation]**
- **I (Issue):** State the specific question.
- **R (Rule):** Quote the statute. List its elements. Add the case rule or holding interpreting it, with citations.
- **A (Application):** Go element by element:
  - Element 1, then your facts and evidence, then any analogous or distinguishable case
  - Element 2, and so on
  - **Counterarguments:** the other side's likely defenses (statute of limitations, exceptions, failure to mitigate, payment already made, the other party breached first) and your answer to each
- **C (Conclusion):** Whether this element or claim is likely met.

**Issue 2: [e.g., damages / amount recoverable]**
- **I:** What amount can be recovered?
- **R:** The measure of damages, any statutory multiplier or cap, and the small-claims limit in [jurisdiction]
- **A:** Calculate it with supporting documents: an itemized table, plus costs and filing fee
- **C:** The total to request

**Issue 3 (if needed): [procedure: right court, venue, deadline, pre-suit notice]**
- **I / R / A / C** the same way

### Overall conclusion and next steps
- The likely outcome and the strongest theory
- Evidence still to gather; witnesses to bring
- Pre-suit steps (a demand letter, required notices)
- Filing logistics: court, fee, service on the defendant, hearing date
- Collection plan if you win

## Where to find the law (free or low cost)
- State legislature's site (statutes); your state court system's small-claims self-help pages
- Google Scholar (case law), CourtListener, Justia, your state court's official opinions site
- Public or county law libraries, which often offer free Westlaw or Lexis access and librarian help
- Nolo guides (for example, *Everybody's Guide to Small Claims Court*) for plain-English background
