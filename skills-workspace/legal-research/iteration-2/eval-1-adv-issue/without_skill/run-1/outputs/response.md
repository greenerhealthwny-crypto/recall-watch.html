# Marketplace Chargeback After In-Person Pickup (New York): Issue Framing and Research Path

> This is legal research guidance, not legal advice. Every statute, rule, and dollar limit below is from memory. Check each one against a current official source before you rely on it (see Step 6).

## 0. Clear up the facts first

Your facts contain a contradiction that will decide the case: the buyer **picked the item up in person** but told the payment provider it **"never arrived."** Before you research anything, write down your version of events with dates and keep the evidence:

- Marketplace and payment-app messages (meeting time and place, "got it, thanks," and so on)
- The payment record: app, date, amount, the note attached to it, and whether it was marked "goods and services" or "friends and family"
- Any proof of the handoff: witnesses, photos, a safe-exchange-zone or doorbell camera, location history
- The chargeback or dispute notice and your response deadline
- The listing itself (description, condition, photos)

Also find out **which kind of dispute this is**. It changes the analysis:

| Payment method | Who decides | Main governing rules |
|---|---|---|
| Credit card behind the app | Card issuer / card network | Fair Credit Billing Act (15 U.S.C. § 1666 et seq.), Regulation Z (12 C.F.R. pt. 1026) |
| Debit card or bank-funded transfer | Bank / app | Electronic Fund Transfer Act (15 U.S.C. § 1693 et seq.), Regulation E (12 C.F.R. pt. 1005) |
| App's internal dispute (e.g., PayPal or Venmo purchase protection) | The app | The app's user agreement and seller-protection terms (private contract, not statute) |

## 1. Define the problem (Nolo step: state the question in your own words)

**Plain-English question:** A buyer received goods in person, paid for them, and then got the money reversed by falsely claiming non-delivery. Can I get the $200 back, and from whom?

**Framed legal issues:**

1. **Main issue (breach of contract / action for the price):** Under New York law, when a seller delivers goods and the buyer accepts them, then cancels or reverses payment, can the seller recover the price from the buyer?
2. **Other theories:** Can the seller recover under **unjust enrichment** (the buyer kept both the goods and the money), or for **fraud** (a false statement made to get the payment reversed)?
3. **Forum and procedure:** Can the seller bring this in **New York small claims court**, in which court and county, and within what time limit?
4. **Platform track:** Can the seller win the dispute through the payment provider? (This is mostly a contract and terms-of-service question, not a court question.)

## 2. Classify the issue (Nolo step: put it in a legal category)

- **Civil, not criminal.** A false chargeback can sometimes be a crime (for example, larceny by false pretenses under N.Y. Penal Law art. 155). But a prosecutor brings criminal cases, not you, and a criminal case does not put $200 back in your pocket quickly. You can file a police report to support your dispute. Your recovery path is civil.
- **State law, not federal.** The contract between you and the buyer falls under New York law. Federal law (FCBA/EFTA) controls only the bank's dispute process. It gives *you*, the seller, almost no rights against the buyer.
- **Substantive area:** sale of goods, which is **UCC Article 2** as adopted in New York (N.Y. U.C.C. Law art. 2). It is also contract law generally.
- **Procedural area:** small claims court practice in New York.

## 3. Background reading (Nolo step: secondary sources before primary law)

Start with plain-language overviews so you learn the vocabulary ("acceptance," "action for the price," "unjust enrichment"):

- Nolo's small-claims and contract-law materials, such as *Everybody's Guide to Small Claims Court* and nolo.com articles on suing buyers and sellers
- The New York State Unified Court System's small claims pages and its free guide (nycourts.gov, "Small Claims")
- CourtHelp (nycourts.gov/courthelp): forms, venue rules, and how to serve the other side
- A legal encyclopedia, if you can reach a law library: *New York Jurisprudence 2d* (sales; contracts; restitution), or *Carmody-Wait 2d* for procedure
- CFPB materials on Regulation E and Z disputes, so you understand the bank's side

## 4. Find the primary law: statutes

Pull the current text of each provision below and read it closely. These are starting points, not confirmed citations:

**Sale of goods (N.Y. U.C.C. Law, Article 2):**
- § 2-301: the buyer's duty to accept and pay according to the contract
- § 2-606: what counts as "acceptance" of goods (for example, taking them and not rejecting them)
- § 2-607: a buyer who accepts goods must pay at the contract rate
- § 2-709: the seller's **action for the price** when the buyer has accepted the goods. This is likely your strongest single provision.
- § 2-201: the statute of frauds. It applies only to sales of $500 or more, so it should not affect a $200 sale. Confirm that.
- § 2-725: a four-year statute of limitations for sale-of-goods contracts

**Small claims procedure:**
- N.Y. City Civil Court Act art. 18 (§ 1801 et seq.): small claims in NYC. The limit is believed to be **$10,000**.
- Uniform City Court Act art. 18: city courts outside NYC (believed to be **$5,000**)
- Uniform District Court Act art. 18: Nassau and Suffolk district courts (believed to be **$5,000**)
- Uniform Justice Court Act art. 18: town and village courts (believed to be **$3,000**)
- Venue: the defendant generally has to **live, work, or have a business office** within the court's area. Confirm this in the act that applies to your court.
- Commercial claims (art. 18-A) are for businesses. If you are selling as an individual, they probably do not apply.

**Payment-dispute background (federal):**
- 15 U.S.C. § 1666 and 12 C.F.R. § 1026.13 (billing-error resolution on credit cards)
- 15 U.S.C. § 1693f and 12 C.F.R. § 1005.11 (error resolution on electronic fund transfers)
- Read these to understand the bank's process. You will probably find that they do not give the seller a cause of action.

**Private terms (not law, but binding on you):**
- The payment app's user agreement and seller-protection policy. Many policies exclude in-person or local-pickup transactions, or require specific proof. Check the version that was in effect on your transaction date.
- Facebook Marketplace's Commerce/Purchase Policies. Meta generally does not handle off-platform payments.

## 5. Find the primary law: cases

Search for New York decisions with fact patterns close to yours:

- **Search terms:** "chargeback" AND ("small claims" OR "action for the price"); "stop payment" AND "accepted goods"; "unjust enrichment" AND "chargeback"; "U.C.C. 2-709" AND "accepted"
- **Sources:** free options are Google Scholar (case law, New York courts), CourtListener, and the NY Official Reports / NY Slip Opinions site. Lower-court decisions in the *Misc. 3d* reporter often cover small claims. Paid options are Westlaw and Lexis, often free at a county or public law library.
- **Elements to confirm in case law:**
  - Unjust enrichment: (1) the other party was enriched, (2) at your expense, and (3) equity and good conscience require repayment. Courts often say unjust enrichment is not available where a valid contract covers the same subject. Plead it as an alternative.
  - Fraud: misrepresentation, the defendant knew it was false, *justifiable reliance by the plaintiff*, and damages. Weak point: the buyer lied to the **bank**, not to you, so your reliance is doubtful. Breach of contract is the cleaner theory.
- **Small-claims standard:** small claims courts must do "substantial justice" under the rules of substantive law, and the formal rules of evidence are relaxed (CCA § 1804 and its parallels). Look for cases applying this standard.

## 6. Update and verify (Nolo step: make sure the law is current)

- Check every statute in a **current** official or annotated source (nysenate.gov/legislation for New York statutes; uscode.house.gov and ecfr.gov for federal). Small-claims dollar limits change by amendment.
- Run each case you plan to cite through a citator (Shepard's or KeyCite at a law library; on free tools, check the "cited by" lists) to confirm it has not been reversed or overruled.
- Confirm the current small-claims filing fee, the court's local rules, and service procedures with the clerk of the court where you will file.

## 7. Apply the law and plan next steps

**Likely analysis (to confirm through the research above):** you delivered and the buyer accepted the goods in person. Under UCC §§ 2-606, 2-607, and 2-709, the buyer owes the price. Reversing the payment does not cancel that debt. It means the buyer still has not paid. The chargeback outcome does not bind a court. Your claim is against the **buyer**, for $200 plus filing and service costs.

**Practical order of operations:**
1. **Contest the dispute** with the payment app or bank before its deadline. Submit the messages and proof of the handoff.
2. **Send a written demand** to the buyer, with a deadline, asking for payment. Courts like to see that you made one, and it often settles the matter.
3. If that fails, **file in small claims** in the court for the area where the buyer lives or works. You will need the buyer's **real name and address** for service. Marketplace profiles often do not show them, so collect what you can now.
4. **Collecting the money:** winning a judgment is not the same as being paid. Read up on New York judgment enforcement (CPLR art. 52) and the court's collection guidance.
5. Optional: file a police report or a platform report. A police report can help support your dispute response.

**Open questions to research further:**
- Was the payment marked "goods and services"? If it was sent as "friends and family," how did the buyer reverse it (card chargeback versus an app claim)?
- Does the app's seller-protection policy cover in-person sales at all?
- Is the buyer outside your court's area, or outside New York? That raises venue and jurisdiction problems.
- What is the cost-benefit for a $200 claim? Weigh the filing fee (recoverable as costs), your time, and how likely you are to collect.
