# Facebook Marketplace Chargeback ($200, New York): Framing the Issue and a Research Path

*This is legal information, not legal advice. It follows the method in Nolo's* Legal Research: How to Find & Understand the Law *(19th ed., 2021). Every statute or rule named below is a lead for you to look up and confirm. Read the current text and validate it before you rely on it.*

## 0. What to settle first (these facts change the research path)

Nolo's method begins with the facts and your goal. Before you research, pin these down:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Which payment app?** (PayPal, Venmo, Cash App, Zelle, Apple Pay, other) | Each has its own **user agreement and dispute process**. That contract, not a statute, will probably decide the first round. |
| **How did the buyer fund the payment?** (credit card, debit card, bank account, app balance) | A **credit card** brings in the card issuer's chargeback process and federal billing-error law. A bank or debit transfer brings in different rules. An app-balance payment may stay entirely inside the app's own process. |
| **Is it a true "chargeback" or an in-app dispute/claim?** | A chargeback is reversed by the buyer's **card issuer or bank**. An app "dispute" is decided by the **app**. They are different forums with different deadlines for your response. |
| **What proof of handoff do you have?** | Marketplace messages arranging the pickup, time and place, a witness, photos, a signed receipt, the buyer's messages after the pickup. This is what wins both a dispute and a small-claims case. |
| **Where in New York?** (NYC borough, another city, or a town or village) | The **small claims dollar limit and the court** depend on where you sue (see §4). |
| **Where does the buyer live?** | You generally have to sue where the defendant lives, works or does business. An out-of-state buyer makes collection much harder. |
| **Your goal** | Win the platform dispute? Sue for $200 in small claims? Report fraud? Or just understand your options? Nolo says the goal decides how deep you go. |

## 1. Frame the issue (Nolo's four steps, Ch. 3)

### Step 1: Civil or criminal? Substantive or procedural? State or federal?

- **Civil, mainly.** You want your money (damages) back from a private person, so this is civil.
- **A possible criminal side.** Taking an item in person and then falsely claiming it never arrived to get the money back can amount to theft by deception or fraud. This is "friendly fraud." Only a prosecutor brings charges, and they won't get you your $200 back directly. A police report can still be useful evidence in a dispute or in court. Treat it as secondary.
- **Substantive issues:** Was a contract for the sale of goods formed and performed? Do you have a right to the price? Did the buyer breach, or commit fraud?
- **Procedural issues:** the dispute or chargeback response process and its deadlines; which small claims court, what to file, serving the buyer, evidence at the hearing, and collecting a judgment.
- **State or federal?** Both:
  - **State (New York):** contract and sale-of-goods law (NY Uniform Commercial Code, Article 2), small claims procedure, and the NY Penal Law if you go the criminal route.
  - **Federal:** the consumer payment rules that shape the bank or card dispute. Nolo's chart lists **consumer protection** as an area of both state and federal law.
  - **Private contract:** the payment app's **user agreement** and seller-protection terms. It is not "law" in the primary-source sense, but it binds you and the buyer as users, and it usually decides the dispute first.

### Step 2: Area(s) of law

- **Contracts / commercial law (sale of goods):** the main area.
- **Consumer law / creditor-debtor (payment disputes):** chargeback and dispute rules.
- **Tort / criminal (fraud, larceny):** secondary.
- **Civil procedure (small claims):** for the remedy.

### Step 3: Which resources will help?

See §3 (secondary sources) and §4 (primary law).

### Step 4: Write the research question

> **Goal:** Get back my $200, or keep it, after a Facebook Marketplace buyer who picked up the item in person reversed the payment by claiming it never arrived. First through the payment app's or bank's dispute process, and if that fails, through New York small claims court.

**Specific questions:**
1. What do the payment app's user agreement and seller-protection policy say about in-person pickup and "item not received" claims? What is my deadline to respond, and what evidence counts?
2. If the buyer funded it with a card, what process does the card issuer use, and can I submit evidence through the app?
3. Under New York's UCC Article 2, was there a completed sale? Can I recover the price for goods the buyer accepted?
4. Which New York small claims court do I file in, what is its dollar limit, what does filing cost, and how do I serve the buyer?
5. What evidence will the small claims judge want to see?
6. Is the buyer's conduct a crime under the NY Penal Law, and is a police report worth filing?
7. If I win, how do I collect the judgment?

## 3. Start with secondary sources (Ch. 2 and 4)

Start here to learn the vocabulary and pick up citations:

1. **The payment app's own help pages and user agreement.** Read this first, since it controls the dispute. Look for: seller protection eligibility (many policies cover only shipped items with tracking, so in-person pickups may be **excluded**), "item not received" rules, the response deadline, and how to upload evidence. Save a dated copy of the version in force on the day of the sale.
2. **Nolo.com:** plain-English articles on small claims court, suing for breach of contract, and collecting a judgment. Nolo's *Everybody's Guide to Small Claims Court* covers preparing evidence and witnesses.
3. **NY State Courts (nycourts.gov) small claims pages and the CourtHelp self-help site:** forms, filing fees, limits by court, how service works, and what to bring to the hearing. Court self-help staff can explain procedure but cannot give legal advice.
4. **CFPB (consumerfinance.gov):** explainers on payment-app disputes and on billing-error and electronic-transfer rights. This helps you understand what the buyer's bank or issuer will consider.
5. **A New York practice guide or encyclopedia at a law library** (for example, a NY Jurisprudence title on sales or small claims). Nolo says a state-specific source is "almost always best" for a state-law issue. Ask the law librarian.
6. **UCC treatises (for example, White & Summers on the Uniform Commercial Code):** for the seller's remedies when a buyer has accepted goods.

## 4. Primary law to find and read

*Find and read the current text yourself, using the official New York sources and the annotated codes. Don't rely on these descriptions. Section numbers are leads to confirm, not authority.*

### A. The contract between you and the buyer: NY Uniform Commercial Code, Article 2 (Sales)
- A sale of a used item for $200 is a **sale of goods**, so UCC Article 2 applies.
- Look for the sections on **acceptance of goods** (a buyer who takes the item and keeps it has generally accepted it) and the **seller's action for the price** for accepted goods (in the standard UCC numbering this is § 2-709; confirm it in the NY UCC).
- Use an **annotated** NY UCC (McKinney's) for the cases that interpret these sections. Nolo stresses that "court interpretations are part of the statute."
- Statute of frauds: the UCC writing requirement for goods starts at $500. Confirm the threshold. At $200 the sale shouldn't need a signed writing, and your Marketplace messages are evidence of the deal anyway.

### B. The payment rails: federal consumer-payment law (for context and the dispute)
- **Credit card funding:** the federal **Fair Credit Billing Act** and **Regulation Z** (12 C.F.R. Part 1026) give cardholders billing-error rights, including for goods "not delivered as agreed". That is the hook the buyer used. The card issuer investigates, and you, the merchant side, can rebut it with evidence of the handoff.
- **Bank or debit funding:** the **Electronic Fund Transfer Act** and **Regulation E** (12 C.F.R. Part 1005) mainly protect against *unauthorized* transfers and certain errors. A buyer who authorized the payment and then claims non-delivery usually has weaker rights here than with a card, but check the current rules and the CFPB's guidance on peer-to-peer apps.
- Get the current text at **ecfr.gov** and **govinfo.gov**. The book points to GPO's FDsys, which govinfo.gov has since replaced.
- **The key point for you:** these rules decide what the *bank or app* does. They don't decide whether the buyer owes you $200 under contract law. Losing the dispute doesn't stop you from suing the buyer.

### C. The remedy: New York small claims procedure
- Small claims in New York is set up by court-specific statutes: the **NYC Civil Court Act** (for the five boroughs), the **Uniform City Court Act** (cities outside NYC), the **Uniform Justice Court Act** (towns and villages), and the **Uniform District Court Act** (Nassau and parts of Suffolk). Each has its own small claims article.
- **Dollar limits differ by court.** As commonly published they are about **$10,000 in NYC**, **$5,000 in city courts** outside NYC, and **$3,000 in town and village courts**. *Verify these on nycourts.gov before filing.* A $200 claim fits in any of them.
- Look up: where the defendant must live, work or do business; the filing fee; how the clerk serves the defendant (often by mail); what to do if the buyer doesn't appear (a default judgment); and how to enforce a judgment.
- Small claims courts use simplified rules and are generally not bound by strict rules of evidence. Read the court's own description of how hearings are run.

### D. If you consider the criminal route: the NY Penal Law
- Read the **larceny** article, including larceny by false pretense or by false promise. Theft of property worth $1,000 or less is generally **petit larceny**, a misdemeanor; confirm the section and threshold.
- Remember: prosecution belongs to the district attorney. Restitution can follow a conviction, but don't count on it for $200. A police report mainly serves as **evidence** you can send to the app or bank.

### E. Cases
- Search **Google Scholar** (Case law → select New York courts), **Justia**, or **FindLaw** for NY decisions on chargebacks, accepted goods, and payment reversals. Use the annotations in the McKinney's UCC volumes too.
- Most small claims decisions aren't published, and Nolo notes that trial courts generally don't issue binding opinions. The binding authority is **Appellate Term, Appellate Division and Court of Appeals** decisions.
- When you read a case, separate the **holding** from the **dicta**. Ignore the headnotes for anything you'll rely on. Check whether the case's issue actually matches yours.

## 5. Validate everything (Ch. 8)

- **Statutes:** check the **pocket part**, or the online update, of the annotated code. Use the official NY Legislature site (nysenate.gov or public.leginfo.state.ny.us) for current text. Shepardize or KeyCite statutes for amendments and for cases that interpreted them.
- **Federal regulations:** use **ecfr.gov** for the current version, and check the Federal Register for recent changes.
- **Cases:** run any case you plan to cite through **Shepard's or KeyCite** to confirm it hasn't been reversed or overruled. You can usually do this free on the public-access terminals at a **county or law school law library**.
- **App terms:** confirm the version of the user agreement that applied on the date of the sale. Apps update their terms often.
- **Court limits and fees:** confirm them on nycourts.gov. They change.

## 6. Action-oriented plan (in the order Nolo would suggest)

1. **Respond to the dispute now.** Deadlines are short, often about 7–10 days; check yours. Upload your evidence: the Marketplace chat setting up the pickup, the time and place, any photo of the item or the handoff, witness names, and any later messages from the buyer.
2. **Ask the buyer in writing** to cancel the dispute. Keep the message calm and factual. This also creates a record for a judge.
3. **Report the buyer to Facebook** and keep a screenshot of the report. Optionally file a police report and add it to your dispute evidence.
4. **If the dispute goes against you,** send a short **demand letter** stating the facts, the $200, and a deadline. Then file in the right **NY small claims court** for the $200 plus filing costs.
5. **At the hearing,** bring printed messages, photos, witnesses (or a written statement where the court allows it), and the app's dispute decision.
6. **Prevention next time:** for in-person sales, use cash or a payment method without chargeback rights, or get a signed and dated pickup receipt.

## 7. IRAC outline for your notes

- **Issue:** Under New York law, can a seller recover the $200 price from a buyer who accepted an item at an in-person pickup and then reversed the payment by claiming the item never arrived?
- **Rule:** NY UCC Article 2 on acceptance of goods and the seller's action for the price (confirm the sections). Possibly also general breach-of-contract and unjust-enrichment principles. Add any NY appellate case you find and validate.
- **Analysis:** Offer and acceptance through Marketplace messages; payment; in-person delivery and acceptance (your evidence); the reversal was a refusal to pay for goods accepted. The buyer's likely defense is that the item was never received or wasn't as described. Rebut it with your proof.
- **Conclusion:** Likely recoverable if you can prove the handoff. The deciding factor is evidence, not law.

## 8. When to involve a lawyer

For $200, small claims court is designed for you to handle yourself. In many courts you don't need a lawyer, and the clerks help with procedure. A lawyer makes sense only if this is part of a pattern, for example a buyer running this scam on many sellers, or if the app freezes larger sums in your account. For low-cost help, look for New York legal aid, a bar association referral service, or limited-scope ("unbundled") help.
