## Hebrews

- **Author:** Unknown. The letter never names its writer. Paul, Barnabas, Apollos, Luke, Priscilla and others have all been suggested. Most modern introductions say only that the writer was a well-taught Jewish Christian who knew Timothy (Hebrews 13:23).
- **When:** Probably before AD 70, the year Rome destroyed the Jerusalem Temple. The letter speaks of the temple sacrifices as if they were still going on. A date in the 60s is usually given.
- **What it's about:** Jewish Christians under pressure were tempted to drift back into the old Jewish system. The writer shows that Jesus is *superior*: greater than the angels, greater than Moses, and the perfect High Priest whose single sacrifice replaces the old sacrifices and brings in a new covenant. The letter repeatedly warns readers not to fall away. It urges them to hold firmly to their faith and to persevere. Chapter 11, the "hall of faith," lists heroes of faith, and chapter 12 calls readers to "run with endurance the race God has set before us," keeping their eyes on Jesus.

## James

- **Author:** James, the brother of Jesus and leader of the church in Jerusalem (not James the apostle, son of Zebedee).
- **When:** Probably the mid-40s AD, before the Jerusalem Council (about AD 49). That would make it one of the earliest New Testament books. Some introductions date it to the late 40s or before James's death around AD 62.
- **What it's about:** A practical letter to Jewish believers "scattered abroad," focused on *genuine faith that shows itself in action*. Its themes include:
  - finding joy and maturity through trials
  - asking God for wisdom
  - being doers of the word, not only hearers
  - not favoring the rich over the poor
  - controlling the tongue
  - humility and submission to God
  - patience in suffering
  - prayer

  Its best-known line is that "faith by itself isn't enough. Unless it produces good deeds, it is dead and useless" (James 2:17).

## Galatians

- **Theme:** Freedom in Christ, and salvation by grace through faith rather than by keeping the Jewish law. Paul wrote it (about AD 48–49, or the mid-50s, depending on the view taken) to the churches of Galatia. Teachers there, often called "Judaizers," were insisting that Gentile believers had to be circumcised and follow the law of Moses. Paul does three things in the letter:
  - He defends the gospel he preached and his own authority as an apostle.
  - He argues that people are made right with God through faith in Christ, as Abraham was, not by obeying the law.
  - He calls believers to live by the Spirit. That freedom is not an excuse to sin. Its fruit is "love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control" (Galatians 5:22–23).

  Galatians is often called the "charter of Christian liberty."
