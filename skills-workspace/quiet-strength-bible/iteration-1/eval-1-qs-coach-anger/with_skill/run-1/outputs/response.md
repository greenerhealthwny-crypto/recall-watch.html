# Anger and Leadership: A Coach's Study Plan from the Quiet Strength New Testament (NLT)

Your Bible's front matter was written by a coach, with coaches in mind. In his letter (front matter, pp. A1–A41), Tony Dungy calls the Bible **"God's playbook for us."** Players are supposed to study the playbook *before* the game, not after they've fallen behind. A player who struggles on the floor usually isn't sure what to do, so the coach tells him to "get in his book." Dungy's advice is to read the Bible and pray every day: "that's the best way to be successful in the game of life."

That fits your situation well. The time to prepare for the moment you lose your temper is Monday morning, not the third quarter.

Below are the passages the edition's **Quiet Strength Verse Finder** (pp. A11–A31) lists under anger and leadership, plus a few related practices, and a four-week plan for studying them.

---

## 1. Anger: "Anger must be handled wisely"

The Verse Finder lists anger under **Practices of a Winning Life**. Its principle statement says anger "can be a destructive force and must be dealt with wisely. Don't let it get a foothold."

| Reference | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Ephesians 4:26–27** | Anger is not automatically sin, but it must not take over, and you deal with it the same day. Notice the "foothold" idea: unresolved anger gives the enemy room to work. |
| **James 1:19** | The order matters: be quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to get angry. Try that as a sideline rule. Listen to the player's explanation before you react. |

Read each passage in its full chapter. **Ephesians 4:29–32** goes on to talk about the words that come out of our mouths and ends with kindness and forgiveness. **James 1:20** says why it matters: human anger doesn't produce the righteousness God wants. Both apply directly to what you say in a huddle.

## 2. Leadership: "Leadership must be developed"

The Verse Finder's leadership principle: if you want to be a leader, ask God to develop **honesty, kindness, patience, purity and integrity** in you. Patience and kindness are on that list. On this view, keeping your temper is part of leadership itself, not a side issue.

| Reference | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Titus 2:7–8** | Be an example of good works, and let your teaching show integrity and seriousness. Your players learn more from how you act than from what you say. |
| **Hebrews 13:17** | This verse speaks to followers, but notice that leaders "are accountable to God." You will answer for how you treat the kids in your care. |

Titus was a young leader with a hard job. The edition's overview (p. 187) describes the letter as a set of challenges for an overseer: leadership qualifications, sound teaching and good works. **Titus 1:7** is worth adding here, since it lists "not quick-tempered" among the qualities of a leader.

## 3. Related practices for a coach

The Verse Finder's quick-lookup table sends "Leading a team" to Leadership, Integrity, Humility, Service and Responsibility, and "Anger or a conflict" to Anger, Conflict, Reconciliation and Revenge. These are the most relevant for you:

| Practice (Verse Finder wording) | Key references | Why it matters for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-control must be pursued.** Self-control brings honor to God, so ask him to help you | 1 Peter 1:13; 2 Peter 1:5–6 | Self-control is something you build over time, like conditioning. 2 Peter 1:5–6 shows it growing step by step. |
| **Kindness must be demonstrated** | Ephesians 4:32; **2 Timothy 2:24** | 2 Timothy 2:24 describes a servant of the Lord who is kind to everyone, able to teach, and patient. It reads like a description of a good coach. |
| **Humility must be pursued.** True greatness begins with humility | Matthew 20:26; Luke 9:48; Luke 14:7–11 | In Matthew 20:26 the greatest is the one who serves. You coach for the players' good, not for your pride or the scoreboard. |
| **Reconciliation must be sought** | Matthew 18:15; Proverbs 14:9 | When you've blown up at a player, go and make it right with him. A sincere apology from a coach can teach more than a winning season. |
| **Integrity must be protected** | Titus 2:7; Proverbs 10:9; 1 Timothy 3:7 | The coach in practice and the coach on Sunday should be the same person. |
| **Responsibility must be accepted** | Matthew 25:23; Luke 16:10 | Being faithful with a gym full of teenagers is "small things" faithfulness that matters a lot. |
| **Renewal must be pursued.** Depend on God to change your heart | Ephesians 4:22–24; Psalm 51:10 | Willpower alone usually doesn't change a temper. Psalm 51:10 ("Create in me a clean heart") is a good prayer to say before practice. |

## 4. From Psalms and Proverbs (in the back of your edition)

The edition's overview (p. 301) describes Proverbs as practical wisdom for every area of life. The Verse Finder doesn't list these under anger, but they are classic verses on the subject and worth reading:

- **Proverbs 15:1**: a gentle answer turns away wrath, and harsh words stir up anger.
- **Proverbs 16:32**: someone who controls his temper is better than a mighty warrior.
- **Proverbs 29:11**: fools vent their anger, and the wise quietly hold it back.
- **Psalm 46:1–3**: the edition's opening verse for "a life of quiet strength." God is our refuge and strength, ready to help in times of trouble, including a close game that's slipping away.

## 5. How to study it: the edition's three steps

The front-matter article "A Life of Quiet Strength" gives a simple method. Use it on each passage above:

1. **Observation: what does it say?** Restate the verse in your own words, or underline the key phrase. For example, in James 1:19, *quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to anger*.
2. **Interpretation: what did the author mean?** Look at why the book was written. Use the edition's book overviews, such as the one for Titus, or a commentary.
3. **Application: "So what?"** What difference does this make at Tuesday's practice? Write one concrete action.

## 6. A four-week plan

| Week | Focus | Read | One action to try |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Anger handled wisely | Ephesians 4 (whole chapter); James 1:19–27 | Decide on a reset routine before you speak: a breath, a step back, or a timeout. Settle every blow-up the same day (Ephesians 4:26). |
| 2 | Self-control | 2 Peter 1:3–11; 1 Peter 1:13–16; Proverbs 16 | Ask an assistant coach or captain to signal you when you start to lose it. |
| 3 | Leadership by example | Titus 1–2; 2 Timothy 2:22–26 | Correct the play, not the person. Name one player each day you'll encourage specifically. |
| 4 | Humility and reconciliation | Matthew 18:15–35; Matthew 20:20–28; Psalm 51 | Apologize to any player or the team where you crossed a line. Ask God for a renewed heart (Psalm 51:10). |

The edition's tips: underline key statements, note the verses that mean the most to you, and study with others. Working through this with another coach or a men's Bible study can help keep you accountable.

---

## A word from Dungy's own example

Tony Dungy is widely known for coaching without screaming at or demeaning his players. He showed that a coach can be demanding and calm at the same time, and that's the "quiet strength" the book's title refers to. The edition puts it this way: if we line up with God's principles, practices and priorities, "we will stand strong no matter what life throws at us." Wanting to change is a good start. Your players will notice the difference.
