# October 4: Analyze, Scrutinize, Question

**This month's theme: The Emotional Self, Coming to Terms with Our Dark Side.** October is about the first law of human nature: we're emotional creatures first and rational ones second. The work is to catch the moment a feeling starts making your decisions for you.

---

### Today's meditation

During the Civil War, Abraham Lincoln had a habit of writing what historians call "hot letters." When a general or a critic made him furious, he wrote out everything he wanted to say, every cutting line. Then he put the letter away. Many of them were later found in his papers marked "never sent, never signed." The anger was real, and he let it out on paper. He just never let it act for him.

When a feeling hits hard, it shows up as certainty: *this reviewer is unfair, and I have to set the record straight right now.* That certainty is the thing to be suspicious of. Ask where the heat is coming from. Is it the words on the screen, or the 4 a.m. starts, the thin margins, and the sense that your work is being judged by a stranger who spent ninety seconds in your shop? Usually the reaction is far bigger than what caused it, and the size of that gap tells you something.

Analyzing a feeling doesn't mean pretending you don't have it. You look at it closely enough that it loses its hold on you, and then you choose what to do.

**Daily Law:** When a feeling arrives with total certainty, treat that certainty as information to examine, not as an order to act.

---

### Your practice today: the hot-letter rule for reviews

1. **Write the angry reply, but not in the review app.** Use a notes app or paper and get all of it out. That draft is for you and no one else.
2. **Wait at least 24 hours.** Don't reply to any review on the day you read it.
3. **Question the draft.** Before you write the real reply, answer three questions:
   - What exactly did this person say, and what did I *hear* (for example, "you're a bad baker")?
   - Is any part of it true or useful: a stale batch, a long line, a rude moment at the counter?
   - Who is my reply actually for? (Hint: the hundreds of future customers who will read it, not the reviewer.)
4. **Then reply briefly and calmly.** Thank them, acknowledge the specific issue, say what you'll do or invite them to contact you directly, and stop there. For example: *"Thanks for letting us know. I'm sorry the croissants weren't up to standard that morning. We bake fresh daily and I'd love to make it right; please ask for me next time you're in."*

**Journal question for tonight:** Which review stung the most, and what fear did it touch: about the business, your skill, or how people see you?

A gracious reply to a harsh review often wins more customers than a dozen five-star ones, because readers can see you stay steady under pressure. The angry version shows them the opposite, and it stays online for years.
