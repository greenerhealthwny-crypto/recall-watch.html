# 7-Day Daily Laws Practice Plan: New Job, Hospital Admin Office

*Built from the themes of Robert Greene's* The Daily Laws *(mainly February "Apprenticeship," April "The Perfect Courtier," May "Supposed Nonplayers," and October/November "The Emotional Self" and "The Rational Human").*

**The idea behind it:** Greene says you can't opt out of power. People who claim they "don't play politics" are still playing, just without paying attention. So the choice isn't between politics and integrity. You either understand the game or get played by it. Greene's own answer to "how do I do this without becoming a snake?" is mostly **observation, self-control, and doing excellent work**. Manipulating people is a small part of it. This plan stays on that side.

**Your rule for the week:** *Use Greene to see clearly, not to deceive.* Read people, manage your own emotions, and make your work and reputation solid. Don't lie, take credit that isn't yours, gossip, or play people against each other. In a hospital, also don't cut corners on anything that touches patient privacy or compliance.

**Daily rhythm (about 15 minutes total):**
- **Morning (5 min):** Read the day's law and pick one concrete action.
- **At work:** Do the practice.
- **Evening (5–10 min):** Answer the reflection questions in a notebook. Keep it private, and don't write names or anything about patients.

---

## Day 1: Become the Apprentice (Observe, Don't Perform)
**Theme (February, Apprenticeship):** The first months in any new place are for learning, not for impressing anyone. Greene's "Ideal Apprenticeship" puts learning ahead of money and status. Swallow your pride, stay quiet, and absorb.

**Practice:**
- Speak less than usual in meetings. When you do speak, ask questions instead of making claims.
- Map the **informal** organization next to the official chart. Who do people go to for answers? Who quietly decides how the schedule, budget, or credentialing actually works? In hospital admin it's often a long-serving coordinator or a charge nurse, not whoever has the biggest title.
- Write down 3 unwritten rules you noticed. Examples: "Emails to Dr. X get answered, calls don't." "Nobody books the conference room without asking Linda."

**Guardrail:** Watching people closely is fine. Snooping is not. Never look at records, schedules, or systems you don't need for your job. In a hospital that's a HIPAA issue, not a gray area.

**Evening reflection:** *What surprised me about how things actually get done here? Where did I feel the urge to prove myself, and did I hold back?*

---

## Day 2: Make Your Superiors Look Good
**Theme (April, The Perfect Courtier, plus Greene's best-known law, "Never Outshine the Master"):** Insecure bosses feel threatened by people who make them look small. Secure bosses still enjoy subordinates who make their lives easier. Either way, a smart new hire makes the boss look competent and doesn't upstage them.

**Practice:**
- Find out what your manager is being judged on. In hospital admin that might be denial rates, audit readiness, patient complaints, staffing coverage, or budget variance. Ask directly: *"What does a great first 90 days look like from your side?"*
- Do one thing today that takes a small burden off your manager without being asked.
- If you spot a better way to do something, take it to your manager privately and frame it as building on their direction. Don't announce it in a group meeting.

**Guardrail:** You're not flattering anyone or hiding your ability. You're choosing *when and how* to show it so it builds trust instead of resentment. Never cover for anything unsafe, dishonest, or noncompliant to make a boss look good.

**Evening reflection:** *Do I understand what my boss is afraid of and what they're hoping for? Did anything I did today put them on the spot?*

---

## Day 3: Read the People, Especially the "Nonplayers"
**Theme (May, Supposed Nonplayers and toxic types):** Greene warns about people who appear to be above the game but are playing hard underneath. Examples: the martyr who's always overworked and wants you to know it, the "I'm just being honest" person who uses bluntness as a weapon, the helpful colleague who quietly gathers information. He also says to judge people by **patterns of behavior** over time, not by first impressions or what they say about themselves.

**Practice:**
- Choose 2–3 colleagues and quietly note how they act, not what they say. Do they keep promises? How do they talk about people who aren't in the room? How do they react under pressure (a surprise audit, a short-staffed day, an angry physician)?
- Watch for **gossip as a test**. If someone shares dirt early, they're often checking whether you'll add to it. Respond in a neutral, friendly way: *"Huh, I haven't worked with them much yet."*
- Notice who is generous with information and credit. Those are your likely allies.

**Guardrail:** Being able to read people isn't permission to label or exclude them. You're protecting yourself, not building a case. Stay pleasant with everyone. Greene's advice is to stay alert, not hostile.

**Evening reflection:** *Did anyone's actions today not match their words? Did I join in on gossip, even just by nodding along?*

---

## Day 4: Master Your Emotional Self
**Theme (October, The Emotional Self):** Greene says emotions are the main reason people lose power. Someone who reacts on impulse becomes predictable and easy to manipulate. Hospitals are high-stress places: clinicians are exhausted, schedules break, and complaints come in all the time. Whoever stays calm becomes the person others rely on.

**Practice:**
- Use the **pause rule**: when an email or comment stings, wait before replying. That might be a few hours or overnight. Draft your reply, then take out every word that's defensive or sarcastic.
- When someone is short with you, a clinician especially, assume it's about their day and not about you. Answer the actual request.
- Notice one moment today when you felt irritated, anxious, or wanted approval. Name the feeling to yourself.

**Guardrail:** Emotional control isn't the same as faking things or swallowing real problems. If something is unsafe, discriminatory, or abusive, raise it calmly and through proper channels. Staying calm makes that kind of report *more* believable.

**Evening reflection:** *What triggered me today? If I'd reacted on the spot, what would it have cost me?*

---

## Day 5: Guard Your Reputation and Say Less
**Theme (April, The Perfect Courtier, plus Greene's laws on reputation and saying less than necessary):** Your reputation gets there before you do. It's built from many small signals: reliability, discretion, tone. In a new job, people form opinions fast and change them slowly. Talking too much, especially complaining or sharing opinions too early, is the easiest way to lose standing.

**Practice:**
- Pick the 1–2 qualities you want people to associate with you. Examples: *"reliable," "discreet," "the person who actually closes the loop."* Do something today that shows each one.
- Close every open loop you have: answer the pending emails, follow up on what you promised, confirm the meeting.
- Keep personal grievances and opinions about other departments to yourself. The less people know about your frustrations, the less they have to use against you.

**Guardrail:** Being discreet is different from being secretive or two-faced. Say the same thing about people whether they're in the room or not. That consistency is what makes a reputation solid, and it's the clearest sign you're not a snake.

**Evening reflection:** *If three coworkers described me in one word tonight, what would they say? Is that the word I want?*

---

## Day 6: Build Alliances Through Real Usefulness
**Theme (April and August, The Perfect Courtier and The Master Persuader):** Greene says people mainly act out of self-interest, so you'll get further by showing how something benefits them than by asking for favors or appealing to gratitude. You can turn that into something honest: **be useful to people and look for real mutual benefit.**

**Practice:**
- Find one person outside your immediate team, such as someone in billing, IT, a unit secretary, or a department coordinator. Learn what makes their job harder, then do one small thing that helps. Send a clean handoff, a clearer request, or a heads-up before something goes wrong.
- When you need something from someone, frame it around what they care about: *"If we get this form in by Thursday, your team won't have to chase the missing auth next week."*
- Give credit in public. Thank people by name in front of their bosses.

**Guardrail:** Greene's laws on credit can be read cynically. Don't. Take credit for your own work, and give other people credit generously. Generosity with credit is one of the few political habits that is also simply good behavior.

**Evening reflection:** *Who did I help today, and did I help in the way they needed? Who do I need on my side six months from now?*

---

## Day 7: Step Back and See the Whole (Rational Review)
**Theme (November, The Rational Human, plus the long view from September, The Grand Strategist):** Greene's ideal is someone who looks at situations with detachment, sees past the small dramas, and keeps working toward a long-term purpose. The question that ties all his advice together: *What am I actually trying to become, and does today's move serve that?*

**Practice (30 minutes, ideally on a day off):**
1. Reread your notes from Days 1–6.
2. Fill in three short lists:
   - **Allies and helpful people** (by role, not gossip)
   - **People to be careful with**, and the specific behavior that worries you
   - **Unwritten rules** you've learned
3. Write one sentence about your **long game**: why you're in hospital admin and what you want to be known for in a year. Examples: *"The person who makes the department audit-proof," "The coordinator physicians trust."*
4. Pick **one** habit from this week to keep for the next month.

**Guardrail check (be honest):**
- Did I say anything this week I wouldn't want repeated to the person I said it about?
- Did I use anything I noticed about someone to hurt them rather than to protect myself?
- Would I be comfortable if my manager, my coworkers, and a patient's family all saw how I handled this week?

If all three answers are clean, you're playing the game without becoming a snake.

## Quick Reference: Greene's Ideas, Snake vs. Not

| Greene idea | Snake version | Version that keeps your integrity |
|---|---|---|
| Never outshine the master | Hide your ability, flatter constantly | Make your boss look good, offer ideas privately |
| Conceal your intentions | Lie about what you want | Don't overshare your plans or frustrations early |
| Win through actions, not argument | Quietly sabotage people you disagree with | Let good results settle disputes |
| Appeal to self-interest | Manipulate people with false promises | Frame requests around real shared benefit |
| Read people's character | Gather ammunition | Judge people by patterns so you can protect yourself |
| Master your emotions | Fake warmth while you plot | Stay calm so you can see clearly and respond well |

**A last note on Greene:** He describes how power works without judging it, and some of his laws are cynical if you apply them literally. Read them to understand the game you're in, then decide what you'll do based on your own values. In a hospital, your best political asset is the same thing that makes you good at the job: being reliable, discreet, and calm, and keeping in mind that the work exists to serve patients.

Repeat the week as often as you like. Many people run it again at the 30- and 90-day marks, when the office politics have become clearer.
