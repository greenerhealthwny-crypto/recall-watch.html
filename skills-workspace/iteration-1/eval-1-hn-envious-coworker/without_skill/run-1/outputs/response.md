That's an unsettling thing to hear, and it makes sense that it stings more because she was so welcoming at first. Here's how I'd think about it and what I'd do.

## First: check what you actually know

You **heard** this secondhand. Before you build a strategy around it, be honest about how solid the information is:

- **Who told you, and why?** Did they hear it directly, or is it gossip passed along a few times? Does the messenger have their own reasons?
- **What exactly was said?** "This project carries risk" is a normal thing to say about a project. "She's in over her head" is a judgment about you. These are very different, and secondhand retelling tends to turn the first into the second.
- **Where was it said?** Raising a concern in a 1:1 with the manager is different from saying it around the office.

You don't need certainty to act, but the details change which response fits.

## What might be going on

There are a few realistic explanations, and more than one can be true at once:

1. **Rivalry or envy.** This is the obvious read, and it fits a common pattern. When you were new you were no threat, so being generous cost her nothing and made her look good. Three months in, you have a visible project and you're getting attention. If she sees you as competition for recognition, promotion, or the manager's trust, she may be undercutting you quietly while staying friendly in public, because open hostility would make *her* look bad. Praising you in meetings and doubting you in private is a classic way to do that.
2. **Real concerns, handled badly.** She may honestly think the project is risky and that you lack context she has. Some people avoid conflict and take worries to the manager instead of to you. Still unfair to you, but not malicious.
3. **Self-protection.** If the project touches her area, or a failure would land on her, she may be covering herself ahead of time ("I flagged this").
4. **The manager asked her.** Managers often ask experienced team members how a new person is doing. She may have answered honestly in a conversation she didn't start.

The public-praise, private-criticism split does fit #1 or #3 more than #2. But you can handle this well without knowing her motives, so don't let figuring her out become the project.

## How to handle it

**1. Don't confront her with the gossip.** "I heard you told the manager I'm in over my head" usually backfires. She denies it or explains it away, the person who told you gets exposed, and you come across as defensive or as someone who keeps tabs on hallway talk. You can't win a he-said-she-said in your first few months.

**2. Get ahead of the story with your manager.** This matters most. Your manager's opinion counts more than Dana's, so shape it directly and don't mention her:

- Ask for a short project check-in: *"I'd like to walk you through where the project stands, the main risks I see, and how I'm handling them. Is there anything you're worried about that I should address?"*
- Name the risks yourself before anyone else does. A newcomer who says "here are the three things that could go wrong and my plan for each" sounds the opposite of "in over her head."
- Leave room for your manager to bring up concerns. If Dana's comments shaped their view, they'll often show up here, and you can answer the substance.
- Keep these updates going regularly. Don't make it a one-time defense.

**3. Take any real substance seriously.** Is there anything to the "risky" critique? Three months is early. If there are gaps, close them openly: ask for a review, bring in someone experienced, or adjust scope. Fixing a real weakness takes away the ammunition, and your manager sees you as self-aware.

**4. Change how you deal with Dana, quietly.** Stay friendly, professional, and warm on the surface. Don't freeze her out, because that gives her a story ("she's gotten defensive"). But:

- Share less about your doubts, mistakes, and internal struggles with her. Assume anything you vent could reach the manager in a less flattering form.
- Optionally, **pull her in directly**: *"You know this area well, and I'd value your read on the risky parts of my project. What would you watch out for?"* If her concerns are real, you get useful input and she has a direct channel she no longer needs to route around you. If she's working against you, it's harder to tell the manager you're in over your head after you openly asked her advice and acted on it. Either way, you look good.

**5. Build your own relationships.** Don't let Dana be your main guide to the team. Get to know other colleagues, find a mentor or ally outside her circle, and let your work be visible through your own updates rather than through her.

**6. Write things down.** Keep a simple running record of decisions, progress, risks you flagged, and outcomes. It helps your updates and your review, and if things ever escalate into something clearly unfair, you'll have facts to point to and not just feelings.

## When to escalate

If the pattern continues and you find more direct evidence, like her misrepresenting facts, taking credit for your work, or keeping information from you, then a calm, specific conversation is warranted. Have it with her first if you feel safe doing so ("I want to make sure we're on the same page. If you have concerns about my project, I'd rather hear them directly"), or with your manager, sticking to behaviors and their effects, not motives. Until then, the best response is mostly indirect: steady communication, strong work, and a professional relationship with Dana that's warm but careful.

## The bottom line

You may not be able to stop her from talking. You *can* make sure your manager's view of you comes mainly from you and from your results, not from her. Three months in, that matters far more than working out exactly why she's doing it.
