# What's going on with Dana, and how to handle it

This is close to a textbook case of what Robert Greene, in *The Laws of Human Nature*, calls **the Law of Envy: "Beware the Fragile Ego."** It also overlaps with his **Law of Aggression: "See the Hostility Behind the Friendly Façade."** Below is what's probably happening, one check on your own side, and a plan.

---

## 1. What's probably happening

### Envy wearing the mask of friendship
Greene's central point about envy is that **nobody admits to it**. Envy is the painful feeling that someone else's success makes us smaller, and it's shameful to own, so it comes out in disguise: as criticism, gossip, "concern," or even friendship. Look at what you've seen:

| What Dana does | How Greene would read it |
|---|---|
| Very friendly right away: lunch, "I'll show you the ropes" | **Overly eager early friendship** is one of his named warning signs. Getting close to a promising newcomer lets someone size you up, and sometimes it lets them keep you in a junior role. |
| Congratulates you in team meetings | Public praise costs nothing and keeps her reputation as a generous colleague safe. It's the mask doing its job. |
| Tells your manager you're "risky" and "in over your head" | This is the envy coming out, framed as **"concern"** and aimed at the person who decides your future. |
| Supportive in front of others, undermining in private | Greene's **Passive-Aggressive Charmer**: a pleasant front covering hostility. |

Her specific phrases fit his **Insinuating-Doubt** strategy. She isn't attacking your work outright, which would look petty. She's planting doubt about whether you can handle it. "In over his/her head" makes her sound worried for you while it chips away at your manager's confidence in you.

### Why now?
Greene says envy is **most intense between peers** and is set off by particular things: **a peer pulling ahead**, sudden success, or talent that looks effortless. Three months in, you have a project visible enough for her to talk to the manager about. While you were the new person who needed her help, you weren't a threat. Once you started to look like competition, the arrangement changed.

He tells the story of **Jane Williams and Mary Shelley**. Jane became Mary's close friend and confidante, and the whole time she was quietly undermining Mary and taking the attention Mary wanted. The friendship was real enough to be convincing, and that's why it worked.

### Which type might Dana be?
Greene describes several kinds of enviers. Two are most likely here:
- **The Status Fiend** measures herself by rank and resents anyone gaining on her. If she's been there longer and expected to be "the one," your quick visibility hurts.
- **The Attacher** gets close to promising people and turns on them once they start to rise.

Don't decide yet. Watch which one fits as more behavior comes in.

---

## 2. Check your own side first

Greene insists on this step. Ask yourself:

- **How solid is the information?** You "heard" this. Was it from the manager, or from a third person who might have their own agenda or have garbled what she said? Gossip about gossip is the weakest evidence there is.
- **Is there a fair reading?** Could Dana have raised a real concern about project risk, clumsily, that got retold as a personal attack? Is any part of "risky" accurate?
- **Confirmation bias.** Now that you suspect her, every smile will look fake. Greene's ideal is the **Inner Athena**: calm, clear, unhurried. Judge by **patterns of repeated behavior**, not one secondhand report.

One data point doesn't establish a character. Two or three consistent ones do. Your job right now is to **observe, not retaliate**.

---

## 3. How to handle it

### Don't
- **Don't confront her angrily or accuse her.** You'd be giving her what she wants. You'd look emotional and insecure, which supports "in over their head," and she'd play the hurt party. She has the more established position, and in an open fight she wins the sympathy.
- **Don't badmouth her to the manager or the team.** You'd end up playing the same game she is, from a weaker spot.
- **Don't stop being cordial.** A visible chill tells her you know, and she'll change tactics.

### Do

**1. Protect yourself where it matters: with your manager.**
Envy can only damage you through your manager's view of you, so fill that space with facts. Don't mention Dana. Set up a proactive check-in on your project:
- "I want to make sure you have full visibility into where this stands. Here are the risks I've identified and how I'm mitigating each one."
- Name the risks before anyone else can. That takes the force out of "risky" because it shows you see the whole picture, and it disproves "in over your head" in the most convincing way.
- Ask what the manager would like to see more of. People who are in over their heads don't ask that question.

From then on, send short, regular status updates. A paper trail of competence is your best defense against insinuation.

**2. Reduce the envy trigger.**
Greene's defenses against envy are not glamorous, but they work:
- **Downplay a little.** Don't hide your success, but don't parade it either.
- **Share credit visibly,** including with Dana if she actually helped you early on. ("Dana helped me get oriented when I started; that made a difference.") It's disarming and hard for her to keep undermining someone who credits her in public.
- **Show a flaw or ask for her input on something small.** Envy feeds on the sense that someone has it easy. Letting her see your effort and imperfection takes some of the sting away.

**3. Make it harder for her to whisper.**
- Keep her in the loop through **open channels**: group updates, shared docs, project meetings. The more transparent your work is, the less room there is for a private story about it.
- If you want to test her, ask her directly and pleasantly, at a normal moment: *"You've been here longer than me. Do you see any risks in my project I'm missing? I'd really value your take."* That gives her a legitimate way to raise concerns. If she has real ones, you've learned something. If she says "No, it looks great!" to your face and keeps saying "risky" to the manager, you've confirmed the pattern without any confrontation.

**4. Keep a quiet record.**
Write down dates and specifics: what you heard, from whom, what she said in meetings. You won't use it to attack her. It's there so that if this becomes a real problem, such as a performance conversation, you have facts and not feelings.

**5. Adjust your trust without announcing it.**
Keep being friendly with her. Just stop sharing anything sensitive: doubts, setbacks, frustrations with the project. Anything vulnerable you give an envious colleague can become material for "in over their head."

### If it continues
If the pattern is confirmed and starts affecting your standing, raise it with your manager calmly and in terms of the work, not the person: *"I've heard some concerns about the project may be going around. I'd rather hear them directly so I can address them. Is there anything you're worried about?"* That brings it into the open without you making an accusation.

---

## 4. The bigger lesson

Greene's advice is to **stop taking this personally.** Dana's behavior almost certainly comes from her own fragile ego and her place in the pecking order, which existed before you arrived. Study her the way a scientist studies a specimen, with calm interest and without moral outrage. That distance protects you emotionally and keeps your judgment clear.

Watch the same dynamic in yourself, too. As you succeed, someone newer will eventually pass you. The higher response Greene describes is **Mitfreude**, taking joy in others' success, and turning envy into **emulation** instead of sabotage. Being that colleague for the next new hire is how you avoid becoming a Dana.

**Short version:** stay friendly, give your manager overwhelming evidence that you're competent, share credit, keep your guard up, and let your results answer her.
