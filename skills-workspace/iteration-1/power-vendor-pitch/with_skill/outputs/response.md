# Short answer: probably not a scam, but this is a sales script built to make you decide on their terms

You're not necessarily being cheated. But all four parts of the pitch are standard pressure tactics, and Robert Greene's *48 Laws of Power* describes each one. When someone uses all four on you at the same time, slow down. You can say no to the deadline without saying no to the product.

## What's happening, law by law

| What the rep is doing | The law being used on you | What it's designed to do |
|---|---|---|
| "Free" setup and training if you sign | **Law 40: Despise the Free Lunch** and **Law 12: Selective Generosity** | A gift right before the ask makes you feel you owe them something and lowers your guard. The setup isn't free. It's built into a 3-year price you haven't negotiated yet. |
| Only two options, both 3-year terms | **Law 31: Control the Options** | You feel like you're choosing, but both choices give them what they want: a long lock-in. The real decision ("Should we commit for 3 years, and to this vendor?") never gets asked. Greene's example is Kissinger handing Nixon a set of options built to lead to the one Kissinger wanted. |
| "You're so smart for seeing the value" | **Law 21: Play a Sucker to Catch a Sucker** and **Law 33: Find the Thumbscrew** | Flattering how smart you are makes it harder to back out, because questioning the deal would mean questioning your own judgment. In the great diamond hoax, the San Francisco financiers were sure they were the smart ones in the room. |
| "This week" | **Law 35: Master the Art of Timing** ("forced time") | A false deadline rushes you so you don't compare options, check references, or read the contract. Greene's rule is to never look hurried, and never let someone else set your clock. |

Greene's general defense: **slow down, say less, judge by actions rather than words, and keep your own goals in view.**

## What to do

### 1. Take back the timeline (Law 35)
Tell the rep calmly: *"We don't make 3-year commitments in a week. We'll decide by [date 3–4 weeks out]."* Then pay attention to what happens. A good vendor will still want your business next month. If the "free" offer disappears because you asked for three weeks, that tells you how much it was really worth.

### 2. Ask for the options they didn't offer (Law 31 defense)
Don't pick A or B. Ask for prices on:
- A **month-to-month** or **1-year** term
- **Setup and training as a separate line item**, so you can see what "free" really costs
- The **same 3-year deal with an exit clause** (for example, cancel with 60–90 days' notice after year 1, or cancel without penalty if they miss service levels)

If they won't even give you a 1-year price, the lock-in is probably where their profit is.

### 3. Separate the gift from the decision (Laws 12 and 40)
Compare the **total 3-year cost**: subscription × 36 months, plus price increases, per-user fees, support tiers, and add-ons. A $3,000 setup that's "free" on a contract you can't leave isn't a discount. It's a cost you can't avoid.

### 4. Say less (Law 4)
Don't tell the rep your budget, how badly you need the software, or which competitors you're looking at. When they quote a price, stay quiet for a moment. People will often fill a silence with a concession.

### 5. Plan to the end (Law 29)
Before you sign anything multi-year, know how you would leave it. Have someone (ideally your attorney or practice-management advisor) check the contract for:
- **Auto-renewal** clauses and how much notice you need to give to cancel
- **Annual price increases** (look for a cap, such as 3–5% a year)
- **Early termination fees** (some charge the whole remaining contract)
- **Getting your data out**: in what format, at what cost, and how long you have after the contract ends. This matters most for a clinic.
- **A HIPAA Business Associate Agreement (BAA)** if the software touches patient information. Don't go live without one.
- **Uptime and support commitments** written into the contract, not just promised by the rep
- What happens if the vendor is **acquired or goes out of business**

### 6. Make them prove it (Law 9: actions, not arguments)
Ask for **2–3 references from clinics your size** that have used it for more than a year, and call them. Ask them: "What surprised you after you signed? How was the training, really? Have you tried to get your data out?" Get at least **one competing quote**. That gives you leverage and a reality check.

### 7. Watch for the thumbscrew in yourself (Law 33)
The flattery works because most of us like being seen as the smart, decisive one. Tell yourself the smart move is to take your time. If it helps, bring a colleague into the decision (*"My partner / office manager has to sign off"*). Then the pressure isn't on you alone, and you have a polite reason to slow things down.

## A script you can use

> "Thanks, we're interested. Before we decide, I need three things: pricing for a 1-year term, the setup and training cost listed separately, and a copy of the full contract including termination, renewal, and data-export terms. We'll also need a signed BAA. We'll make a decision by [date]. If that timeline doesn't work for you, we understand."

That last sentence matters. It shows you're willing to walk away, and that willingness is where your negotiating power comes from (Law 20: Do Not Commit to Anyone).

## The reversals (where this advice has limits)

- **Some deadlines are real.** End-of-quarter discounts exist, and reps do have quotas. If the product clearly fits, references check out, the contract has a reasonable exit, and the 3-year total beats the alternatives, signing can be a good decision. The problem isn't a 3-year term. The problem is a 3-year term you didn't get to examine.
- **Free can actually be free.** Greene's reversal of Law 40: take the free lunch when it costs you nothing and you're under no obligation. If you'd be happy with this vendor for 3 years anyway, free training is a real benefit.
- **Don't treat the rep like an enemy.** Their behavior is normal sales technique, not a sign they're a bad person. Stay friendly and firm. You may be working with this company for years, and **Law 19 (Know Who You're Dealing With)** says not to make needless enemies.
- Greene's book is history and interpretation, not research. Use it to spot the tactics. The decision itself should come from your clinic's needs and the numbers.

**Bottom line:** You aren't being scammed, but you are being steered. Push back on the deadline, ask for the options they left out, price everything separately, read the exit terms, and call references. If the deal is good, it will still be good in three weeks.
