Your instincts are worth listening to. A candidate who is very charming, gets vague when you ask for specifics, and badmouths former bosses is showing a pattern that hiring research and experienced managers both flag. That doesn't mean this person is a bad hire. It means the interview hasn't given you enough evidence yet, and the panel's enthusiasm may be a reaction to charisma more than to proof of competence.

In a small nonprofit, one program coordinator can shape your relationships with funders, partners, and the community. Missing the warning signs costs more there than at a large organization. Before you make an offer, here's what I'd do.

## 1. Separate what you saw from how it felt

Ask each panelist to write down, on their own, before you talk as a group:
- Which specific things did the candidate say they *personally* did? (Not "we launched," but "I built the intake process.")
- What results did they name, with numbers, dates, or names?
- What concerns did they have, even small ones?

When panelists talk first and write later, the most enthusiastic voice usually sets the group's opinion. If the written answers are mostly "great energy, would be fun to work with," then the panel liked the person but didn't actually see evidence they can do the job.

## 2. Run a follow-up interview built to get specifics

Vagueness can come from nerves, from being humble about team credit, or from exaggerating. A structured follow-up will show you which one it is. Pick one or two projects they mentioned and go deep:

- "Walk me through the [X] project from the start. What was the situation when you came in?"
- "What was your specific role? Who else was involved, and what did they own?"
- "What was the budget, the timeline, and how many people did it serve?"
- "What went wrong, and what did you do about it?"
- "How did you measure success? What were the actual numbers?"
- "If I called the person who supervised that project, what would they say your biggest contribution was?"

**What a good answer sounds like:** concrete details, comfort with follow-up questions, honesty about mistakes, and credit shared with others where it was earned.

**What a warning answer sounds like:** a story that changes or grows when you probe, claiming credit for everything, deflecting ("it's hard to explain"), or bringing up blame or conflict again.

## 3. Give them a work sample

This is the most reliable check you have, because charm doesn't help much here. Use a short, realistic task (paid if it's more than an hour or two) that matches the real job, for example:
- Draft a 90-day implementation plan for one of your actual programs (with sensitive details removed).
- Write a short grant progress report from a set of sample data.
- Plan a community health event: timeline, budget, volunteers, logistics.
- Handle a written scenario: "A partner clinic just pulled out two weeks before launch. What do you do?"

Notice whether the work is organized, realistic, and detailed, and whether they ask good clarifying questions.

## 4. Take the badmouthing seriously, but check it

Criticizing *two* former bosses in an interview is a meaningful signal. Bad bosses exist, but most candidates know not to complain about them to a potential employer. It can point to:
- Poor judgment or weak professional boundaries
- A habit of blaming others and not taking responsibility
- Trouble with authority or feedback, which matters a lot in a small organization where you'll likely be their direct supervisor

To test it fairly, ask: "You mentioned some challenges with past supervisors. Looking back, what would you do differently in those situations?" Someone self-aware will think about their own part. Someone who keeps blaming will repeat the complaints.

## 5. Do real reference checks, including ones they didn't hand you

This is where the specific claims get confirmed or fall apart.
- **Ask for a former direct supervisor as a reference**, ideally from one of the jobs where they had the conflict. If they refuse or can't name anyone, that tells you something.
- **Verify the specific projects.** "They told us they led the X initiative, which served about 400 families. Can you tell me about their role in it?" Then listen for whether the reference confirms it, plays it down, or hesitates.
- **Ask questions that are hard to answer with a script:**
  - "Would you hire them again? Why or why not?"
  - "How did they handle feedback or disagreement with you?"
  - "What kind of support or supervision did they need to do their best work?"
  - "How did they get along with coworkers, partners, and the people you serve?"
- **Pay attention to tone and what isn't said.** Faint praise, long pauses, and "I can only confirm dates of employment" (when other questions would normally get answered) are all worth noting.
- **Backchannel references** (people you know at their former organizations) can help a lot. Health and nonprofit circles are small. Be careful and fair, follow your organization's policies and local law, and never let a backchannel comment jeopardize the candidate's current job.

## 6. Check the basics

- Confirm employment dates and titles.
- Verify any credentials or certifications they mentioned (for example, CHES, community health worker certification, project management training).
- Look at their LinkedIn and any public work (reports, presentations, news coverage of programs) to see whether it matches their story.
- If the role involves vulnerable clients, health data, or money, run whatever background checks your policies and funders require.

## 7. Decide using the evidence, not the vibe

After the steps above, ask yourself:
- Did their specific claims hold up when you probed and checked them?
- Did the work sample show they can actually coordinate a program?
- Did the references describe someone you'd want to supervise?
- Did they show any accountability for the past conflicts?

If the answers are mostly yes, you may have a charismatic person who is also capable, which is a great combination for a role that involves partners and the community. If the specifics stay vague, the references are lukewarm, and the pattern of blaming others continues, I'd pass, however much the panel enjoyed them. A pleasant interview lasts an hour, but you'll be working with this person every day.

## If you do hire them

- Set clear, measurable goals for the first 90 days, written down and agreed on.
- Use a probationary or introductory period if your policies allow one.
- Hold regular one-on-ones early on, and watch how they handle feedback and who gets credit or blame when things go wrong.
- Write things down. If problems show up, you'll want a clear record.

**Bottom line:** You noticed something your panel may have missed. Don't drop the candidate because of a gut feeling, but don't make an offer on charm either. Run a structured follow-up, a work sample, and serious reference checks, and the evidence will tell you which kind of candidate this is.
