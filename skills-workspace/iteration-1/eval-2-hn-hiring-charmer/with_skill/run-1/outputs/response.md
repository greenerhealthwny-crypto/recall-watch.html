Trust your hesitation. The fact that the whole panel loved this person is a reason to slow down, not to speed up. Here's how I'd look at it, using Robert Greene's *The Laws of Human Nature*, which is largely about judging character before you commit to someone.

## What the pattern suggests

Greene's main rule for judging people is **"character is destiny."** People repeat their patterns, so judge them by what they've actually done over time, not by how well they come across in a room. Each thing you noticed is mild on its own. Together they match a type he warns about.

- **Charm with no specifics.** Greene calls one of his "toxic types" **the Big Talker**: dazzling ideas, lots of energy, very little follow-through, and someone else always to blame. Big Talkers stand out in interviews because interviews reward the exact thing they're good at. Greene also points out that **real events are messy.** Someone who actually ran a project remembers the budget fight, the partner who dropped out, and what they'd do differently. Polished stories with no detail are the warning sign.
- **Badmouthing two former bosses.** One of the clearest character signs Greene lists is *how someone talks about past partners and bosses.* One bad boss happens to everyone. Two, raised without being asked, in an interview where they're supposed to be on their best behavior, points toward **blame bias**: they don't learn from what goes wrong, they assign it to someone else. It can also point to the **Relentless Rebel**, who is charming and anti-authority and eventually can't stand anyone above them, including you.
- **Grand claims about leading projects.** "I led" is easy to say. In a small nonprofit, the program coordinator *is* the follow-through: grant deliverables, partner sites, data for funders, volunteer schedules. Any gap between the story and what they really did will show up quickly.

To be fair to the candidate: this is a hypothesis to test, not a verdict. Some people are vague because they're nervous or were never taught to talk about their own work. Some former bosses really were bad. The steps below are meant to tell those cases apart.

## Check your side (and the panel's)

Greene always asks you to look at your own biases too:

- **Appearance bias:** we take charm and confidence at face value. Charisma is the trait that most reliably overrides judgment.
- **Group effect:** panels move toward agreement. Once two people are excited, the others tend to adopt the mood. Did each panelist write down a rating *before* you discussed the candidate? If not, the enthusiasm may be one person's reaction that spread to the rest.
- **The "inflaming individual":** some people produce a strong emotional reaction in everyone they meet. Treat that reaction as information about the candidate, not as evidence they can do the job.

A good question to ask the panel: *"What specific thing did they tell us they accomplished, with a number or an outcome attached?"* If nobody can answer, the panel was responding to charm, not to results.

## What to do before you make an offer

**1. Hold a short second interview built around specifics.** Pick one or two projects they claimed and go deep:
- "Walk me through the timeline. What was the budget and who approved it?"
- "What was *your* part, and what did others on the team do?"
- "What went wrong, and what did you change?"
- "How did you measure whether it worked? What were the numbers?"
- "If I called the person who supervised that project, what would they say you did best, and what would they say you could do better?"

Watch for stories that stay at the level of "we transformed outreach," a "we" that turns into "I" only for the wins, and setbacks that are always someone else's fault.

**2. Ask about the former bosses directly, but neutrally.** "You mentioned it was hard working with [boss]. What did you learn from that, and what would you do differently?" A strong candidate shows some ownership: "I should have raised concerns earlier." A weak one starts another round of grievances. How someone talks about a conflict tells you how they'll talk about you to your board, funders and partners.

**3. Check references, including people they didn't list.** Ask for a reference from one of the two supervisors they criticized, or from someone who directly supervised the projects they described. If they refuse or dodge, that tells you something. On every reference call, ask:
- "What did they personally own on [project]?"
- "How did they handle a deadline they were going to miss?"
- "Would you hire them again, for a role with a lot of independent follow-through?" (Listen for hesitation.)

Where it's appropriate, a quick call to someone in your network who knows their previous organization can also help. Keep it fair and focused on the job.

**4. Give a practical work sample.** For example: "Here's a mock community health program with a $40K grant, three partner sites and a six-month timeline. Draft a one-page work plan with milestones and how you'd report outcomes to the funder." Give the same task to your other finalists. Big Talkers do well talking and much worse on a concrete deliverable. This is also the fairest way to judge everyone.

**5. Watch how they treat people with less power.** How did they treat whoever scheduled the interview, greeted them, or answered their emails? Greene treats behavior toward subordinates and support staff as one of the most reliable character tells, because people relax their act around those they don't think matter.

**6. Compare them to your other candidates on the job, not on the impression.** Score everyone against the same job-related criteria: organization, follow-through, partner relationships, reporting and data, and fit with your mission. Sometimes the less impressive interviewee is the stronger coordinator.

## If you still want to hire them

If they do well on specifics, references and the work sample, that's good news. Maybe they just interview vaguely. If you go ahead:
- Put clear 30/60/90-day deliverables in writing so expectations aren't vague.
- Use a real probationary period and hold check-ins that look at completed work, not updates on how things are going.
- Notice early whether problems keep getting explained as someone else's fault.

## The bigger idea

Greene's broader advice is to become a calm, strategic observer of people: not cynical, not swept along, just watching what someone actually does over time. In a small health nonprofit, one hire shapes your culture, your partners' trust and your funders' confidence. A few extra days of checking costs much less than a bad hire. If this candidate is the real thing, the checking will show that and you can hire them with confidence. If not, you'll have avoided a mistake the whole panel was about to make together.

*(This draws on Greene's synthesis of psychology and history. His "types" are archetypes for spotting patterns, not diagnoses, and the checks above are what let the evidence decide.)*
