# Following Linda: The Laws That Apply and a 90-Day Plan

You're dealing with two separate problems, and they need different handling:

1. **A legitimacy problem.** The staff are measuring you against someone they loved, and to them she now looks better than she ever was.
2. **A process problem.** Client intake is a mess and needs an overhaul.

If you go after #2 before you've made progress on #1, people will hear the overhaul as "the new director tearing down Linda's legacy." That's what "that's not how Linda did it" really means. It's grief, and it's loyalty, and it's also a fair question: *why should we trust you?*

The good news is that *The 48 Laws of Power* speaks almost directly to this. Below are the laws that matter most, the ones to be careful with, and then a concrete 90-day plan.

---

## The laws that apply most

### Law 41: Avoid Stepping into a Great Man's Shoes
**This is your central law.** Greene's point is that when you follow a beloved or famous predecessor, anything you achieve gets credited to their foundation and anything you get wrong gets blamed on you. Trying to *be* Linda, or to beat her on her own terms, is a losing game. You have to set up your own identity and your own way of doing things so you aren't permanently measured against hers.

**In practice:**
- Don't copy her style, and don't define yourself as "not Linda" either. Both keep her at the center.
- Build your own territory: a clear, visible agenda that belongs to you. A fixed intake process can be that signature.
- Don't fall into Greene's trap in the other direction. You don't have to wipe out her memory. **Honor the predecessor and separate yourself from her.** The usual move is to praise what she built and position yourself as the person who will *carry it forward* in a new era, not replace it.

### Law 45: Preach the Need for Change, but Never Reform Too Much at Once
**This is your second most important law.** People are attached to familiar forms even when those forms are broken. Greene's advice is to wrap change in respect for the past: keep familiar names, rituals and symbols while changing what actually happens underneath, and change in steps rather than all at once.

**In practice:**
- Make the case for change *before* you announce any. Let the data and the staff's own frustrations show that intake is broken.
- Present the overhaul as continuity: "Linda built this program on getting clients the right help quickly. Intake is where that's slipping, and I want to protect what she built."
- Roll it out in phases. Keep familiar forms, names or meeting rhythms where they don't do harm.

### Law 43: Work on the Hearts and Minds of Others
Coercion produces resistance. People follow when they feel invested. Find out what each person cares about and why, and let them have a hand in shaping the change.

**In practice:** Hold one-on-ones with everyone. Have the people who do intake every day help design the fix. A process they built is one they'll defend. A process you handed down is one they'll quietly undermine with "that's not how Linda did it."

### Law 38: Think as You Like but Behave Like Others
If you flaunt that you're different from the group too early, you look like an outsider attacking its values. Privately you can think the old way is broken. Publicly, show respect for the culture before you try to shift it.

**In practice:** In the first month, don't criticize past practice in front of the team, even when it's plainly dysfunctional. Ask questions instead: "Walk me through why we do it this way."

### Law 19: Know Who You're Dealing With; Do Not Offend the Wrong Person
Some staff are just grieving. Others may have lost status when Linda left, or wanted your job, or were her informal lieutenants. Those are very different people and need different handling.

**In practice:** Map the room. Who were Linda's closest people? Who held informal authority? Who applied for your role? Who is quietly frustrated with the current intake mess and would welcome change? That last group is your coalition.

### Law 4: Always Say Less Than Necessary
New leaders often over-explain, over-promise and over-justify, especially when they feel they're being compared to someone. Every extra word becomes something to debate.

**In practice:** When you hear "That's not how Linda did it," don't argue and don't defend yourself. Try something like: *"Tell me more about how she handled it. What worked about it?"* Then listen. Often you'll learn something useful, and the comparison loses its sting because you didn't fight it.

### Law 28: Enter Action with Boldness
Once you've done the groundwork, carry out the change with confidence. Hesitating, or reversing course at the first complaint, tells people that resistance works.

**In practice:** Take your time *deciding*. Once you've decided, don't waver. A half-committed rollout is the worst of both worlds.

### Law 29: Plan All the Way to the End
Think through the whole arc before you start: who resists, where the new process will break, what "done" looks like, and how you'll measure it.

**In practice:** Write down the end state for intake (cycle time, error rate, client experience, staff workload) before you launch anything, and plan the resistance points ahead of time.

### Law 46: Never Appear Too Perfect
Linda has become a legend, and you can't compete with a legend. What you *can* be is human and approachable. Admitting what you don't know yet builds trust and takes the edge off envy.

**In practice:** Say it openly: "I'm not going to get everything right, and I'm relying on you to tell me what I'm missing."

### Law 9: Win Through Your Actions, Never Through Argument
You won't talk anyone out of their loyalty to Linda. A visible win will do more than any speech.

**In practice:** Find one small, painful intake problem and fix it quickly (see days 31-60). Results settle the "is the new director any good?" question better than words.

## Laws to use carefully, or deliberately ignore

The 48 Laws describes how power works. It doesn't tell you how to treat your team. A few laws would backfire badly here:

- **Law 7 (Get Others to Do the Work, but Take the Credit):** Do the opposite. Give the team generous, public credit for the intake redesign. Your legitimacy grows when *they* look good under your leadership.
- **Law 15 (Crush Your Enemy Totally):** Don't treat Linda loyalists as enemies. Most of them are your future best employees once they trust you. Save firm action for real, ongoing sabotage after fair warning, and even then handle it through normal performance management.
- **Law 3 (Conceal Your Intentions):** Don't hide that intake is changing. Secrecy in a grieving team breeds rumors. Be open about *the goal* and humble about *the method*.
- **Law 1 (Never Outshine the Master):** This one does apply, but upward. Make sure your own boss or board backs the intake overhaul, and present it as advancing *their* priorities.

## Your 90-day plan

### Days 1-30: Listen, map, and honor (no major changes)

**Goal:** Earn the right to change things. Understand intake in depth.

- **One-on-ones with every direct report** (and key frontline intake staff). Ask:
  - What did Linda do that you most want to keep?
  - What frustrates you about how intake works today?
  - If you were me, what would you fix first?
  - What should I know that nobody will tell me?
- **Honor Linda publicly, once and sincerely.** Acknowledge her 20 years at a team meeting. Name specific things she built. If she's open to it, consider a coffee with her: her blessing ("you're in good hands") can carry real weight, and she may know exactly where the intake bodies are buried. Don't keep invoking her after that.
- **Walk the intake process yourself.** Shadow staff. Follow a few real client files from first contact to service. Act as a mystery client if that's appropriate.
- **Gather baseline data:** time from first contact to completed intake, drop-off rate, duplicate data entry, error and rework rate, staff hours per intake, client complaints, compliance gaps.
- **Map the people:** grievers, informal leaders, possible rivals, and quiet reformers who are already frustrated.
- **Handling "That's not how Linda did it":** treat it as information, not a challenge. "Tell me how she did it. What made that work?" Write down the answers.
- **Brief your boss** on what you're seeing and get explicit support for an intake redesign.

**Avoid:** announcing an overhaul, rebranding things, changing your office or long-standing rituals, criticizing the past.

### Days 31-60: Build the case and the coalition, and get one quick win

**Goal:** Turn private observations into shared ownership of the problem.

- **Share the diagnosis, not the solution.** Show the team the intake data and the client journey map. Let them reach the conclusion themselves: "This is costing clients and costing us."
- **Frame it as continuity:** "Linda built this program around [her core value, e.g. treating every client with dignity]. Our intake process is getting in the way of that. I want us to fix it together."
- **Form a small intake redesign team** of 4-6 people. Include at least one respected Linda loyalist and at least one frustrated reformer. The loyalist's involvement does more for legitimacy than anything you can say.
- **Deliver one quick win in the first two to three weeks of this phase.** Pick a narrow, visible, low-risk fix that staff have complained about: a redundant form, a duplicate data-entry step, a confusing handoff, an unclear eligibility checklist. Fix it fast and credit the people who suggested it.
- **Define what success looks like**, with targets for the metrics from your baseline.
- **Decide what to keep.** Have the team explicitly name the parts of the current process (or of Linda's approach) that should be preserved. This is Law 45 in practice: change the substance, keep the meaningful symbols.

### Days 61-90: Pilot, then commit

**Goal:** Launch the redesigned intake process in a controlled way and show that it works.

- **Pilot the new process** with one team, one program or one intake channel for 3-4 weeks. Don't do a big-bang launch.
- **Document it simply:** a one-page flow, clear roles, a standard checklist or form, and handoff rules. Train people hands-on, not by memo.
- **Track the metrics weekly** and share them openly, including what isn't working. Adjust quickly.
- **Commit firmly (Law 28).** Expect a dip and some complaints. Listen to the substance, fix real problems, and don't reverse course just because something is unfamiliar.
- **Celebrate and credit** the redesign team publicly when the pilot shows results.
- **At day 90:** present results to your boss and team, set the full rollout timeline, and outline what comes next. By now the story should have moved from "the person who replaced Linda" to "the director who fixed intake with us."

## Handling resistance as it comes up

| What you hear | What it usually means | How to respond |
|---|---|---|
| "That's not how Linda did it." | Grief, or anxiety about what's changing | "What did you like about her way? Let's make sure we keep that." |
| Quiet non-compliance with new steps | Not convinced, or not trained enough | Talk privately, ask what's getting in the way, retrain. Escalate only if it continues. |
| One person leading an opposition camp | Lost status, or wanted your job | Meet one-on-one, give them a real role in the redesign, and be clear about expectations. |
| "We tried that before and it didn't work." | Useful history | "What happened? What would make it work this time?" |

## The short version

- **Don't compete with Linda's ghost** (Law 41). Honor her once, sincerely, then build your own record.
- **Spend the first month listening and learning**, not changing (Laws 38, 19, 4).
- **Let the data and the staff make the case for change**, and present it as carrying forward what Linda valued (Laws 45, 43).
- **Win early with one small, visible fix** (Law 9), then **pilot the full overhaul and commit to it** (Laws 29, 28).
- **Give your team the credit** and be openly human (Law 46). That's how a successor turns into a leader in their own right.

Most teams stop saying "that's not how Linda did it" somewhere around month four to six. That tends to happen once they've seen you protect what mattered to them and fix what was frustrating them.
