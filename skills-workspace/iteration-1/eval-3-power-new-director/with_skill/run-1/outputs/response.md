# Replacing a Beloved Director: The 48 Laws for Your First 90 Days

Robert Greene wrote a law for almost exactly your situation: **Law 41, Avoid Stepping into a Great Man's Shoes.** His argument is that a successor to someone admired has to do twice as much to escape the comparison. Copying the predecessor guarantees you lose the comparison, and attacking the predecessor turns you into the villain. The way out is to **change the game so the comparison stops applying.**

Your intake overhaul can be that change, as long as you run it so it reads as *your* project and not as *undoing Linda*.

---

## First, map the court

Before picking tactics, it helps to see who holds what kind of power in your situation:

| Player | What they have | What they want |
|---|---|---|
| **Linda (absent)** | Moral authority, twenty years of goodwill, a "Linda way" that staff treat as the default | Nothing now, but her memory is the standard you'll be measured against |
| **Long-tenured staff** | Informal power, institutional knowledge, the ability to slow you down quietly | Respect, stability, and reassurance that their way of working still counts |
| **Your boss / leadership** | Formal authority over you | Results, and no staff revolt |
| **Clients** | The reason intake matters | Faster, clearer and less frustrating intake |
| **You** | The title, but not yet the trust | Credibility, and an intake process that works |

The diagnosis: you have **positional power but not relational power yet.** "That's not how Linda did it" is the staff using the one lever they hold, which is comparison to someone they trust. Most of the time it isn't hostility. It's grief, anxiety and loyalty dressed up as process talk. Treat it that way.

## The laws that apply most (and how to use them)

### 1. Law 41: Avoid Stepping into a Great Man's Shoes *(your core law)*
- **Greene's story:** Alexander the Great didn't try to be his father Philip. He went somewhere Philip never went, and that's why he outgrew him.
- **Your move:** Don't compete with Linda on Linda's ground, which is relationships and history. Build your identity on a new axis that is clearly yours: **a client experience that works.** Say it plainly: "Linda built a team that cares about clients. My job is to give that team a process that matches how much you care."
- **Reversal:** Greene says to use the predecessor's legacy wherever it still has value. Some of what Linda did probably *did* work. Keep it on purpose and say out loud that you're keeping it. That buys you a lot of credibility.

### 2. Law 45: Preach the Need for Change, but Never Reform Too Much at Once *(your biggest risk)*
- **Greene's story:** Thomas Cromwell pushed sweeping reforms through quickly and ended up executed. Leaders who survive dress new changes in familiar forms.
- **Your move:** Make the case that intake has to change, but carry out the change in **stages**, and present each stage as a continuation of the team's values. Keep familiar names, forms and rituals wherever they don't cause harm. Change the parts that are actually broken first.
- **Reversal:** In a real crisis, sweeping change can be welcome. If intake is creating compliance, safety or client-loss problems, you have more room to move quickly. Name the crisis explicitly so the speed makes sense to people.

### 3. Law 9: Win Through Your Actions, Never Through Argument
- **Greene's story:** Michelangelo "fixed" an official's complaint about a statue's nose by pretending to chisel and letting marble dust fall. The official was happy and the statue was unchanged.
- **Your move:** Don't debate "the Linda way" against "the new way" in staff meetings. You'll win the argument and lose the room. **Pilot one fix, measure it, and let the numbers and the client feedback make the case.** "Let's try it for three weeks and look at the data together" beats any speech.
- **Reversal:** Real discussion among peers is healthy. Arguing isn't banned, it's just not your main tool.

### 4. Law 43: Work on the Hearts and Minds of Others
- **Greene's story:** Marie Antoinette ignored how her people felt, and it cost her everything.
- **Your move:** Staff will accept a new intake process when *they* want it, not when you order it. Find what frustrates them about the current mess, such as duplicate data entry, clients falling through the cracks, or chasing missing paperwork, and present the overhaul as relief from that. Change that comes from authority alone gets slow-walked.
- **Note:** Understanding what people care about is not the same as exploiting it. Be sincere.

### 5. Law 18: Do Not Build Fortresses to Protect Yourself (Isolation Is Dangerous)
- **Your move:** The instinct when you're being compared unfavorably is to retreat to your office and lead by email. Don't. Sit at the intake desk, shadow a full client intake from start to finish, and eat lunch with the team. You'll learn where the process actually breaks, which is often not where the documentation says, and staff will see that you respect the work.
- **Reversal:** Take some solitude to think and plan. Just don't let it become hiding.

### 6. Law 19: Know Who You're Dealing With (Do Not Offend the Wrong Person)
- **Your move:** In your first 30 days, work out who the informal leaders are. Usually it's one or two long-tenured people whose opinion the others follow. Greene warns especially about "the serpent with a long memory" and the quiet person everyone underestimates. Bring those people *in* early. Ask for their help in diagnosing intake. A respected veteran who co-owns the redesign is worth more than any memo you could send.

### 7. Law 4: Always Say Less Than Necessary *(in the early weeks)*
- **Your move:** Don't announce a grand overhaul in week one. Listen far more than you talk. When someone says "that's not how Linda did it," don't defend yourself. Ask a question instead (scripts below). Every early promise you make is one you can be held to.
- **Reversal:** Too much silence looks like you're hiding an agenda, and in a transition people already fear a hidden one. Tell people *how* you'll decide ("I'm spending the first month learning, then we'll pick what to fix together") even before you tell them *what* you'll decide.

### 8. Law 46: Never Appear Too Perfect
- **Your move:** Linda's memory gets more flawless every week. You can't out-perfect a ghost. Admit what you don't know yet, ask staff to teach you, and own your mistakes openly. A little humility makes you easier to root for and harder to resent.

## Laws to use carefully, or deliberately not use

- **Law 1 (Never Outshine the Master), reinterpreted.** Linda isn't your boss, but in the staff's eyes she is still the "master." Running her down, even subtly ("well, *before*, things were chaotic..."), makes staff defend her and resent you. Speak well of her, sincerely and specifically.
- **Law 3 (Conceal Your Intentions).** I'd mostly drop this one here. In a change effort, secrecy breeds rumor, and rumor breeds resistance. Be open about the goal of better intake. Be careful only about the timing and order of the specifics.
- **Law 42 (Strike the Shepherd).** If one person is actively organizing resistance, as opposed to just grieving out loud, deal with it **privately, directly and fairly**: clear expectations, a real chance to engage, and documentation if it continues. Never humiliate them in public. In a team that loved Linda, you'd create a martyr, which is the law's own reversal.
- **Laws 15 and 17 (Crush Your Enemy, Cultivate Unpredictability).** Don't use them. Your staff aren't enemies, and keeping people unsettled would wreck the trust you need to rebuild.
- **Law 26 (Keep Your Hands Clean).** Use only the defensive version: **own your decisions.** If part of the new process fails, don't blame the staff member who implemented it. Taking responsibility openly earns more respect here than any scapegoat would.

## Your 90-day plan

### Days 1–30: Listen, map, honor *(Laws 4, 18, 19, 41)*
- **Hold one-on-ones with everyone** (20–30 minutes each). Ask the same three questions: *What did Linda do that we must keep? What about intake frustrates you most? If you ran this, what's the first thing you'd fix?*
- **Shadow intake from end to end.** Follow at least five real clients from first contact to fully onboarded. Time each step and count the handoffs, re-entries and dropped balls.
- **Collect baseline numbers** quietly: time from first contact to completed intake, rate of incomplete files, client drop-off, error or rework rate, and staff hours per intake. These become your evidence for Law 9.
- **Honor Linda in public, once and well.** Name specific things she built that the team is proud of. That separates "respecting Linda" from "freezing everything Linda did."
- **Identify two or three informal leaders** and ask them, privately, to help you diagnose intake.
- **Don't announce an overhaul yet.** Tell people what's happening: "I'm spending this month learning how intake really works before I change anything."

### Days 31–60: Diagnose together, pilot one fix *(Laws 43, 45, 9)*
- **Share what you found** in a team meeting, framed around the team's own words from the one-on-ones ("Six of you told me the duplicate data entry is killing you").
- **Form a small intake working group** with the informal leaders, including at least one person who's skeptical. Their names on the redesign matter more than yours.
- **Pick one pain point to fix first.** Choose something visible, low-risk and frustrating to staff, such as a single intake form instead of three, a shared tracker so no client gets lost, or a clear handoff checklist.
- **Run it as a time-limited pilot** ("three weeks, then we decide together") and measure it against your baseline.
- **Keep what works from the Linda era** and say so explicitly: "We're keeping Linda's welcome call. Clients love it."

### Days 61–90: Show results, expand, lock in *(Laws 9, 41, 47, 35)*
- **Present the pilot results** using the numbers *and* the staff's own experience, with the working group presenting alongside you.
- **Roll out the next phase** of the intake redesign in stages, with the working group as co-owners. Write the new process down so it no longer lives in anyone's head, Linda's or yours.
- **Give credit generously** to the people who built it. Greene's Law 7 says to take the credit, but in your position, sharing it is how you earn lasting authority.
- **Stop at the mark (Law 47).** Don't follow a successful pilot by overhauling scheduling, billing and job roles all at once. Lock in the intake win, let it settle, and then pick the next target.
- **Brief your boss** with before-and-after metrics. That's your foundation for whatever you change next.

## Scripts for "That's not how Linda did it"

Don't argue (Law 9) and don't get defensive (master your emotions). Get curious:

- *"Tell me more about how Linda handled it. What was the part that worked?"* Often there's a real insight behind the comment. Find it and keep it.
- *"That's helpful. What problem was her approach solving? Let's make sure the new version still solves it."*
- *"You're right, it's different. Let's try it for three weeks and look at the numbers together. If it's worse, we'll change it."* This lowers the stakes and moves the fight from opinion to evidence.
- If it becomes a pattern with one person, raise it privately: *"I've noticed the Linda comparison comes up a lot. I respect how much she meant to you. What would help you feel better about where we're going?"*

The comment will fade on its own once staff associate *you* with something good, like the intake process that stopped losing clients' paperwork. That's Law 41 working.

---

## Signs it's working by day 90
- Staff say "the new intake" instead of "your intake" or "not Linda's way."
- At least one veteran staff member defends the new process to someone else.
- Your baseline metrics have measurably improved on at least one dimension.
- People bring you problems early instead of working around you.

---

**A caveat:** *The 48 Laws of Power* is built on historical anecdotes and Greene's interpretation of them, not management research, and Greene is deliberately amoral. The laws above are the ones that hold up ethically in a workplace you want to lead for years. They also line up closely with standard change-management and new-leader advice: listen first, find early wins, co-create the change, and respect the culture you inherit. You don't need to be ruthless to win this. You need to be patient, visibly competent, and generous with credit.
