**Short answer:** Your aunt shouldn't stop her blood pressure pills on her own, and *The Ministry of Healing* doesn't really ask her to. Ellen White was very critical of the "drugs" of her time. But she also told people to use sensible remedies, to work with qualified physicians, and that using real treatment shows no lack of faith. Her natural remedies are a strong addition to blood pressure medicine. They are not a reason to quit it suddenly without her doctor.

## What the book says

**1. Her eight "true remedies."** The best-known passage is in the chapter "The Use of Remedies":

> "Pure air, sunlight, abstemiousness, rest, exercise, proper diet, the use of water, trust in divine power—these are the true remedies."

She believed most illness comes from breaking the laws of health, and that the most lasting cure is to change the habits that caused it. Many Adventists know this list as NEWSTART.

**2. Her warnings about drugs.** In the same chapter she warns against relying on "drugs" and "poisonous preparations." She says they often only hide symptoms or change the form of the disease, and that people take them to escape the trouble of changing their habits.

The historical setting matters here. She wrote this in 1905, when doctors routinely gave mercury compounds (calomel), arsenic, strychnine, opium and alcohol-based tonics. Many of these really were poisonous and did more harm than good. Her criticism was aimed at that kind of medicine. Modern blood pressure drugs had not been invented yet. Today's medicines are tested in large clinical trials and have been shown to prevent strokes, heart attacks, kidney failure and death.

**3. She did not forbid treatment.** In the same section of the book, in the chapter on praying for the sick, she writes that it is "no denial of faith" to use remedies God has provided. She says people who pray for healing should not neglect "remedial agencies." Elsewhere in her writings (*Selected Messages*, Book 2) she makes clear she was not setting a rigid rule against every remedy. She also says a person should not be left to suffer when something can help. She actively supported trained physicians and medical schools. That work led to Loma Linda University and the Adventist hospital systems.

**4. How her own church applies it.** Adventist doctors and hospitals prescribe blood pressure medicine every day, alongside lifestyle medicine. The Adventist Health Studies are a good example of her principles at work. Adventists who eat plant-based diets and keep healthy habits have lower blood pressure and live longer. Even so, Adventist doctors still treat high blood pressure with medicine when it's needed. The church's view is "both/and," not "either/or."

## Why stopping suddenly is dangerous

- **Blood pressure can come back quickly, sometimes higher than before.** Some drugs cause a dangerous rebound if stopped abruptly, especially **clonidine** and **beta-blockers** like metoprolol or atenolol. That can bring on a stroke, heart attack or very high blood pressure.
- **High blood pressure usually has no symptoms.** She may feel fine while damage is happening. "I feel okay" doesn't mean her pressure is okay.
- **Lifestyle changes take time.** Diet, exercise, weight loss and less salt can lower blood pressure a lot, often by 5–11 points or more. That usually takes weeks to months, and the effect differs from person to person.

## A path that respects both her faith and her health

1. **Keep taking the pills as prescribed for now.**
2. **Tell her doctor she wants to try a lifestyle approach.** Many doctors welcome this. Ask whether a supervised plan to cut back the dose is possible over time.
3. **Put Ellen White's remedies into practice in earnest.** Eat a mostly plant-based, low-salt diet (the DASH diet fits well), walk daily, sleep enough, drink water, avoid alcohol and tobacco, limit caffeine, manage stress, and pray. The book supports all of this, and so does modern research.
4. **Check blood pressure at home and keep a log.** Bring it to appointments.
5. **Let the doctor lower or stop the medicine only once the numbers show it's safe.** This happens for some people. Done this way, natural remedies get their chance without risking a stroke.

You might also put it to her this way. The book itself treats caring for the body as a duty to God. Taking a medicine that protects her from a stroke, while she builds the healthy habits Ellen White taught, fits that teaching well. If she would like a church source, her pastor or an Adventist doctor can confirm this. They will very likely say the same thing.

*This is general information, not medical advice. Her doctor or pharmacist should guide any change to her medicines.*
