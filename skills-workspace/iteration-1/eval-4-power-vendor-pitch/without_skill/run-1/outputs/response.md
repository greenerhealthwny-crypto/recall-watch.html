## Short answer: you're being sold to with some classic pressure tactics. That doesn't mean the product is bad, but it does mean you shouldn't sign this week.

Here's what each part of the pitch is doing:

| What the rep is doing | What it's for |
|---|---|
| **"Sign this week"** | Fake urgency. If the deal really goes away, it was never about your clinic. If it doesn't, the deadline was invented. Either way, the rush keeps you from comparing vendors or reading the contract closely. |
| **Two options, both 3-year terms** | A false choice. You're being led to pick *which* 3-year deal, not *whether* to sign one. Most vendors also sell month-to-month, 1-year, or pilot options. Ask for them. |
| **"Free" setup and training** | The cost is built into the 3-year price. Setup is cheap for the vendor compared with locking you in for 36 months. It's a sweetener for the long term, not a gift. |
| **"You're so smart for seeing the value"** | Flattery. It makes you feel like a smart buyer and makes backing out or pushing back feel awkward. When someone praises your judgment before you've made a decision, notice it. |

Any one of these is normal sales behavior. All four together means the rep is working hard to close fast, and you should slow down.

## What to do

**1. Take the deadline away.** Tell the rep something like: *"We don't make multi-year commitments on a one-week timeline. If the offer can't wait two to three weeks for our review, we'll pass."* Good vendors will wait. If the "deal" turns out to be available next month, you've learned something about how they operate.

**2. Ask for the options they didn't show you.**
- Pricing for month-to-month and 1-year terms
- A paid or free pilot (30–90 days) at one site or with a few users
- The cost of setup and training if you bought them on their own. That tells you what "free" is actually worth.

**3. Work out the real 3-year cost.** Get it in writing: license or subscription fees, per-user or per-provider charges, annual price increases (look for escalator clauses, often 3–10% a year), support tiers, add-on modules, integrations, data storage, and any fees for extra training or customization.

**4. Read the contract for lock-in traps.** In clinic software, look especially for:
- **Auto-renewal**: many contracts renew for another full term unless you cancel 60–120 days ahead
- **Early termination**: what you owe if you leave in year 1 (often the remaining balance)
- **Data exit**: can you export all your patient and practice data in a usable, standard format, and is there a fee or a time limit?
- **Performance terms**: uptime guarantees, support response times, and what you get if they miss them
- **Price protection**: a cap on increases during the term and at renewal
- **HIPAA**: if the software touches patient information, they must sign a Business Associate Agreement (BAA). If they're vague about this, walk away.

**5. Get at least two competing quotes.** Even if you like this product, a competitor's quote is your best leverage. It's also the only real way to know whether this is "good value."

**6. Check references yourself.** Ask the vendor for 2–3 clinics your size that have used the product for over a year, and look for others through your professional network or online reviews. Ask them: *"What surprised you after signing? How is support? Would you sign again?"*

**7. Negotiate if you still want it.** Once you've taken back control of the timeline, you can ask for:
- A shorter term, or a 3-year term with an **opt-out after year 1** (or after a 90-day pilot) for poor performance
- Free setup and training **plus** a 1-year term
- Price locked for the full term and capped at renewal
- Free data export when you leave
- Extra training hours or a dedicated onboarding contact

**8. Have someone else review it.** Before you sign a multi-year commitment, have a healthcare attorney, your practice manager, or a trusted peer read the contract. An hour of review costs far less than three years stuck with the wrong system.

## Bottom line

You're not being scammed, but you are being *managed*. The urgency, the narrow choices, and the flattery are all meant to make you decide before you've done your homework. Slow down, ask for the options they left out, get the full costs and exit terms in writing, and compare at least one competitor. If the product really is a good fit, it will still be a good fit in three weeks, and you'll probably get a better deal.
