# Og Mandino's Ten Scrolls vs. Modern Habit Science

*The Greatest Salesman in the World* (1968) is an inspirational parable, not a research book. Still, much of its advice lines up with what psychologists later found about habits, goals and emotion. Some of it is unusual or goes beyond the evidence.

Notes on sources: points about the book come from the text. Points about research are general summaries of well-known findings *(std)*, not a full literature review. Treat effect sizes and details as "roughly," and check the original studies if you need precision.

---

## 1. A quick recap: what Mandino prescribes

- **Ten scrolls**, each with one principle: habits (I), love (II), persistence (III), self-worth (IV), living each day as your last (V), mastering emotions (VI), laughter (VII), goals at a "hundredfold" (VIII), acting now (IX), and a prayer for guidance (X).
- **The method (Scroll I):** read **one scroll for 30 days**. Read it **silently on waking**, **silently after the midday meal**, and **aloud before bed**. Never skip a day. Then move to the next scroll: about **ten months** in all.
- **The theory:** repetition sinks the words into the "other mind," the part that never sleeps. An act repeated becomes easy, then a pleasure, then a habit. "Only a habit can subdue another habit."

---

## 2. Where Mandino and habit science agree

| Mandino's idea | What research broadly says *(std)* | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Habits decide outcomes** (Scroll I: the only difference between those who fail and succeed is their habits) | A large share of daily behavior is repeated in the same contexts and runs with little thought. Self-control often works best by building routines, not by willpower in the moment. | Strong agreement |
| **Repetition makes behavior automatic** ("easy, then a pleasure, then a habit") | Habits form through repetition of a behavior in a stable context, until the context triggers it. Automaticity rises with practice, then levels off. | Agreement on the mechanism of repetition |
| **Fixed cues: waking, after lunch, before bed** | Linking a new behavior to an existing routine ("after X, I will Y") is a core tactic. Implementation intentions (Gollwitzer) and habit stacking build on this. Mandino ties his readings to three anchors you already have every day. | Strong agreement; this is the best-supported part of the method |
| **Replace a habit rather than just stop it** ("only a habit can subdue another habit") | Habit-change work favors substituting a new response to the same cue over pure suppression. | Agreement |
| **Counter-actions for moods** (Scroll VI: "If I feel depressed I will sing… If I feel fear I will plunge ahead") | Close to *if-then plans*, and to "opposite action" in DBT and to behavioral activation for low mood: act against the urge, and feelings often follow. | Strong agreement in spirit |
| **Moods are cyclical; read the buyer's mood** (VI) | Emotion research agrees moods fluctuate and pass. Recognizing others' states is part of emotional intelligence. | Broad agreement |
| **"This too shall pass" and laughter** (VII) | Like *cognitive reappraisal* and self-distancing: reframing events and seeing them in perspective lowers distress. Humor as a coping style is linked to better well-being, especially self-enhancing (not self-defeating) humor. | Agreement |
| **Act now; start immediately** (IX) | Procrastination research supports reducing the gap between intention and action. Starting a task lowers resistance; "two-minute" start rules and implementation intentions help. | Agreement |
| **Set goals at several horizons; compete with your own past self** (VIII) | Goal-setting theory (Locke & Latham) finds specific, challenging goals beat "do your best." Comparing to your own past performance (mastery orientation) tends to be healthier than comparing to others. | Agreement, with a caveat on the size of the goals (see below) |
| **Persistence and the law of averages** (III) | Grit and persistence matter in outcome-heavy fields like sales. Framing rejection as expected cuts its emotional cost. | Partial agreement (see the caveat below) |
| **Love, praise and no gossip** (II) | Warmth, genuine appreciation and trust build relationships; that's backed by work on rapport and prosocial behavior. Silently saying "I love you" works like a loving-kindness intention. | Agreement in spirit |
| **Self-worth based on uniqueness** (IV) | Self-affirmation research (Steele; Cohen & Sherman) finds that affirming your core values can buffer threat and defensiveness. | Partial agreement |

## 3. Where Mandino's method is unusual

### a) The habit he builds is *reading*, not the target behavior

This is the most distinctive feature. In most habit science, you repeat the **behavior you want** (make the call, go to the gym) in a stable context. Mandino's daily habit is **reading a text about the behavior** three times a day. He bets that the words, sunk into the subconscious, will then drive action.

- The closest modern relatives are **affirmations**, **mental rehearsal** and **self-talk**. They have some support as *supports* for action, but much less than direct practice.
- Several scrolls do include behaviors (Scroll III's "one more attempt," Scroll IX's hourly "I will act now," Scroll II's silent greeting). Getting results probably depends on *doing* those, not just reading.

### b) The "other mind" theory

Mandino explains the method through a "mysterious" subconscious that never sleeps and absorbs what you read before bed. Modern research describes habits differently: as **learned links between context and response**, stored in procedural memory, built by doing. There is evidence that sleep consolidates memory, but not that reading a text aloud at night reprograms behavior.

### c) "30 days" is a guess, and probably too short for full automaticity

The one-scroll-per-month rhythm is neat, but research on habit formation (notably Lally and colleagues, 2009) found automaticity took **a median of about 66 days**, with a very wide range (roughly 18 to over 250 days) depending on the person and behavior. Thirty days may be enough to start a routine, not to finish building one.

### d) "Never skip a day"

Mandino says a missed day "cannot be retrieved." In the same Lally study, **missing a single day did not materially slow habit formation**. The bigger risk is the "what-the-hell" effect: after one slip, people quit altogether. A modern version would say: never miss twice.

### e) Sequential, one principle at a time, for ten months

Most programs work on several behaviors at once or focus on one keystone habit. Mandino's **strict sequence** (finish Scroll I before II, and so on), with each principle getting a full month of attention, is rare. It has an upside: **focus** and low load. The downside: a skill you need now (for example, persistence) waits until month three.

### f) Reading aloud before bed

Reading aloud has some research support for *memory* (the "production effect": saying words improves recall compared with reading silently). Using it as a nightly ritual for character change is Mandino's own design, and makes it feel more like a recitation or prayer than a habit-science protocol.

### g) "A hundredfold" goals

Goal-setting theory backs hard goals, but **only while they stay believable and come with a plan**. Mandino says multiply your best past result by **100** and aim at the moon to hit an eagle. Modern research on extreme "stretch goals" suggests they can **backfire** (discouragement, cutting corners) unless you already have resources and recent momentum. Read his "hundredfold" as a metaphor, and set the working target at a level that is hard but reachable.

### h) Positive thinking without planning for obstacles

Mandino asks you to never consider defeat and to remove words like *impossible* and *hopeless*. Gabriele Oettingen's research found that **pure positive fantasizing can lower effort**, while **mental contrasting** (picture the goal, then the obstacle, then plan for it; known as WOOP) improves follow-through. Mandino's emotion "counter-actions" in Scroll VI are close to this, but his persistence scroll mostly skips the "plan for the obstacle" step.

### i) Affirmations can backfire for some people

Saying "I am nature's greatest miracle" (Scroll IV) may help someone with a stable sense of self. One well-known study (Wood, Perunovic & Lee, 2009) found that strong positive self-statements could make people with **low self-esteem feel worse**, because the words clash with what they believe. Values-based affirmation ("what matters to me and why") tends to work better than global praise of yourself.

### j) The law of averages taken literally

"Each nay brings me closer to yea" is good motivation, but statistically it's the **gambler's fallacy** if your attempts are independent and unchanged: the 41st call is not more likely to succeed because 40 failed. It holds true in practice only because more attempts give more total chances, and because each rejection can teach you something that raises your success rate.

### k) Prayer and the spiritual frame

Scroll X asks the reader to pray for **guidance only**, never for gold or victories, and to ask for "ability equal to your opportunities." Habit science has nothing quite like this. The closest secular parallels are **daily intention-setting**, **humility**, and **reflection practices**. Research on religious coping and on gratitude and reflection suggests these can support well-being, but the book's frame is explicitly faith-based. Readers can keep it or adapt it to their own beliefs.

### l) Habits as something to become "a slave" to

Mandino's language is total: "I will form good habits and become their slave." Modern approaches tend to stress **flexible, self-compassionate** habit-building. Rigid all-or-nothing rules link to higher dropout after slips.

## 4. Summary

| | Mandino | Modern habit science *(std)* |
|---|---|---|
| What you repeat | Reading the principle (3×/day) | The behavior itself |
| Cues | Waking, after lunch, bedtime | Stable context cues and if-then plans |
| How long | 30 days per scroll | Varies; median ~2 months for automaticity |
| Missed days | Never; a day "cannot be retrieved" | One miss matters little; avoid two in a row |
| Mechanism | Words sink into the "other mind" | Context-response associations built by doing |
| Goals | A "hundredfold" your best | Specific, hard, *believable*, with a plan |
| Setbacks | Never consider defeat | Plan for obstacles (mental contrasting) |
| Emotions | Counter-act each mood with an action | Opposite action, reappraisal, behavioral activation |
| Spirit | Prayer for guidance | Intention-setting, reflection (secular) |

**Bottom line:** Mandino got the **structure** right: daily repetition tied to fixed cues, replacing bad habits with good ones, acting against bad moods, acting now, and competing with your own past self. Where he's unusual is the **content of the habit** (reading and reciting rather than doing), the **subconscious theory**, the **rigid 30-day, no-misses rule**, and the **extreme goals and pure positivity**.

### If you want to use the scrolls in a science-informed way

1. Keep the three daily readings tied to waking, lunch and bedtime. That's the strong part.
2. Pair each scroll with **one concrete behavior** you do daily (for Scroll III: one more call after you want to stop).
3. Expect a scroll's behavior to take **longer than 30 days** to become automatic. Keep doing it after you move on.
4. If you miss a day, **just don't miss two**.
5. Make Scroll VIII's goals **hard but believable**, and add an "obstacle and plan" step.
6. If big self-affirmations feel false, use **value-based** statements instead.
