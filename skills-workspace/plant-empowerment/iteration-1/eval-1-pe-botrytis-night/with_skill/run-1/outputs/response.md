# Botrytis in the heads and wet lower fruits: what to change

## Short answer

Your night strategy is the classic "active climate": a high minimum pipe temperature plus a screen gap to dump humidity. It is probably *causing* the free water that Botrytis needs, not preventing it. Night RH of 90% is not the real problem. Botrytis needs **free water**, and free water only forms where a plant part is **colder than the dew point** of the air around it. Your two symptoms point to two cold spots:

1. **Plant heads lose heat by radiation (heat emission) and get hit by cold air dropping through the 5% gap.** The heads are the part of the crop that "sees" the cold glass and the night sky. Under a gap they also get cold air falling from above the screen. They cool below dew point, so they get wet and weak, and Botrytis follows.
2. **Lower fruits lag behind at sunrise.** Heavy fruits warm up slowly. In the morning the upper crop warms and starts to evaporate, which raises the absolute humidity (AH). The air's dew point then rises above the temperature of the cold fruits, so water condenses on them. Opening the screen in the morning drops cold air and makes this worse.

The Plant Empowerment (GPE) approach turns your setup around:
- **Close the screen fully.**
- **Remove moisture by ventilating above the screen**, not through a gap.
- **Heat only when there is heat demand.**
- **Make air movement with fans, not with pipe heat.**
- **Manage the evening and the sunrise transitions slowly.**

## Why the current settings work against you

**45 °C minimum pipe.** It does create air movement. But it also forces extra evaporation from the crop all night, so you produce *more* moisture. Then you need the screen gap to get rid of that moisture. Heating that is not needed for temperature mainly feeds the humidity problem you are fighting.

**The 5% screen gap.**
- A gap sets up a circulation: cold air drops down near the walls and through the gap, and warm air rises out. Over a long bay this can give temperature differences of up to about 8 °C (the book's figure). Your aspirator box usually does not see the cold spots.
- Even small gaps, under about 10 cm, still cause this circulation.
- Wider gaps no longer save energy.
- Crop under or near the gap loses the screen's protection against heat emission and "sees" the cold roof.
- Clear March nights in the Netherlands are a high-risk time for heat emission. Under a clear sky the roof can lose around 150 W/m². The book's rule of thumb is that a crop loses about **5 W/m² per °C** it is warmer than what it "sees" overhead.

**Moisture leaves by only two routes:** condensation on a cold surface (the roof) and ventilation driven by the AH difference between inside and outside. A **fully closed** screen makes the roof colder. More moisture then condenses on the glass above the screen, and AH above the screen drops toward outdoor levels. Moisture then moves from the crop side through the screen fabric, driven by the AH difference across it. You do not need a gap to get humidity out. You need a moisture-permeable screen, an AH difference across it, and some vent opening **above** the screen if the roof cannot take it all.

## What to change (in this order, step by step)

### 1. Cut unnecessary evaporation first
- **Replace the fixed minimum pipe temperature** with heating driven by real heat demand, or by crop temperature getting close to dew point. Lower it in steps over a series of nights rather than all at once, and watch the effects described in the "What to measure" section below.
- **Use vertical fans for air movement** in place of pipe heat, if you have them or can install them. Vertical fans push warm air down into the heads, even out the vertical temperature profile and carry moisture up toward the screen and roof. Horizontal fans do much less for this problem.
- **Don't leave the crop or floor wet at the end of the day.** Avoid late irrigation and check your last irrigation timing and night drain.

### 2. Close the screen fully, without a moisture gap
- **Go from 95% to fully closed.** In GPE this is the main protection of the plant head against heat emission.
- **Remove humidity by opening the vents a little *above* the closed screen.** Use small openings on **both leeside and windside**, not one side wide open. Control the vents on the AH difference, not on RH alone.
- **If you have two screens:** keep both closed and ventilate above them. If you still must create a gap, gap the lower screen first. Don't open a gap in the single layer directly above the heads. On very cold nights the book prefers an airtight lower screen with any gap in the upper one, so that moisture does not condense on the colder upper screen.
- **Check your seals** at the gutters, trusses and side walls. Cold air leaking in at the edges creates the same cold drop as a gap. Vertical screens (apex seals) above the screen help on long bays.

This departs from common Dutch practice. Many growers still believe the gap is what "makes room" for humidity. GPE argues that the gap's cold air drop and the heat emission it allows do more harm than good. The exception is when it is only a few degrees colder outside. Then a screen closed with a gap is still better than an open one.

### 3. Fix the evening transition
- **Close the energy screen about 1 hour earlier** than your outdoor-temperature rule says, and earlier still after a clear, sunny day.
- **Close it fully while the greenhouse is still about 1 °C above the night set point.** Then let the temperature come down slowly.
- **Avoid a fast temperature drop at dusk.** Cooling at constant AH sends RH straight up and brings the dew point close to crop temperature. Keep inside AH a few g/m³ above outdoor AH so that ventilation can still remove moisture. If RH climbs too far, raising the temperature slightly gives more exhaust capacity than opening a gap.
- **Avoid the late-afternoon combination of warm, active roots and a sudden drop in evaporation.** This drives root pressure and guttation. Guttation means more free water and weaker cells, both of which feed Botrytis.

### 4. Fix the sunrise transition (lower fruits)
- **Raise the greenhouse temperature gradually, well before sunrise**, so heavy fruits warm up together with the air. A quick boost at sunrise does not help the fruits.
- **Keep the screen closed longer in the morning.** Open it only when the air above the screen has been warmed by the sun. A net radiation sensor under the screen can help you time this. Then open in small steps.
- **Start morning irrigation in step with rising radiation**, not before it. Cold, wet slabs at sunrise slow uptake.

### 5. Check the crop balance (March)
In March light is rising quickly. A plant load that is too high, or a 24-hour temperature that does not match the light sum (RTR), gives weak, assimilate-short cells that Botrytis attacks easily. Plot 24-hour mean temperature against the daily light sum and keep each day near your target line. Check plant load against your plan for the expected light.

## What to measure while you make the changes

- **AH at three points:** below the screen, above the screen and outdoors. A flat AH trend below the screen means the moisture balance is in equilibrium, whatever the RH figure. The AH difference across the screen shows whether moisture can pass through it. If AH above the screen is already close to outdoor AH, opening the vents more above the screen won't help much.
- **An aspirator box above the screen.** If you don't have one, this is the most useful addition for this problem.
- **An IR leaf/head temperature sensor** compared with the dew point. This tells you directly whether the heads are condensing. Leaf temperature should stay above dew point. VPD (leaf to air) below about 0.2 kPa means almost no evaporation and a condensation risk.
- **Fruit temperature at sunrise** compared with the dew point, measured on a few lower trusses with a probe or a thermal camera.
- **A pyrgeometer, or at least a log of clear vs cloudy nights.** Botrytis outbreaks often follow clear, starry nights.
- **Multiple or wireless sensors**, especially near walls, gables and spots that used to sit under the gap, to find cold spots your main box misses.

What success looks like: Botrytis pressure falls, there are no droplets on the fruits in the morning, the heads stay warmer than dew point, and gas use goes down. The RH reading at night may stay around 90%, or even rise a little, under a fully closed screen. That is fine as long as the crop stays above dew point and the air keeps moving.

## Caveats

- **Make the changes stepwise.** Start with the screen closing time and the minimum pipe temperature, check your own data for a few nights, then take the next step. Every change also affects the other balances.
- **Screens and greenhouses differ.** This advice assumes a moisture-permeable energy screen. A very airtight screen or a blackout screen needs more ventilation above it, or a screen with more permeability.
- **For Botrytis control products, hygiene (removing infected tissue, clean stem wounds) and residue rules, follow your crop protection adviser.** Climate correction removes the cause but doesn't cure plants that are already infected.
- The figures above (≈8 °C gap effect, 5 W/m²·°C, ≈150 W/m² clear-sky loss, close 1 h earlier / 1 °C above set point) are rules of thumb from *Plant Empowerment: The Basic Principles*. Treat them as starting points, not guarantees.
