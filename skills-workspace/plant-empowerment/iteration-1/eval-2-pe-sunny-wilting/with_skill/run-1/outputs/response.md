# Midday wilting in cucumber: fix the water balance before you take light away

**Short answer:** I wouldn't start with whitewash or the shade screen. What you describe is a plant water-balance problem, and your ventilation strategy is making it worse. Shading treats the symptom, costs you light and photosynthesis, and often starts a negative spiral. Change how you ventilate and how you water first. Use shade only as a last resort, if the crop still wilts after those changes.

This follows the *Growing by Plant Empowerment* (GPE) approach. It differs from common practice, so the reasoning is set out below.

---

## What your symptoms are telling you

| Observation | What it means physically |
|---|---|
| Wilting around solar noon on sunny days | Radiation, and so evaporation demand, peaks. Water uptake and transport can't keep up. |
| **Leaves feel warm**, likely warmer than the air | Stomata are partly closing. A well-watered leaf with open stomata stays *colder* than the air. A leaf that stays above air temperature is short of water. |
| RH drops to ~50% | Large ventilation openings are replacing greenhouse air with drier outside air. |
| Vents go wide open at 24 °C, with CO2 dosing | You're ventilating on temperature alone, which also blows out the CO2 you're paying for. |

### The negative spiral

Under strong sun, a plant evaporates roughly in proportion to the radiation it absorbs, whatever the air humidity. Low RH doesn't help it cool. It only dries the leaf boundary layer, and once water supply can't keep up, the stomata start to close. Then:

1. Stomata pinch, and the leaf heats above air temperature (the warm leaves you feel).
2. Less evaporation makes the greenhouse warmer, so the vents open further.
3. Wider vents make RH fall further and push CO2 out, so you lose the dosed CO2.
4. Lower RH and lower enthalpy mean you need still more ventilation, and stomatal closure deepens.
5. The result is midday wilting, lost photosynthesis, and an ABA-driven stomatal closure that takes **hours** to recover. The afternoon is affected too, not only noon.

As an illustration: with air at ~28 °C and 50% RH and the leaf a degree warmer than the air, leaf-to-air VPD is roughly 2 kPa. GPE treats about **1.5 kPa** as the point where the stomata are closing and real water stress has begun. A healthy range under high light is roughly 0.3–1.5 kPa. Cucumber can evaporate a lot at low VPD as long as the stomata stay open.

### Why shading is the wrong first step

- Whitewash and shade screens reduce water stress by cutting light. They do little for greenhouse temperature, and they reduce photosynthesis and root growth.
- Weaker roots mean the plant hits its water limit at an even *lower* light level next time, which calls for more shading. That is the shading spiral.
- Whitewash is hard to take off on cloudy days, so you lose light in the dull spells too.

---

## What to do instead, in small steps

### 1. Ventilate less, and ventilate differently (the biggest lever)
- **Raise the ventilation temperature and open more gradually.** Wide-open vents at 24 °C is very early for a dosed cucumber crop under strong sun. Let the greenhouse run warmer. The book's example compares 28 °C/75% RH with 25 °C/75% RH: at the warmer, humid setting the crop lost about 10% less water, stomata stayed more open and photosynthesis was faster. Raise the set point a step at a time, for example 1 °C every few days, and watch the crop's response.
- **Steer the vents on humidity, enthalpy and CO2 as well as temperature.** Single-factor temperature control is what drives the spiral. A practical way in is to switch to humidity-based vent control in the **late afternoon** first, when the risk of overheating is lowest, then move the start earlier day by day.
- **Use windside and leeside vents together at small openings** rather than the leeside wide open. This gives a gentler, more even air exchange, with less dry air falling straight onto the heads. The old idea that windside ventilation harms the crop is largely outdated.
- **Aim to keep RH higher as PAR rises**, mainly by restricting ventilation. Higher humidity under high light lets the stomata stay open with a slightly cooler leaf, which is better for CO2 uptake.

### 2. Make the CO2 work for you
- With less ventilation the same CO2 dose keeps a higher concentration, so you get temperature, CO2 and humidity working for photosynthesis together. Higher CO2 also raises the optimum temperature, which is another reason to accept a warmer midday.
- Dose into the **upper, light-intercepting layer**, and check CO2 at crop height around noon. If it collapses when the vents open, you're paying for CO2 that goes straight out.

### 3. Make sure water supply runs ahead of demand
- **Start irrigating early and anticipate the sun.** From sunrise, raise slab water content ahead of evaporation with frequent short drip cycles. On sunny days a somewhat lower EC eases uptake. Even a short stress costs hours of photosynthesis.
- **Irrigate on accumulated radiation** (pyranometer or PAR sum), not on fixed times.
- **Check the roots and root temperature.** Cold slabs in the morning or few active white root tips limit uptake. Remember that slab weight and drain only show *supply*. The plant can still be stressed if root capacity or stem transport is the bottleneck.
- **Keep fruit load and harvests regular.** Cucumber fruits are part of the plant's water buffer, and a heavy one-off harvest removes it.

### 4. Get air moving around the leaves
- Stomatal regulation only works with air movement. **Vertical fans** are effective under more-closed vents: they cool the heads and bring the vertical temperature and CO2 profile into line. Horizontal fans do much less.

### 5. If you have misting or fogging, use it
- Misting covers the gap between energy input and the roots' maximum uptake, and it lets you ventilate less. Control it on radiation, PAR and leaf temperature, not on RH alone. RH-only control tends to over-mist at the wrong moments.

### 6. Shade only as a last resort, and the gentlest kind
If the crop still wilts after the steps above:
- Prefer a **movable screen** used only briefly around peak radiation, so you keep the light the rest of the day. Prefer a **diffuse** screen or coating if possible: diffuse light reaches deeper into the canopy and causes less water stress in the head.
- Avoid heavy whitewash for the whole summer. It takes light on every day, including the ones where the crop could have used it.

---

## What to measure so you can see if it's working

- **Infrared leaf temperature vs air temperature.** This is the key signal. Leaves colder than air means the stomata are open and you're on track. Leaves staying above air means water stress.
- **VPD calculated from leaf temperature.** Aim to keep it below about 1.5 kPa through midday.
- **CO2 at crop height through the day.** It should hold up much better at noon with less ventilation.
- **Absolute humidity (AH) trend and inside vs outside AH**, rather than RH alone. This shows whether you can still remove moisture when needed.
- **Irrigation vs radiation sum, slab water content, drain and root temperature**, especially in the morning.
- **Plant response:** less midday wilting, faster afternoon recovery, and head/tip vigour.

## Things to keep an eye on

- **Temperature balance (RTR):** a warmer day raises the 24-hour average. That's fine on bright days, because the plant makes more assimilates to burn. Keep the 24-hour average in line with the daily light sum, and lower the night temperature a little if needed. Too warm without enough light makes the plant weak and generative.
- **Evening humidity:** when you ventilate less by day, cool down slowly in the evening so inside AH stays a few g/m³ above outside. This avoids condensation and guttation risk (for example Mycosphaerella) late in the day.
- **Plant load:** a lower, steady fruit load makes high-light days easier for the crop to handle.

These are rules of thumb from GPE. Greenhouses, cultivars and climates differ, so make one change at a time, check it against your own leaf temperature, CO2 and humidity data, and adjust from there.
