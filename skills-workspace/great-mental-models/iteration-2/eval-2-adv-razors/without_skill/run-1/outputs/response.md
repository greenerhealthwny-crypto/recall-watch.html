# Occam's Razor vs. Hanlon's Razor (and First Principles Pricing)

## The Two Razors in One Line Each

- **Occam's Razor:** When several explanations fit the facts, prefer the simplest one, the one that needs the fewest assumptions. It's about **likelihood**: which story is most probable?
- **Hanlon's Razor:** "Never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity," or more kindly, by carelessness, busyness, or ordinary human stuff. It's about **intent**: don't assume someone meant to hurt you.

**How they differ:**

| | Occam's Razor | Hanlon's Razor |
|---|---|---|
| Question it answers | *What most likely happened?* | *Did they mean it?* |
| Domain | Any explanation (events, systems, causes) | People's behavior and motives |
| Main trap it guards against | Overly complex, conspiracy-style stories | Taking things personally; assuming bad intent |
| Main payoff | Better diagnosis | Better relationships and less wasted emotion |

They often point the same way, since "they were careless" is usually simpler than "they plotted." But they're different tools. Occam's cuts away extra *assumptions*. Hanlon's cuts away assumed *malice*.

## The Scenario: A Marketplace Buyer Ghosts You

You list a dresser on Facebook Marketplace for $250. A buyer messages:

> "Is this still available? I can pick up Saturday at 2."

You say yes, hold it for them, and turn down two other people. Saturday at 2 comes. No one shows. You message at 2:20 and 3:00. Nothing. "Seen" shows up, then silence.

### Stories your brain might come up with

1. They were a scammer gathering info or testing if your account was real.
2. A competitor flipper wanted to take your listing off the market.
3. They saw your profile, didn't like something about you, and blew you off on purpose.
4. They found a cheaper dresser, or changed their mind, and felt awkward saying so.
5. Something came up (kids, work, car trouble) and they forgot or felt bad.
6. They message 20 sellers at once and "is this available" meant very little to them.

### Apply Occam's Razor

Ask: *which explanation needs the fewest extra assumptions?*

- Stories 1–3 each need several things to be true: a motive, a plan, and a reason to target *you*.
- Stories 4–6 need almost nothing beyond normal behavior. Low-commitment messages on a free platform, with no deposit and no penalty for not showing, often lead to no-shows.

**Occam's verdict:** The likely answer is boring. The buyer was low-commitment, changed plans, and found it easier to disappear than to explain. It's a *system* problem (free, frictionless messaging) more than a person problem.

### Apply Hanlon's Razor

Ask: *am I reading malice into this?*

Story 3, "they blew me off on purpose," is a malice story. Even if a few ghosters are rude, the much more common reasons are avoiding an awkward message, being disorganized, or just not thinking about your Saturday at all.

**Hanlon's verdict:** Don't take it personally. Don't send an angry message, leave a bad review in anger, or let it sour how you treat the next buyer.

### What each razor tells you to *do*

- **Occam's (fix the system):** Since no-shows are a normal, predictable part of the platform, design around them:
  - Ask the buyer to confirm the morning of pickup. No confirmation means the item is still open.
  - Don't turn down other buyers. Say "First person to show up with cash gets it" or keep a backup list.
  - For high-value pieces, ask for a small deposit through a payment app (from people you've messaged with enough to trust), or skip holds altogether.
  - Count on a no-show rate. If 1 in 4 pickups fall through, build that into your time and pricing.
- **Hanlon's (protect your mood and reputation):** Send one calm, friendly follow-up, like "No worries if plans changed. I'll relist it today." Then move on. Stay polite, since the same buyer may come back, and the next buyer gets your best self.

**A caveat:** Both razors are rules of thumb, not laws. If someone asks for your phone number "to verify," sends a fake payment screenshot, or wants you to click a link, that's a pattern that *does* point to a scam. At that point the simplest explanation really is fraud. The razors help you default to the likely answer. They don't tell you to ignore evidence.

## First Principles Thinking on Pricing

**First principles thinking** means breaking a problem down to its basic truths and building up from there, instead of copying what everyone else does ("similar dressers list for $200, so I'll list for $200").

Pricing by copying has two problems. Other sellers might be underpricing (many flippers don't count their time). And it ignores your actual costs and your goals.

### Step 1: Ask what a price actually has to do

A price has to:
1. **Cover all costs**, including the hidden ones
2. **Pay you for your time** at a rate you've chosen on purpose
3. **Match what a buyer will pay**, given the value they see
4. **Sell within a reasonable time**, since sitting inventory has real costs

### Step 2: List every real cost (the floor)

Example: a solid-wood dresser.

| Cost | Amount |
|---|---|
| Purchase price | $60 |
| Supplies (sandpaper, stain or paint, topcoat, hardware) | $45 |
| Gas and pickup | $15 |
| Platform or payment fees (if any) | $0–$15 |
| Share of tools and space (wear, garage, storage) | $10 |
| **Direct cost total** | **~$135** |

### Step 3: Price your time honestly

Count *all* the hours, not just painting:

| Task | Hours |
|---|---|
| Finding and buying | 1.0 |
| Pickup and transport | 1.0 |
| Cleaning and repair | 2.0 |
| Sanding and finishing | 4.0 |
| Photos and listing | 0.5 |
| Messaging, no-shows, and the sale | 1.5 |
| **Total** | **10 hours** |

If you want to earn **$25/hour**, your time is worth **$250**.

**Cost-based floor:** $135 + $250 = **$385**

### Step 4: Find the ceiling (buyer value)

Now look at it from the buyer's side. What are their real alternatives?

- A new particleboard dresser from a big-box store: ~$200–$300, which won't last
- A new solid-wood dresser of similar size: often $700–$1,500+
- Other used, refinished solid-wood dressers in your area: check what has *sold*, not just what's listed

So for a nicely finished solid-wood dresser, buyer value may sit somewhere around **$350–$550**, depending on style, size, condition, and local taste.

### Step 5: Compare floor vs. ceiling

- **If floor < ceiling** (e.g., $385 floor vs. $500 ceiling): price toward the ceiling. Try $475 and leave a little room to negotiate.
- **If floor > ceiling:** this piece doesn't work at your target hourly rate. That's the key insight first principles gives you. Your choices are:
  - Buy cheaper
  - Cut hours (simpler finish, fewer repairs, batch similar jobs)
  - Pick pieces with higher buyer value (solid wood, known brands, mid-century, sets)
  - Accept a lower hourly rate on purpose for this one, knowing why
  - Pass on similar pieces next time

### Step 6: Add the time factor

Unsold inventory isn't free. It ties up cash and space. Set a simple rule, for example:
- List at your target price.
- If there are no serious offers in **10–14 days**, drop 10%.
- Never go below your **direct cost floor** (~$135 here) unless you're clearing space on purpose.

### Step 7: Fold in what the razors taught you

From the ghosting scenario, Occam's Razor said no-shows are normal and predictable. That's a real cost too. If roughly 1 in 4 pickups falls through and each one costs you 30–60 minutes, add that time to your "messaging and sale" hours. First principles means pricing the business as it *actually* runs, not as it runs on a perfect day.

---

## Summary

- **Occam's Razor:** The buyer most likely flaked for an ordinary reason. Fix the *system* (confirmations, no holds, backup buyers).
- **Hanlon's Razor:** They almost certainly didn't mean to hurt you. Don't take it personally. Stay polite and move on.
- **First principles pricing:** Build your price from real costs + the hourly rate you've chosen (the floor), then check it against buyer alternatives (the ceiling). If the floor is above the ceiling, change *the piece or the process*, not just the price tag.
