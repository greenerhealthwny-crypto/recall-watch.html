# Occam's Razor vs Hanlon's Razor: The Ghosting Marketplace Buyer
### Plus first-principles thinking on pricing
*Based on* The Great Mental Models, Vol. 1 *(Shane Parrish and Rhiannon Beaubien, Farnam Street)*

---

## Part 1: The two razors

Both are "razors": rules of thumb that cut away unlikely explanations so you can act on the likely one. They cut along different lines.

| | **Occam's Razor** | **Hanlon's Razor** |
|---|---|---|
| **The rule (from the book)** | Simpler explanations are more likely to be true. Prefer the one with the fewest moving parts. | Don't put down to malice what is more easily explained by stupidity. The likeliest explanation has the least intent. |
| **What it cuts** | **Complexity**: explanations that need many things to all be true at once. | **Intent**: explanations that assume someone *meant* to harm you. |
| **The question it asks** | "How many assumptions does this story need?" | "Does this story need someone to be against me?" |
| **The trap it guards against** | Overthinking; building elaborate theories. | Paranoia; taking things personally (what the book calls "the Devil Fallacy"). |
| **The book's caveat** | Some things really aren't simple. Don't force a simple answer onto a complex reality (the book's medical sidebar makes this point). | Malice does exist. The razor sets your *default*, not your conclusion. |
| **The book's example** | Medicine: the common cause of a symptom is more likely than a rare one. | Road rage: the driver who cut you off was most likely distracted, not out to get you. |

**The short version:** Occam asks *how complicated* your story is. Hanlon asks *how hostile* it is. A story can be simple and malicious ("he's a jerk"), so Occam alone won't save you from taking things personally. A story can be harmless and still complicated, so Hanlon alone won't stop overthinking. You need both.

## Part 2: The scenario

You list a mid-century dresser on Facebook Marketplace for $250. A buyer messages: *"Is this still available? I can pick up Saturday at 2."* You say yes, hold it, turn down two other people, and send your address. Saturday at 2 comes and goes. No message. You send "Still coming?" and get nothing. They've ghosted you.

### Step 1: List the possible explanations
1. They found a cheaper dresser and didn't bother to tell you.
2. Something came up (work, kids, car trouble) and they forgot to message.
3. They changed their mind or couldn't afford it and felt awkward saying so.
4. They're a scammer or troll wasting sellers' time on purpose.
5. A rival seller is fake-booking your items to keep you off the market.
6. They saw your address, are casing your house, and plan to come back.
7. They never saw your last message (notifications off, Messenger glitch).

### Step 2: Apply Occam's razor (cut the complexity)
Count what each story needs to be true:

- **#2, #3, #7 (forgot, changed mind, missed the message):** need one ordinary thing to happen. Very few assumptions.
- **#1 (found a cheaper one):** needs a competing listing plus not bothering to cancel. Still simple and common.
- **#4 (deliberate time-waster):** needs a person who gets something out of wasting your time, with no gain to them. More assumptions.
- **#5 (rival seller plot):** needs a rival who knows you, watches your listings, creates fake accounts, and thinks this is worth the effort. Many moving parts.
- **#6 (casing your house):** needs a criminal who chose Marketplace furniture as the cover story, chose you, and is planning a crime. Many moving parts.

**Occam's verdict:** #1, #2, #3 and #7 are the most likely. #5 and #6 need too many things to line up.

### Step 3: Apply Hanlon's razor (cut the intent)
Now sort by how much hostile intent each story needs:

- **No intent against you:** #2 (forgot), #7 (missed the message).
- **Thoughtless, not hostile:** #1 (cheaper option, didn't cancel), #3 (awkward, avoided the message). This is the "stupidity" end in the book's sense: carelessness, poor manners, avoiding discomfort.
- **Hostile intent:** #4 (time-waster), #5 (rival), #6 (casing).

**Hanlon's verdict:** the likeliest story is carelessness, not malice. Ghosting usually happens because cancelling feels awkward and costs the buyer nothing.

### Step 4: Notice where they differ
- **#4 (deliberate time-waster)** is fairly simple, so Occam alone doesn't rule it out. It's Hanlon that pushes it down, because it needs intent when carelessness explains the same facts.
- **#7 (missed the message)** has no malice, but it's slightly less common than "forgot." Both razors are fine with it.
- **#5 and #6** fail *both* tests: complicated *and* hostile. These are the stories your anxious brain may jump to at 2:15 on a Saturday, and they're the least likely.

### Step 5: Act on the likely story without being naive
The book is clear that razors guide judgment; they don't replace it. You don't assume malice, but you also don't ignore real risk.

- **Emotionally:** let it go. It's very likely not about you. That saves you a bad afternoon and a snippy message.
- **Practically:** design for carelessness, since that's the common cause:
  - Ask for a confirmation message the morning of pickup. No confirmation, no hold.
  - Don't turn down other buyers until the first one confirms. Say "first to show with cash gets it."
  - For high-value items, ask for a small deposit (Venmo, PayPal or similar).
  - Share your exact address only after the day-of confirmation.
- **Safety:** for #6, you don't need to believe it to take cheap precautions. Meet in a public spot or porch pickup for small items; have someone home for big ones. That covers the rare bad case without assuming everyone is a threat.
- **Update if evidence changes:** if the same account books and ghosts you three times, or the person shows up somewhere odd, the evidence has changed. Update your view (the book's "reality is the ultimate update").

## Part 3: First principles thinking on pricing

**Core idea from the book:** separate the basic facts from the assumptions, then rebuild from the parts you can't reduce any further. The book's two tools are **Socratic questioning** and the **Five Whys**.

### The usual approach (reasoning by copying)
"Similar dressers on Marketplace go for $200–$300, so I'll list at $250." That's reasoning by comparison. It's quick and often fine, but it takes on other sellers' assumptions and mistakes. Many of those listings never sell, and you can't see the price they finally sold for.

### Step 1: Strip it to the basic parts
What is a price really made of? Break it into facts you can't reduce further:

1. **Your cost floor:** what you paid + supplies (sandpaper, stain, hardware) + fuel + platform or payment fees.
2. **Your time:** total hours (sourcing, hauling, cleaning, repair, refinishing, photos, listing, messaging, pickup) × the hourly rate you want.
3. **Holding cost:** storage space, capital tied up, and the risk of damage while it sits.
4. **Buyer's value ceiling:** what *this* buyer would otherwise pay to get the same use: a new dresser of similar quality, delivery, the hassle of assembly, plus any premium for style, solid wood or brand (e.g., a mid-century look).
5. **Demand and speed:** how many local buyers want this kind of piece right now, and how fast it sells at each price.

**The basic truth:** your price must sit **between your full cost floor (1–3) and the buyer's value ceiling (4)**. Demand and speed (5) decide where in that range you land.

### Step 2: Socratic questioning (the book's six steps)
Applied to "this dresser is worth $250":

1. **Clarify your thinking:** what does "worth $250" mean? That someone *might* pay it? That it sells within a week? That you earn $25/hour on it?
2. **Challenge assumptions:** "comparable listings show the market price." Do they? Listing prices aren't sale prices. Some listings sit for months.
3. **Look for evidence:** what have *your* similar pieces actually sold for, and how fast? How many messages did you get in the first 24 hours? Lots of quick messages means you're probably too low. None in a week means too high or bad photos.
4. **Consider other views:** how does a buyer see it? To someone who would otherwise buy a $600 new solid-wood dresser plus delivery, $350 may feel like a deal. To a student furnishing a first apartment, $250 is a lot.
5. **Examine consequences:** if you price at $350 and it takes 3 weeks, what does that cost you in storage and in not buying the next piece? If you price at $200 and it sells in an hour, how much did you leave on the table?
6. **Question the original question:** is "what price?" even the right question? Maybe it's "which pieces should I buy at all, given what they'll sell for?" Pricing really starts at the moment you *source*.

### Step 3: Five Whys on a pricing problem
Say your pieces are selling slowly.
- **Why are they selling slowly?** → Few messages per listing.
- **Why?** → Listings don't get many views or clicks.
- **Why?** → The photos look like a garage and the first photo doesn't show the piece clearly.
- **Why?** → I shoot them where I work on them, in poor light.
- **Why?** → I never set up a simple photo spot.

**Root cause:** presentation, not price. Cutting the price would have "fixed" the symptom and cost you money each time. That's the value of the Five Whys: it stops you from treating price as the answer to every problem.

### Step 4: Rebuild a price from the ground up
Worked example (swap in your own numbers):

| Part | Amount |
|---|---|
| Bought dresser | $60 |
| Supplies (stain, sandpaper, new pulls) | $35 |
| Fuel + fees | $15 |
| **Cash cost** | **$110** |
| Time: 8 hours × $20/hour target | $160 |
| **Full cost floor (cash + your time)** | **$270** |
| Buyer's alternative: new solid-wood dresser + delivery | ~$600–$800 |
| Local demand for mid-century style | High (fast replies on past listings) |

**Rebuilt price:** list around **$375**, with a floor of about $300 you won't go below.
- Notice that the copied $250 price would have sold the dresser **below your full cost**. You'd have made $140 over cash cost but paid yourself under $18/hour.
- If no one bites at $375 after a week, cut the price on a set schedule (e.g., 10% per week), not on emotion.

### Step 5: Rules that follow from the first-principles view
- **Set the floor before you list.** Know the number you'll walk away from. This also makes haggling less stressful.
- **Price for the buyer's alternative,** not for other flippers' listing prices.
- **Track sale price and days-to-sell** for every piece. Over time your own data replaces guesswork (the book's "reality is the ultimate update").
- **Price at sourcing.** If you can't see a sale price at least 2–3× your cash cost once you add your hours, skip the piece.
- **Anchor high, with room to negotiate.** Marketplace buyers expect to haggle. Build that in.

## How the three tools fit together
- **Occam's razor** keeps your *explanations* simple: "they forgot" beats "rival conspiracy."
- **Hanlon's razor** keeps your *attitude* calm: carelessness, not malice. Fix the system, don't resent the person.
- **First principles** keeps your *pricing* tied to real costs and real buyer value, not to whatever other sellers guessed.

**Caveat (from the book):** these are thinking aids, not answers. Some situations really are complex, and some people really are acting in bad faith. Use the razors to set your default, then update when the evidence changes.
