# The Map Is Not the Territory and the Circle of Competence (The Great Mental Models, Vol. 1)

These are the first two of the nine general thinking models in *The Great Mental Models, Volume 1* by Shane Parrish and Rhiannon Beaubien (Farnam Street). The book's larger point is that good thinking depends on having a **variety** of models in your head, not just more of the ones you already use. These two models work as a pair. The first warns that every model you hold is incomplete. The second asks which models you have actually earned the right to rely on.

---

## 1. The map is not the territory

**Core idea:** A map, model, or abstraction is a *reduction* of reality. It is useful because it leaves things out, but that also makes it imperfect, and it can go out of date. The phrase comes from Alfred Korzybski (1931). A map of a city, a financial statement, a scientific theory and a company org chart are all maps. None of them is the thing it describes.

### Key lessons from the book

1. **Reality is the ultimate update.**
   Maps are snapshots, and the territory keeps changing. When your experience contradicts your model, believe the territory and update the map. A model you never revise becomes a liability.

2. **Consider the cartographer.**
   Every map shows its maker's values, priorities, viewpoint and limits: what they chose to measure, what they left out, and why. Before you trust a model, ask who made it, for what purpose, and what they could not see.

3. **Maps can influence territories.**
   A map does not only describe reality. Once people act on it, it can reshape reality. The book's example is Jane Jacobs' critique of city planners who forced real, organic neighbourhoods to fit their tidy planning models, which damaged the living cities they meant to improve. When you impose a model on a complex system, you can break what made it work.

The chapter also includes a sidebar on the **Tragedy of the Commons**, itself a model. It is useful, but it is still a simplification of how shared resources are really managed.

### Practical takeaways
- Use maps, because you cannot navigate without them, but hold them loosely.
- Ask regularly: *Is this map still current? Who drew it? What does it leave out?*
- Watch for the moment the map starts to drive decisions that the territory does not support.

## 2. Circle of competence

**Core idea:** Know the boundary between what you *really* know and what you only *think* you know. Inside your circle you can decide quickly and accurately. Outside it, you are exposed, often without realising it.

### The Lifer vs. the Stranger
The book contrasts two people:
- **The Lifer** has lived in a place (or worked in a field) for years. They know its details, its history, its hidden risks and what usually goes wrong. Their knowledge is deep and tested.
- **The Stranger** has just arrived. They may have read about the place and *feel* informed, but they don't know what they don't know.

Most of us are Strangers in more areas than we would like to admit.

### Ego creates blind spots
The main danger is not ignorance but **ego**. People overestimate their competence, especially in areas next to the ones they really know. Success in one field gives false confidence in another. A circle of competence takes honesty about its edges.

### How to build and maintain your circle (the book's three tools)
1. **Curiosity and a desire to learn.** A circle is built over years through deliberate study and experience. It is never finished, and the world changes under it.
2. **Monitoring.** Keep an honest record of your track record: your decisions, your predictions and how they turned out. A written record gets around the way memory flatters us.
3. **Feedback.** Seek outside feedback, because you are a poor judge of your own boundaries. Others can see your blind spots more easily than you can.

---

## 3. How to operate outside your circle

You cannot stay inside your circle all the time. Life forces decisions in unfamiliar areas: medical choices, legal issues, a new job, a new market. The book gives three strategies:

1. **Learn at least the basics of the area.**
   You won't become an expert, but knowing the fundamentals and the vocabulary helps you ask good questions and spot obvious nonsense. Accept the limits of what you have learned, because a little knowledge can feel like a lot.

2. **Talk to someone whose circle of competence covers that area.**
   Find a real "Lifer." Don't just ask for the answer. Ask how they think about it, which questions they would ask, and what they would check. Watch for incentives: the expert's interests may not match yours (this is the map lesson again: *consider the cartographer*).

3. **Use broad mental models.**
   General thinking tools work in any area. They give you a structure for reasoning even when you lack specialist knowledge. From Volume 1 these include:
   - **First principles thinking:** separate the underlying facts from assumptions and claims.
   - **Second-order thinking:** ask "and then what?"
   - **Probabilistic thinking:** consider base rates and the chance that your own estimates are wrong.
   - **Inversion:** ask what would guarantee a bad outcome, then avoid it.
   - **Occam's and Hanlon's razors:** prefer simple explanations and don't assume malice.

### A short checklist when you're outside your circle
- **Name it:** "This is outside my circle." Saying it out loud slows overconfidence.
- **Check the map:** Whose model am I relying on? Is it current? What are the cartographer's incentives?
- **Learn the basics** well enough to ask good questions.
- **Consult a Lifer** and ask how they reason, not only what they conclude.
- **Apply general models**, especially inversion and second-order thinking.
- **Size the bet to your ignorance:** prefer choices that are small, reversible, or limited on the downside. Most of the damage outside your circle comes from large bets you can't undo. *(This is an application of the book's asymmetry and inversion ideas rather than a quote from it.)*
- **Track the result** (monitoring) so that this decision either widens your circle or shows you where its edge is.

---

## How the two models connect
- Your circle of competence is the set of maps you have **tested against the territory** many times. That is why the Lifer's map is reliable and the Stranger's is not.
- Outside your circle you rely on other people's maps, so "consider the cartographer" matters most there.
- Inside your circle, "reality is the ultimate update" keeps you from going stale. Expertise based on an old map is how competent people get blindsided.

**Caveat (also from the book):** models are aids, not answers. Both of these chapters are reminders to stay humble about the models you hold.
