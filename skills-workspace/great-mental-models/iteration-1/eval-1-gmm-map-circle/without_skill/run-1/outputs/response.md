# The Map Is Not the Territory and the Circle of Competence (*The Great Mental Models, Vol. 1*)

*Answered from memory of Shane Parrish and Rhiannon Beaubien's book (Farnam Street). The ideas below follow the book, but the wording is paraphrased, not quoted.*

---

## 1. The Map Is Not the Territory

**Core idea:** Every model, theory, statistic, plan, org chart or financial statement is a *map*: a simplified picture of something more complex. Simplifying is what makes a map useful. It is also what makes it dangerous when we forget that it leaves things out. The phrase comes from Alfred Korzybski.

### Key lessons

1. **A map is a reduction.** A map that showed everything would be as big as the territory and of no use. A map shows only what its maker thought mattered. So you should always ask what it leaves out.
2. **Reality is the final judge.** If the map and the territory disagree, trust the territory. Results that go against your model are information, not noise.
3. **Maps go out of date.** Territories change and maps stay fixed. A model that worked yesterday may not fit today. Update it from fresh, first-hand observation. *(The book's example: maps and charts that kept showing features that no longer existed, or had never existed.)*
4. **The mapmaker shapes the map.** Every map carries its maker's goals, values, blind spots and incentives. Ask who made it, why, and what they wanted it to show.
5. **The map can change the territory.** People and systems change their behaviour to fit the measure. This is Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. The book also discusses Elinor Ostrom's work, which showed that the simple "tragedy of the commons" model does not describe how real communities often manage shared resources.
6. **Use maps, but hold them loosely.** You cannot work without models, so the answer is not to throw them away. Use them knowing their limits. Pair a map with direct experience of the ground ("go and see") and with people who know the local detail.

**Practical questions to ask of any map:** What does it leave out? When was it last checked against reality? Who made it, and why? Is my behaviour, or other people's, changing what it measures?

## 2. Circle of Competence

**Core idea:** Each of us has areas where we really understand how things work. That is the circle of competence. Inside it we can make good decisions fast. Outside it we are amateurs, and the danger is that we often *don't know we're amateurs*. The idea is closely linked to Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger.

### Key lessons

1. **Knowing the edge of the circle matters more than its size.** A small circle with a clear edge beats a large one with a fuzzy edge. Buffett and Munger put it this way: you don't need to be an expert in everything, only to know where your expertise ends.
2. **Real competence vs. the feeling of it.** Inside your circle you know what you don't know. You can predict how things will go, explain why they went that way, and spot problems early. Outside it, you may *feel* sure without having the knowledge to back it up. That is overconfidence, and it is linked to the Dunning-Kruger effect.
3. **The lifelong resident vs. the tourist.** The book contrasts someone who has lived in a place for decades with someone who has visited briefly. The resident knows the history, the quirks and the dangers. The tourist knows the headlines. Expertise means being a resident.
4. **You build a circle over years, and you have to maintain it.** A circle comes from years of experience, mistakes and feedback. It also shrinks if you neglect it, because the world keeps changing and knowledge goes stale. Keeping it up means:
   - **Curiosity and continuous learning.**
   - **Monitoring.** Keep an honest record of your decisions and results, for example a decision journal, so that memory doesn't flatter you.
   - **Feedback.** Ask trusted people for honest, outside views, because you can't see your own blind spots.
5. **Stay inside it when the stakes are high.** Most serious mistakes happen when people act outside their circle and don't realise it. A famous example is a top expert in one field who loses heavily in another field they took to be "similar".

---

## 3. How to Operate Outside Your Circle

You can't always stay inside your circle: life forces decisions on unfamiliar ground. The book suggests ways to act wisely when you are outside it:

1. **Learn the basics of the area, and admit that's all you have.** Learn enough to know the main variables and the right questions to ask. Don't mistake that for mastery.
2. **Talk to people whose circle covers this area.** Find real experts and ask them more than "what should I do?". Ask *how* they think about it, *what they would look for*, and *what usually goes wrong*.
3. **Check the expert's incentives.** Advice is a map too. Ask what the advisor gains from your choice. Look at their track record, not only their credentials.
4. **Use broad mental models to fill the gaps.** First principles, second-order thinking, inversion and probabilistic thinking work in any field. They help you reason about a field you don't know.
5. **Leave a margin of safety.** Assume your map is incomplete. Make smaller and reversible bets, build in buffers, and avoid choices where one mistake would ruin you.
6. **Invert: decide what to avoid.** When you don't know what works, it is often easier to find and avoid the obvious ways to fail.
7. **Watch your confidence.** If you feel very sure in an area where you have little experience, treat that feeling as a warning sign.
8. **Over time, grow the circle on purpose.** If you will keep working in this area, treat each decision as a chance to learn. Record what you expected and what happened, and ask for feedback.

---

## How the two models connect

Your circle of competence is the area where your *maps are accurate*: you know the territory well enough to see where your models fail. Outside the circle you depend on other people's maps, which you can't check yourself. That is why you need humility, expert input, an eye on incentives, and a margin of safety there.

> **Short version:** Treat every model as a simplification and keep checking it against reality. Know clearly where your real understanding ends. When you step past that edge, slow down, borrow expertise carefully, use general principles, and protect yourself against being wrong.
