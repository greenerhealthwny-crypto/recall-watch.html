# Should You Quit Your Job to Start a Bakery? Using the Nine Models from *The Great Mental Models, Vol. 1*

Shane Parrish's first volume covers nine general thinking tools:

1. The Map Is Not the Territory
2. Circle of Competence
3. First Principles Thinking
4. Thought Experiment
5. Second-Order Thinking
6. Probabilistic Thinking
7. Inversion
8. Occam's Razor
9. Hanlon's Razor

The models aren't meant to give you an answer. They give you different angles on the decision so you can see it more fully. Below, each one is applied to the bakery decision, with questions to answer and things to do. A short synthesis at the end pulls them together.

---

## 1. The Map Is Not the Territory

**The idea:** Every model, plan, or picture of reality is a simplification. It's useful, but it isn't the thing itself, and it leaves out detail.

**Applied to your bakery:**
Your mental picture of "owning a bakery" is a map. It probably shows the smell of fresh bread, regulars who know your name, and creative freedom. The real territory includes alarms at 3:30 a.m., a broken oven on a Saturday, health inspections, staff who don't show up, flour price swings, rent increases, and evenings spent on payroll and bookkeeping.

A business plan or spreadsheet is a map too. Revenue projections are guesses written down as numbers.

**Questions to ask:**
- Where does my picture of bakery life come from: media, Instagram, visits as a customer, or actual work behind the counter?
- Which of my spreadsheet assumptions (foot traffic, average ticket, margins) have I checked against real data?

**Action:** Go and look at the territory. Work shifts at a local bakery, even unpaid on weekends. Talk to three to five bakery owners, including at least one whose bakery closed. Ask them what surprised them most.

## 2. Circle of Competence

**The idea:** Know what you know, and know where that knowledge ends. Inside your circle you have a real edge. Outside it you're more likely to fool yourself.

**Applied to your bakery:**
A successful bakery needs several separate skills:
- **Baking at scale:** consistent product, in volume, on a schedule (not the same as baking well at home)
- **Operations:** sourcing, inventory, waste control, food-safety compliance, equipment
- **Finance:** pricing, cash flow, margins, taxes, loans
- **Marketing and sales:** location choice, branding, social media, wholesale accounts
- **People management:** hiring, training, scheduling, keeping staff

**Questions to ask:**
- Which of these am I actually competent in, with evidence, and which do I only think I'd be fine at?
- Does my current job give me skills that transfer? Someone in accounting, operations, or hospitality starts with a real advantage.

**Action:** List every skill and rate each one honestly: Inside, Edge, or Outside. For anything Outside you can learn it before you quit (a course, a part-time job, a mentor), partner with someone who has it, or hire for it. Building your circle of competence while you still have a paycheck is far cheaper than doing it afterward.

## 3. First Principles Thinking

**The idea:** Break a problem down to its basic truths and reason up from there, instead of copying what others do or reasoning by analogy.

**Applied to your bakery:**
Don't start from "a bakery is a retail storefront with a lease and display cases." Start from the fundamentals:
- What do I actually want? Possibilities include creative work, autonomy, community, leaving a job I dislike, or building something of my own. Each points to a different solution.
- What is a bakery, at its core? It turns cheap inputs (flour, butter, sugar, labour) into higher-value products sold to people who want them, at a price that covers costs plus a profit.
- What has to be true for it to work? Enough customers × enough margin × enough volume, before you run out of money.

Reasoning from those basics opens up other formats:
- Cottage-food or home bakery, where local law allows it
- Farmers' markets and pop-ups
- Wholesale to cafés and restaurants
- Subscription or pre-order bread boxes
- Shared commercial kitchen rental instead of your own lease
- A stall inside an existing business

**Action:** Write down your core goal in one sentence. Then ask whether a full retail storefront is the only way, or even the best way, to reach it. Often the answer is a smaller, cheaper first step.

## 4. Thought Experiment

**The idea:** Imagine scenarios in detail and follow them through to see what happens, especially when real experiments are costly or impossible.

**Applied to your bakery:**
Run a few in your head, or better, on paper:

- **The ordinary Tuesday:** It's 18 months in. Walk through a full day hour by hour. Does it energize you or wear you down?
- **The bad month:** Sales come in 40% below forecast for three months straight. What do you do? How long can you last?
- **The success case:** It's working, and you're busy six days a week. Is that what you wanted, or do you miss having weekends?
- **The regret test (Bezos's regret-minimization framework):** You're 80 and looking back. Which would you regret more, trying and failing or never trying?
- **The "veil" test:** If a friend described your exact situation (savings, skills, family, market), what would you advise them?

**Action:** Write out each scenario in detail. Pay attention to how you feel at each one, because your reactions are data.

## 5. Second-Order Thinking

**The idea:** Look past the immediate result and ask, "And then what?" Most people stop at first-order effects.

**Applied to your bakery:**

| Decision | First-order effect | Second-order effects |
|---|---|---|
| Quit your job | Freedom and time to build | Lost income, health insurance, retirement contributions; strain on relationships; a résumé gap if you return |
| Sign a 5-year lease | You have a location | Fixed cost whether sales come or not; often a personal guarantee, so personal liability |
| Price low to win customers | Faster traffic | Thin margins, customers anchored to low prices, burnout from volume |
| Work 80-hour weeks | The business gets off the ground | Health, family, and passion erode; the thing you loved becomes a chore |
| Take on a loan | Capital to start | Monthly obligations that remove flexibility and raise the stakes of failure |

There are positive second-order effects too: new skills, a network, credibility as a founder, a life that fits your values, and options that wouldn't exist if you'd stayed.

**Action:** For each big decision in your plan (quitting, lease, loan, hiring), write at least two "and then what?" consequences. Watch for decisions that are hard to undo. Leases and personal guarantees are the main ones.

## 6. Probabilistic Thinking

**The idea:** The future is uncertain, so think in likelihoods and ranges rather than certainties. Weight outcomes by how probable they are and how big their consequences would be.

**Applied to your bakery:**
- **Base rates:** Restaurants and food businesses fail at well-known, high rates. A large share of new food-service businesses close within the first few years. Start from that outside view, then adjust for your specific advantages (experience, location, capital, demand already proven) rather than assuming you're the exception.
- **Range of outcomes:** Instead of a single forecast, build three:
  - *Pessimistic:* slow ramp, unexpected costs, 50–60% of projected revenue
  - *Base:* something realistic
  - *Optimistic:* strong word of mouth
- **Fat tails:** A single event can matter enormously: a pandemic, construction blocking your street, a key-equipment failure, a health problem. Ask what protects you from ruin, not just what's most likely.
- **Asymmetry:** What's the worst realistic downside, and can you survive it? A good bet doesn't need to be likely to succeed, but failure must be survivable.

**Action:** Calculate your runway. How many months could you cover your personal and business expenses with zero profit? A common rule of thumb is 12–18 months of personal living expenses saved, separate from startup capital. Then ask: if the pessimistic case happens, would I be okay?

## 7. Inversion

**The idea:** Approach a problem backward. Instead of asking "How do I succeed?", ask "What would guarantee failure?" and then avoid those things. (Jacobi: "Invert, always invert.")

**Applied to your bakery:**
How to make sure your bakery fails:
- Quit with no savings, so every slow week becomes a crisis
- Sign a long, expensive lease in a low-traffic spot before testing demand
- Never test whether people will pay your prices
- Underprice and ignore the cost of your own labour
- Do everything yourself until you burn out
- Ignore cash flow until the account runs dry
- Skip permits, insurance, and food-safety compliance
- Make products you love that customers don't want

Now flip each one into a rule:
- Build a cash cushion before you quit.
- Prove demand cheaply (markets, pre-orders, pop-ups) before committing to fixed costs.
- Price from real costs, including your own wage.
- Plan for help and rest from the start.
- Track cash weekly.
- Get licensing and insurance in order first.
- Let customer feedback shape the menu.

You can also invert the decision itself: "What would make staying in my job the clearly right choice?" If you can't answer that, it tells you something. If you can, it shows what you'd be giving up.

**Action:** Run a pre-mortem. Imagine it's two years from now and the bakery has closed. Write the story of why. The most likely causes become your top risks to reduce.

## 8. Occam's Razor

**The idea:** Among competing explanations, the simplest one, with the fewest assumptions, is usually the best place to start.

**Applied to your bakery:**
1. **Count your assumptions.** If your plan only works when the lease negotiation, a wholesale deal, a social-media hit, and a quick staff hire all go right, it rests on a lot of assumptions. The simpler plan with fewer things that must go right is usually the sturdier one.
2. **Find the simplest explanation for your desire.** Is it really about baking? Or is the simplest explanation that you're unhappy in your current job? If it's the job, a new job, a change of role, or a sabbatical might fix it with much less risk. If baking keeps pulling at you even in a good job, that's a stronger signal.
3. **Use the simplest test.** Before any big plan, the simplest experiment is to sell something. Ten loaves at a market this weekend tells you more than a 40-page business plan.

**Action:** Write the simplest version of your plan that could work and the fewest conditions that must be true. Then go test the single riskiest one.

## 9. Hanlon's Razor

**The idea:** Never attribute to malice what can be adequately explained by stupidity, or more kindly, by ignorance, carelessness, or people having their own priorities.

**Applied to your bakery:**
This model is more about the people around the decision and the journey than the go/no-go call itself:
- **Skeptical friends and family:** When people question your plan, they probably aren't trying to crush your dream. More likely they're worried, don't know the details, or are projecting their own risk tolerance. Treat their doubts as free stress-tests, not attacks.
- **Your employer:** If you negotiate a part-time arrangement or a leave of absence and the answer is a clumsy "no," it's likely policy or inertia, not personal. Don't burn bridges. That job may be your safety net.
- **Once you're running:** Suppliers who deliver late, landlords who are slow, customers who leave a harsh review, and inspectors who are strict are usually overwhelmed or following rules, not out to get you. Assuming good faith keeps you calm and keeps relationships workable, and relationships are the lifeblood of a small local business.
- **Yourself:** When early mistakes happen, and they will, they're the result of inexperience, not proof that you're a fraud. Be as charitable with yourself as with others.

**Action:** Take the hardest criticism you've heard about your plan and assume it was meant kindly and contains some truth. What would you change because of it?

## Putting It All Together

The models point in a consistent direction. Usually, the question isn't "quit or don't quit." It's "how do I turn this into a series of smaller, reversible bets?"

**A staged approach the models support:**

1. **Explore (0–3 months, keep your job):** Work shifts in a real bakery (Map/Territory). Map your skill gaps (Circle of Competence). Clarify your core goal (First Principles).
2. **Test (3–9 months, keep your job):** Sell at markets, take pre-orders, or rent a shared kitchen on weekends. Track real costs, prices, and demand (Occam's Razor: the simplest test). Run your pre-mortem (Inversion).
3. **Prepare:** Build 12–18 months of personal runway plus startup capital. Model pessimistic, base, and optimistic cases (Probabilistic Thinking). Map the second-order effects of the lease, loan, and lost benefits (Second-Order Thinking).
4. **Decide with evidence:** Quit when the tests show real demand, the numbers work in the pessimistic case, and your thought experiments still leave you excited (Thought Experiment). Then reduce reversibility risks: shorter leases, avoiding personal guarantees where possible, and leaving your employer on good terms (Hanlon's Razor).

**A simple decision checklist:**

- [ ] I've worked in a real bakery and still want this. *(Map/Territory)*
- [ ] I've covered my skill gaps by learning, partnering, or hiring. *(Circle of Competence)*
- [ ] I know my core goal, and a bakery is the best way to reach it. *(First Principles)*
- [ ] I've pictured the ordinary Tuesday and the bad month, and I'm still in. *(Thought Experiment)*
- [ ] I've mapped the downstream consequences of quitting, the lease, and any loans. *(Second-Order)*
- [ ] I can survive the pessimistic scenario financially. *(Probabilistic)*
- [ ] I've run a pre-mortem and have a plan for the top failure causes. *(Inversion)*
- [ ] My plan relies on as few assumptions as possible, and I've tested the riskiest one. *(Occam's Razor)*
- [ ] I've taken criticism seriously and kept my relationships and bridges intact. *(Hanlon's Razor)*

If you can tick most of these boxes, quitting looks like a calculated leap rather than a gamble. If you can't, the models aren't saying "never." They're telling you what to do next, while your paycheck still covers the cost of learning.
