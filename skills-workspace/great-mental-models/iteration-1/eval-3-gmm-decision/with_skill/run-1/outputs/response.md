# Should you quit your job to start a bakery? The nine models from *The Great Mental Models, Vol. 1*

Shane Parrish and Rhiannon Beaubien argue that how well you think depends on the models you carry, and that you need **several different ones**, not more of your favourite. So the models below are not nine separate verdicts. Each is a different lens on one decision. I don't know your finances, your skills or your market yet, so I've written each section as questions to answer and tests to run. The answers will make the decision for you.

---

## 1. The map is not the territory

**The idea:** A model is a simplified version of reality. It is useful, but it leaves things out and it can go out of date (Alfred Korzybski, 1931). The book gives three tools: **reality is the ultimate update**, **consider the cartographer**, and **maps can influence territories**. For that last one, it uses Jane Jacobs's account of city planners who forced cities to fit their models.

**Applied to you:** Right now your "bakery" is a map. It is probably a warm picture of mornings, flour, regulars and the smell of bread. The territory is 3 a.m. starts, food-safety inspections, rent, equipment repairs, staff who don't show up, thin margins and a lot of standing.

- **Consider the cartographer.** Where did your picture come from? Instagram, baking shows and friends saying "you should open a shop!" all make maps, and each maker has their own bias and blind spots. Bakery owners who are struggling don't usually post about it.
- **Reality is the ultimate update.** Look for direct contact with the territory before you decide. Work weekend shifts at a real bakery. Sell at a farmers' market. Take catering or wholesale orders from a café. Every week of real experience should redraw your map.
- **Is your map of your job out of date too?** You may be comparing an idealised bakery with the worst version of your current job. Map both sides honestly.

## 2. Circle of competence

**The idea:** The book contrasts the "Lifer", who really knows the terrain, with the "Stranger", who only thinks they do. Ego hides where your circle ends. You build and keep a circle through **curiosity and a desire to learn**, **monitoring** your own track record, and **feedback**. When you have to act outside your circle, the book says to (1) learn at least the basics, (2) talk to someone with a strong circle in that area, and (3) use broad mental models.

**Applied to you:** A bakery is at least three businesses in one:

| Skill area | Inside your circle? |
|---|---|
| Baking well, consistently and at volume | ? |
| Running a business: costing, pricing, cash flow, permits, leases, hiring | ? |
| Selling and marketing: location, foot traffic, social media, repeat customers | ? |

Many people who are excellent home bakers are Strangers in the second and third rows. To work outside your circle: learn the basics (food-cost percentages, break-even, local health codes), **talk to two or three Lifers** (current or former bakery owners, ideally including one whose bakery closed), and lean on the broad models in this list. **Monitoring** means tracking real numbers from your trial sales, not how you feel about them.

## 3. First principles thinking

**The idea:** Separate the underlying facts from the assumptions you've stacked on top of them, then rebuild from what can't be broken down any further. The book offers **Socratic questioning** (clarify, challenge assumptions, look for evidence, consider alternatives, examine consequences, question the original question) and the **Five Whys**.

**Applied to you:** Start by asking why you want this, and keep asking.

- *Why a bakery?* "I love baking and I hate my job."
- *Why do you hate your job?* "No autonomy, and I feel like I'm not making anything real."
- *Why does that matter?* "I want to build something that's mine and to work with my hands."
- …and keep going until you reach the core.

Then **question the original question**. "Quit my job vs. start a bakery" is an either/or built on assumptions. Challenge each one:

- *Do I have to quit first?* You could start on weekends, at markets, from a cottage-food licence or a shared commercial kitchen.
- *Does a bakery have to mean a storefront?* Wholesale to cafés, a subscription bread box, a market stall and a pop-up are all much cheaper versions.
- *Is the real problem my job rather than my career?* A different job might solve the autonomy problem with far less risk.

If the Five Whys end at "I need to make things with my hands and answer to myself", there may be several ways to get that. A full-time storefront bakery is only one of them, and it's the most expensive.

## 4. Thought experiment

**The idea:** "Devices of the imagination used to investigate the nature of things." The book's steps are: ask a question, do background research, form a hypothesis, test it with thought experiments, analyze and conclude, then compare and adjust. Its example is that you can't settle "LeBron James vs. Woody Allen at basketball" or "LeBron James vs. Kevin Durant" by playing them, but you can reason your way through them.

**Applied to you:**

1. **Question:** Can a bakery give me a life I prefer to my current one, at an acceptable risk?
2. **Background research:** local rents, equipment costs, competitors, prices per loaf or pastry, permit requirements, how many units you'd need to sell each day to break even.
3. **Hypothesis:** for example, "If I sell X items a day at an average of $Y, I cover costs plus a modest salary within 18 months."
4. **Test it in your head with specific scenarios:**
   - *It's a February Tuesday, it's raining, and you've sold 40% of what you baked. What do you do with the rest, and how does the month's cash look?*
   - *Your oven dies the week before the holidays. Can you survive that?*
   - *It's two years in and the bakery is "successful", which means a 70-hour week. Is that the life you wanted?*
   - *A chain café opens two doors down. Why would people still come to you?*
5. **Analyze and conclude.** Which scenarios break the plan?
6. **Compare and adjust.** Change the plan (lower rent, wholesale income, a part-time job) until the scenarios are survivable.

## 5. Second-order thinking

**The idea:** Think about the effects of the effects. The book's example is the British bounty on cobras in Delhi: people started breeding cobras to collect it. The book uses second-order thinking for two things: **putting long-term interests ahead of immediate gains**, and **building effective arguments**.

**Applied to you:** For each option, keep asking "and then what?"

- **Quit now.** First order: freedom and relief. Second order: no salary, possibly no health insurance, savings draining every month. Third order: money pressure leads to rushed decisions (a bad lease, cutting quality) and strain on your relationships. A helpful way to frame it, not from the book, is to ask how this looks in 10 minutes, 10 months and 10 years.
- **Stay and test on the side.** First order: you're tired, with less free time. Second order: you collect real demand data and a customer list, and you keep your income. Third order: if you do launch, you launch with customers and evidence instead of hope.
- **Turning a hobby into a living.** First order: you get paid for what you love. Second order: baking becomes the thing you *have* to do at 4 a.m. Will you still love it?
- **The people around you.** A partner, children or dependants will feel the second-order effects too.

**For building arguments:** If you'll need buy-in from a partner, a lender or a landlord, second-order thinking makes your case stronger. "Here's what happens if it goes slowly, and here's my plan for that" persuades people far better than "it'll be great."

## 6. Probabilistic thinking

**The idea:** The future is uncertain, so estimate likelihoods with maths and logic. The book's tools are **Bayesian thinking** (update what you believed before with new evidence), **fat-tailed curves** (the extremes are far bigger than a bell curve suggests) and **asymmetries**, or "metaprobability". That last one asks how likely it is that your estimates are any good. The book notes that investors routinely forecast returns of 20–40% that they rarely achieve.

**Applied to you:**

- **Start with a base rate (your prior).** Independent food and restaurant businesses have a high failure rate in their first few years. Look up figures for your area. Assume you're average until you have evidence that you aren't.
- **Update the way Bayes would.** Evidence that should raise your odds: repeat customers at your market stall, wholesale accounts that reorder, a business plan an experienced owner has checked, low fixed costs, a year of living expenses saved. Evidence that should lower them: sales that come only from friends, sellouts that only happen when you underprice, rent that eats more than a modest share of projected revenue.
- **Fat tails.** The outcomes aren't neatly spread around an average. The downside tail includes a personally guaranteed lease and equipment loans that outlast the business, which could mean years of debt. Ask what the worst realistic case is, not the average one, and whether you can survive it.
- **Asymmetries.** Your forecasts are probably too rosy, just like the investors' 20–40%. People usually overestimate sales and underestimate costs and time. **Make your plan work with revenue cut by a third and costs and timeline raised by half.** If it only works in your optimistic case, it doesn't work.
- **Keep the downside small and capped.** Choose versions of the bakery where the worst outcome is "I lost some savings and learned a lot", not "I'm in debt for a decade."

## 7. Inversion

**The idea:** Come at the problem from the other end. "Invert, always invert" (Carl Jacobi). Avoiding stupidity is easier than seeking brilliance. The book gives two approaches: (1) assume what you want to prove is true or false and work out what else would have to be true, and (2) think about what you want to avoid. It also teaches **Kurt Lewin's force field analysis**.

**Applied to you:**

**How would I make this bakery fail for sure?**
- Quit with less than six months of expenses saved.
- Sign a long lease, with a personal guarantee, in a spot with little foot traffic.
- Never test whether strangers (not friends) will pay my prices.
- Price by gut feel instead of by ingredient, labour and overhead cost.
- Do every job myself until I burn out.
- Ignore permits and food-safety rules.

Now avoid each of those. That list is already most of a business plan.

**What would have to be true for this to work?** There is steady demand at a price that covers costs. You can produce consistently at volume. You can carry 12–24 months of runway. You'll still enjoy it when it's a job. Test each of those separately.

**Force field analysis:**
1. **Problem:** I'm unhappy in my job and want to bake for a living.
2. **Objective:** for example, a bakery that covers my costs plus a living wage within a set time, without risking ruin.
3. **Forces pushing toward it:** passion, skill, a customer base you're building, savings, support from people around you, local demand.
4. **Forces holding it back:** gaps in skills, little capital, high rent, competition, risk to your family's finances, physical strain.
5. **Strategize:** strengthen the forces in step 3 (build a market following, save more, take a business course) and weaken the ones in step 4 (find a partner with business skills, use a shared kitchen to avoid a lease, start with wholesale).

## 8. Occam's razor

**The idea:** The simpler explanation is more likely to be true. Prefer the one with the fewest moving parts. The book adds a caveat: some things really aren't simple.

**Applied to you:**
- **On your motive:** Is "I need to open a bakery to fulfil my calling" the right explanation? A simpler one might be "I'm burnt out at work and baking is where I feel good." If the simpler one fits, the simplest fix (a new job, a sabbatical, a regular weekend market stall) may solve most of the problem.
- **On your plan:** Fewer moving parts means less that can go wrong. "Rent a storefront, renovate, hire three people, run a café as well" has many parts. "Bake 100 loaves for Saturday's market" has very few. Start with the version that has the fewest assumptions that must all hold.
- **The caveat:** Sometimes the honest answer *is* "I really want this, I've tested it, and the numbers work." Don't use the razor to talk yourself out of something real. Use it to cut out stories you don't need.

## 9. Hanlon's razor

**The idea:** Don't attribute to malice what is more easily explained by stupidity. The most likely explanation is usually the one with the least intent behind it. The book uses a road-rage example and warns against "the Devil Fallacy".

**Applied to you:** This model matters in two places.
- **Your decision now:** If part of why you want to leave is that your boss or company seems out to get you, check that. Is it malice, or carelessness, poor systems and overload? If it's the latter, the problem might be fixable, or at least less personal than it feels, and you shouldn't make a life decision out of resentment.
- **Running the bakery later:** Suppliers will be late, inspectors will be picky, landlords will be slow and customers will leave odd reviews. Assuming incompetence or distraction rather than an enemy will keep you calmer and your relationships workable. That matters a lot in a small local business.

## A useful check: necessary vs. sufficient

The book's supporting ideas include **necessity and sufficiency**. Loving baking and being good at it are probably **necessary** for a successful bakery. They are nowhere near **sufficient**. You also need demand, pricing that works, cash flow, a location (or a channel to customers) and enough endurance. Don't treat passion as if it covers the whole list.

The book also warns about **causation vs. correlation**. "My friend opened a bakery and quit her job and is happy" doesn't mean the bakery *caused* the happiness, or that it would do the same for you.

## Putting it together: a sequence for deciding

1. **Map check:** Get into the territory. Work in a real bakery and sell to strangers for 2–3 months.
2. **Circle check:** Name your skill gaps. Talk to 2–3 bakery owners, including one whose business failed.
3. **First principles:** Use the Five Whys to find what you really want, then list every way of getting it, not only a storefront.
4. **Thought experiment:** Run the rainy-Tuesday, broken-oven and "successful but 70 hours a week" scenarios.
5. **Second order:** Play each option forward to 10 months and 10 years, including the effects on the people close to you.
6. **Probabilities:** Start from the local base rate. Cut your revenue forecast by a third and raise costs by half. Make sure the worst case is survivable and capped.
7. **Invert:** Write the "how to fail for sure" list and design your plan to avoid every item. Run the force field analysis.
8. **Razors:** Look for the simplest explanation of your motive and the simplest version of the plan. Don't decide out of resentment.

**One likely conclusion from these models:** The choice usually isn't "quit and open a bakery" or "stay miserable." Often the strongest path is a **staged test**. Keep your income, or cut back to part-time if you can. Sell through low-fixed-cost channels until the evidence (repeat customers, real margins, saved runway) earns you the right to quit. Set your **quit criteria** in advance, for example "N months of sales at $X per week, Y months of expenses saved, and a plan checked by an experienced owner." Then reality, not mood, tells you when to jump.

**The models are aids, not answers.** As the book warns, every map is imperfect. If you tell me your savings runway, your baking and business experience, your local market and who depends on your income, I can go through the riskiest of these models with your actual numbers.
