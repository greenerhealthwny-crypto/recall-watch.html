# Eating for Bipolar Disorder: What Dr. Uma Naidoo's *This Is Your Brain on Food* Says, and What to Watch With Lithium

*(Answered from memory, without the book open. Treat chapter details as approximate, and check anything specific against the book itself.)*

## First, the key point

Naidoo is a Harvard-trained psychiatrist and nutritionist. She says plainly that diet **adds to** psychiatric treatment and does **not replace** it. Bipolar disorder is a serious, lifelong condition, and lithium is one of the best-proven treatments for it. Food changes can help your brother's overall health and maybe his mood stability, but he shouldn't cut back or stop lithium because of anything he reads. Any change to his medication should be planned with his prescriber.

## What the book says about bipolar disorder

The book has a chapter on bipolar disorder and schizophrenia. The evidence there is thinner than in her chapters on depression and anxiety, and she says so. Her main points, as best I recall them:

**Foods and nutrients she encourages**
- **Omega-3 fatty acids** (EPA and DHA from fatty fish such as salmon, sardines, mackerel and anchovies). This is one of her main recommendations for bipolar disorder. Some studies suggest omega-3s help most with the depressive side of the illness. The evidence for preventing mania is weaker.
- **A whole-food, Mediterranean-style diet**: vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts, seeds, olive oil and fish. This supports the gut-brain axis and lowers inflammation. Inflammation is raised in many people with mood disorders.
- **Fiber and fermented foods** (yogurt with live cultures, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, miso) to support a healthy gut microbiome.
- **Folate-rich leafy greens and other B vitamins**, plus enough **magnesium** and **zinc**. These are nutrients involved in making neurotransmitters.
- **N-acetylcysteine (NAC)**. This is an antioxidant supplement that has been studied as an add-on treatment for bipolar depression, with mixed results. Like any supplement, it should only be taken with the doctor's approval.

**Foods she advises limiting**
- **Highly processed foods, refined carbohydrates and added sugar.** These cause swings in blood sugar and drive inflammation.
- **The "Western" pattern of fried foods, processed meats and unhealthy fats.**
- **Too much caffeine.** Caffeine can disrupt sleep, and poor sleep is a known trigger for mania. Very high caffeine intake has been linked to manic symptoms.
- **Alcohol.** It destabilizes mood, disrupts sleep and interacts with many psychiatric medications.
- **Gluten.** She discusses this mostly for schizophrenia, where there is a possible link with celiac disease and gluten sensitivity. It isn't a general rule for bipolar disorder. Cutting out gluten mainly makes sense for someone with diagnosed celiac disease or gluten sensitivity.

**Ketogenic diet.** You may see the keto diet promoted for bipolar disorder because some mood stabilizers are also epilepsy drugs, and keto is used for epilepsy. The evidence is early-stage: case reports and small pilot studies. Naidoo treats it with caution. Keto also has a specific risk with lithium, covered below.

## Lithium: what your brother needs to be careful about

This is the most important part. Lithium has a **narrow therapeutic window**. The dose that helps isn't far from the dose that is toxic. Lithium levels in the blood depend closely on **salt and fluid balance**, so diet really does matter here.

1. **Keep salt intake steady.** The body handles lithium much like sodium.
   - **Cutting salt sharply** (a low-sodium diet, a sudden switch to "clean eating," some detox plans) makes the kidneys hold on to lithium. Levels can then rise into the toxic range.
   - **A sudden big increase in salt** can lower lithium levels and make it work less well.
   - The aim is consistency, not a particular amount.

2. **Stay well hydrated, and be careful with dehydration.** Heat, heavy exercise, sweating, vomiting, diarrhea and fever can all raise lithium levels. He should drink fluids regularly, and call his doctor if he is ill with vomiting or diarrhea.

3. **Keep caffeine steady.** Caffeine increases how much lithium the kidneys clear. If he **suddenly quits** coffee, tea or energy drinks, his lithium level can **rise**. If he suddenly starts drinking a lot more, it can drop. If he wants to cut back, he should do it gradually and tell his prescriber, who may check his levels.

4. **Be careful with low-carb, keto and fasting diets.** These diets cause early fluid and sodium loss, can change kidney handling, and may lead to dehydration. Any of these can shift lithium levels. He should only try them with his psychiatrist involved and with extra blood tests.

5. **Ask before taking supplements and herbal products.** Herbal "water pills" and diuretic products (for example dandelion, parsley or juniper in concentrated form, and "detox" teas) can raise lithium levels. He should also check with his doctor before taking NAC, high-dose omega-3s or anything else.

6. **Watch over-the-counter drugs.** This isn't about food, but it matters a lot. Common pain relievers such as **ibuprofen and naproxen (NSAIDs)** can raise lithium levels. Some blood pressure drugs (ACE inhibitors, ARBs and thiazide diuretics) can too. Acetaminophen is usually the safer choice for pain, but he should check with his pharmacist.

7. **Limit alcohol.** It causes dehydration, destabilizes mood and adds to sedation.

8. **Weight and thyroid.** Lithium can cause weight gain, increased thirst and thyroid changes. A whole-food diet like Naidoo's can help with weight. He should satisfy thirst with water rather than sugary drinks. His regular blood tests (lithium level, kidney function, thyroid) are an essential part of treatment.

**Signs of lithium toxicity that need urgent medical attention:** worsening tremor, nausea, vomiting or diarrhea, confusion, unsteadiness, slurred speech, muscle twitching, or unusual drowsiness.

## Practical summary for your brother

- Moving toward Naidoo's style of eating (fish, vegetables, legumes, olive oil, fermented foods, less sugar and processed food) is reasonable, low-risk and likely good for his overall health.
- Make changes **gradually** and keep **salt, fluids and caffeine steady**.
- **Tell his psychiatrist** before any major diet change (especially low-salt, keto or fasting) or before starting supplements. They may want to check his lithium level afterwards.
- Protect his **sleep and a regular routine**. Regular meals help too. These matter a great deal for preventing mood episodes.
- Never adjust or stop lithium on his own.
