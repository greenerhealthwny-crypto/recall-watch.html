# Sleep and fatigue, as covered in *This Is Your Brain on Food*

*This Is Your Brain on Food* (2020) is by Dr. Uma Naidoo, a Harvard psychiatrist and nutritionist. It has a chapter on insomnia and fatigue. The main idea of that chapter, and of the whole book, is that the gut and the brain are closely linked. What you eat affects your gut bacteria, blood sugar and inflammation, and these in turn affect how well you sleep and how much energy you have.

I'm answering from memory, not from the text in front of me. The overall advice is reliable, but the exact lists and quantities in the book may differ a little.

---

## Foods that support sleep

- **Foods with tryptophan, eaten with a complex carb.** Tryptophan is the building block for serotonin and melatonin. Good sources are turkey, chicken, eggs, fish, dairy, nuts and seeds such as pumpkin and sunflower seeds. A small amount of complex carbohydrate helps tryptophan reach the brain, so turkey on whole-grain bread or oats with nuts works better than protein alone.
- **Complex, low-glycemic carbohydrates.** Whole grains, oats, quinoa, legumes and sweet potato. Refined starches cause blood sugar to spike and then crash, which can wake you up.
- **Foods rich in magnesium.** Leafy greens, nuts and seeds, avocado, legumes and dark chocolate in small amounts. Magnesium helps the nervous system relax.
- **Foods that contain melatonin.** Tart cherries or tart cherry juice is the classic example. Kiwi and some nuts are also mentioned in sleep research.
- **Gut-friendly foods.** Fermented foods with live cultures (yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, miso) and high-fiber prebiotic foods (onions, garlic, leeks, asparagus, bananas, beans). A healthy gut helps the body make serotonin and melatonin.
- **Omega-3 fatty acids and vitamin D.** Fatty fish such as salmon, sardines and mackerel. Low vitamin D is linked to poor sleep, and night-shift workers often get very little sunlight.
- **Calming herbal teas.** Chamomile in particular, and other caffeine-free herbal options.

## Foods that fight fatigue

- **Steady energy from whole foods.** The book's main advice for fatigue is to keep blood sugar stable. Combine complex carbs with protein, healthy fats and fiber instead of reaching for sugar.
- **Vegetables, fruits, berries and spices rich in antioxidants and polyphenols.** Chronic fatigue is linked to inflammation and oxidative stress. Colorful produce, green tea, and turmeric taken with black pepper are typical anti-inflammatory choices in the book.
- **Healthy fats.** Extra-virgin olive oil, nuts, avocado and fatty fish.
- **B vitamins, iron and other micronutrients.** Leafy greens, legumes, eggs, fish and lean meats help prevent the nutrient gaps that cause tiredness.
- **Water.** Even mild dehydration makes fatigue and brain fog worse.
- **Probiotic and prebiotic foods.** The same gut-support advice applies to fatigue as to sleep.

## What to avoid or limit

- **Caffeine, especially late in your "day".** Caffeine stays in the body for hours. On nights, have it only early in your shift and stop several hours before you plan to sleep.
- **Alcohol.** It can make you drowsy at first, but it breaks up sleep later and reduces deep, restorative sleep. Don't use a "nightcap" to come down after a shift.
- **Added sugar and refined carbohydrates.** Sweets, soda, white bread, pastries and most vending-machine snacks. They cause energy spikes and crashes, add to inflammation, and are linked to worse sleep and more fatigue.
- **Processed, fried and fast foods, and unhealthy fats.** These include trans fats and too many omega-6-heavy oils. They promote inflammation, harm gut bacteria and leave you sluggish.
- **Heavy, large, spicy or very fatty meals right before sleep.** These can cause reflux and indigestion, which disrupt sleep.
- **Artificial sweeteners and highly processed "diet" foods.** The book raises concerns about their effect on gut bacteria and mood.

## How to apply this to night shifts

These are my suggestions for applying the book's ideas to a shift schedule. They are not direct quotes from the book.

- **Count your "day" from when you wake up.** Have your main meal before or early in your shift. Eat lighter during the early-morning hours, when digestion is slowest, and have a small sleep-friendly snack before your daytime sleep, such as yogurt with tart cherries or oats with nuts.
- **Bring your own snacks to work.** Nuts, fruit, hummus with vegetables and hard-boiled eggs help you avoid sugary vending-machine food at 3 a.m.
- **Stop caffeine about 6 hours before you plan to sleep.**
- **Ask your doctor about vitamin D.** Night workers are often low because they get so little sunlight.
- **Food is only one part of good sleep.** A dark, cool, quiet room, blackout curtains and a regular sleep window matter just as much.

**When to see a doctor:** If you stay exhausted even with decent sleep, or you can't sleep despite these changes, talk to a doctor. Persistent fatigue can come from thyroid problems, anemia, sleep apnea, depression or shift work sleep disorder, and these are worth checking. The book itself says food works alongside medical care, not instead of it.
