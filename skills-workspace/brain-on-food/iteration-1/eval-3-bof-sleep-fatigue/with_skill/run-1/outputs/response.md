# Eating for Sleep and Energy on Night Shifts (Naidoo, *This Is Your Brain on Food*)

Dr. Uma Naidoo covers this in **Chapter 8, "Insomnia and fatigue"**, and the cheat sheet is on pp. 178–179. Your situation is close to the chapter's patient story. "Dumisani" was a police officer and new mother working night shifts. After she changed her schedule and her diet, her mood improved within about 3 months.

## Why night shifts hit so hard (the gut-brain connection)

Naidoo explains that your gut bacteria run on their own daily cycles. Jet lag and night shifts throw those cycles off, along with your brain's. She also cites a 2014 study in which sleep deprivation changed the levels of 27 metabolites in young men. So poor sleep and fatigue are partly a gut problem as well as a brain problem, and food is one way to help.

## Foods to embrace for sleep

| Category | Foods |
|---|---|
| **Omega-3 fatty acids** | Fatty fish: salmon, mackerel, tuna, herring, sardines |
| **Melatonin-containing foods** | Eggs, fish, milk, rice, barley, rolled oats, grapes, pomegranates, walnuts, sunflower seeds, mustard seeds, flaxseeds, asparagus, broccoli, cucumber |
| **Tryptophan** | Turkey, other meats, chickpeas, **especially when combined with carbohydrates** |
| **L-ornithine** | Meat, poultry, fish, eggs, soybeans, quinoa |
| **Chamomile tea** | A cup as you wind down |
| **Foods with helpful micronutrients** | Lettuce, tart cherry juice, barley grass powder, maca, *Panax* ginseng, lingzhi, ashwagandha powder |

**The tryptophan point.** Naidoo calls it the "tryptophan myth": turkey on its own won't make you sleepy, because little dietary tryptophan reaches the brain. Eaten with a carbohydrate, more of it gets there. Her examples are turkey with mashed potatoes, a whole-grain low-sugar cereal with milk, peanut butter on whole-grain toast, and cheese with whole-wheat crackers.

## Foods to embrace for fatigue

| Category | Foods |
|---|---|
| **Anti-inflammatory foods** | Omega-3s, and colorful vegetables for their polyphenols |
| **Minerals** | Magnesium and zinc |
| **Vitamins** | B1, B5, B6, B12, C, D and E |
| **Capsaicin-rich foods** | Chili peppers: cayenne, serranos, jalapeños |
| **Spices** | Black cumin and turmeric. Elsewhere she recommends a pinch of black pepper with turmeric |

On capsaicin, she cites a study in which about 2.5 mg per meal (about 7.68 mg a day) restored energy balance. A little chili in your meals during a shift may help.

## Foods to avoid

- **Caffeine.** You don't have to cut it out completely. Keep it **under 400 mg a day** and have **none after 3 p.m.**
- **Alcohol.** It can help you fall asleep, but it **disrupts sleep**. A drink to "come down" after a shift is likely to make your sleep worse.

**Applying the 3 p.m. rule to night shifts.** Naidoo wrote that rule for people who sleep at night. The idea behind it is to stop caffeine many hours before you plan to sleep. If you sleep at 8 a.m., your version of "3 p.m." falls around the middle of your shift. Have any coffee early in the shift and stop well before your drive home. *(This adaptation is mine, not hers.)*

She also points to the general pattern that recurs throughout the book: limit the **Western diet** (fried foods, red meat, and high-glycemic carbs such as white bread, white rice and pastries), added **sugar**, and **artificial sweeteners**. These matter for night workers, because sugary vending-machine snacks give quick energy followed by a crash.

## A sample night-shift day built from her lists

These are suggestions put together from the cheat sheet, not a plan printed in the book.

- **Before your shift (your "breakfast"):** eggs with sautéed vegetables and a few jalapeños, or steel-cut or rolled oats with walnuts and flaxseed. Have coffee here if you want it.
- **Mid-shift meal:** salmon or sardines with quinoa or brown rice and colorful vegetables, seasoned with turmeric and black pepper. Pack it at home so you don't fall back on fried food.
- **Shift snacks:** walnuts, sunflower seeds, grapes, pomegranate seeds, cucumber, or hummus with vegetables (chickpeas for tryptophan).
- **Wind-down before daytime sleep:** a small snack that pairs tryptophan with carbs, such as whole-grain toast with peanut butter, or low-sugar cereal with milk. Add chamomile tea or a small glass of tart cherry juice. Skip the alcohol.
- **All shift:** drink water.

Make one or two changes at a time and keep track of how you sleep and feel over a few weeks. Naidoo's patients usually improved over weeks to months, not overnight.

## Safety notes

- **Chamomile:** 1–3 cups a day is generally safe. Avoid it if you take **blood thinners** or have surgery coming up, and check with your doctor if you're pregnant *(book)*.
- **Ginseng, ashwagandha, maca, lingzhi and barley grass:** these are on her list, but talk to your doctor or pharmacist before using them regularly, especially if you take any medication. Some of these herbs interact with blood thinners, blood-pressure or diabetes drugs, thyroid medication, or sedatives *(general knowledge, not from the book)*.
- **Melatonin foods are not melatonin supplements.** Before trying a supplement or any sleep aid, ask your doctor about the dose and timing for a shift schedule.
- **Food supports treatment; it doesn't replace it.** Being exhausted all the time and sleeping badly is worth raising with your doctor. Shift-work sleep disorder, sleep apnea, anemia, thyroid problems and low vitamin D or B12 are common and treatable, and diet alone won't fix them *(general knowledge)*. Naidoo also advises coordinating any diet changes with your treating doctor.
- If the exhaustion comes with persistent low mood or hopelessness, tell your doctor. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text **988** (in the US).
