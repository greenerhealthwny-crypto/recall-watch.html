# Gluten, MSG and Caffeine: What Dr. Uma Naidoo Actually Says (and Where You Might Be Overdoing It)

> General information based on Dr. Naidoo's *This Is Your Brain on Food* and the wider research, not personal medical advice. If you have a diagnosed mental health condition, or a history of disordered eating, make diet changes with a clinician or dietitian.

## Short answer

Naidoo doesn't say gluten, MSG or caffeine "wreck mental health" for everyone. Her approach is **targeted and individual**: cut back on what the evidence links to your symptoms, and test it, rather than banning whole categories. Cutting all three at once, with no symptom-based reason, goes further than her book does. It also makes it impossible to tell which change, if any, helped.

---

## 1. Gluten

**What Naidoo says (as I recall the book):**
- Gluten is clearly a problem for people with **coeliac disease**. Coeliac disease is linked to higher rates of depression, anxiety, "brain fog" and, in some studies, ADHD-like symptoms.
- She also discusses **non-coeliac gluten sensitivity**, where some people report mood or cognitive symptoms that improve when they cut gluten.
- She suggests that people with depression, anxiety or brain fog who suspect gluten **try removing it for a period and watch their symptoms**, ideally with a doctor.
- She does **not** say everyone should go gluten-free. Her bigger target is **refined, ultra-processed carbohydrates** (white bread, pastries, sugary cereals), mainly because of blood sugar and inflammation, not gluten as such.

**Where you might be overdoing it:**
- **Get tested for coeliac disease before you cut gluten.** The blood test needs you to be eating gluten. Once you've cut it, the result may be falsely negative.
- **Gluten-free isn't automatically healthier.** Many gluten-free products are highly processed, lower in fibre and higher in sugar. If you cut gluten, swap in whole grains like quinoa, buckwheat, brown rice and oats certified gluten-free, not gluten-free junk food.
- Whole grains bring fibre that feeds the gut microbiome, which is central to Naidoo's whole gut–brain argument. Losing that fibre can work against your mental health goals.

## 2. MSG (monosodium glutamate)

**What Naidoo says:**
- I'm less certain here. As far as I remember, MSG is **not a major theme** of her book. Her concern about additives is broader: ultra-processed foods, artificial sweeteners (such as aspartame), artificial food colours (especially in the ADHD chapter) and preservatives.
- Glutamate is a natural neurotransmitter and occurs naturally in tomatoes, Parmesan, mushrooms, soy sauce and breast milk. The body handles glutamate from MSG the same way as glutamate from food.

**What the wider evidence says:**
- Controlled studies have **not** reliably confirmed "MSG symptom complex" (headache, flushing). They've found **no good evidence that MSG in normal food amounts harms mental health**. A few people may react to very large doses taken without food.
- Much of MSG's reputation comes from a 1968 anecdote and the cultural bias that followed, not from solid data.

**Where you might be overdoing it:**
- Cutting MSG as a single ingredient adds little. If you want to act on the concern, reduce **ultra-processed and fast food** in general, which does line up with Naidoo's advice. MSG is often in those foods, but it isn't the main reason they're a problem.
- If you think MSG affects you personally, keep a symptom diary rather than avoiding every savoury or Asian food.

## 3. Caffeine

**What Naidoo says:**
- In the **anxiety** chapter she recommends **limiting caffeine**, because it can set off or worsen anxiety, jitteriness and panic in sensitive people and can disrupt sleep. She doesn't require everyone to cut it out entirely.
- For **depression**, she notes that moderate coffee intake is linked in research to **lower** depression risk. Coffee contains polyphenols, and moderate amounts may be protective.
- For **ADHD**, caffeine has a mixed, modest effect. It isn't a treatment, and too much can backfire through poor sleep.
- Her message is about **dose, timing and individual sensitivity**: drink less if it makes you anxious, avoid it later in the day, and watch for sugar-loaded coffee drinks and energy drinks.

**Where you might be overdoing it:**
- **Don't stop suddenly** if you drink a lot of it. Caffeine withdrawal (headaches, irritability, low mood, fatigue, anxiety) can last several days and can feel like your mental health getting worse. Taper over one to two weeks instead.
- If caffeine doesn't make you anxious and you sleep well, moderate coffee or tea (green tea also contains calming L-theanine) may do more good than harm.
- A reasonable middle ground: about 1–2 cups of coffee or tea before noon, then decaf or herbal tea.

## 4. Orthorexia: are you at risk?

Orthorexia nervosa is an unhealthy fixation on eating "pure" or "correct" food. It isn't yet a formal diagnosis in DSM-5, but it's well recognised clinically. It can **damage** mental health through anxiety, social isolation and nutrient deficiencies, which defeats the point of eating for your brain.

Watch for these warning signs:
- Your list of forbidden foods keeps growing, often because of things you read online rather than your own symptoms.
- You feel anxiety, guilt or shame after eating a "bad" food.
- You spend a lot of time researching, planning or worrying about food.
- You avoid social meals, restaurants or travel because you can't control the ingredients.
- You judge other people's eating, or feel morally better for your own.
- You lose weight unintentionally, or have low energy, missed periods or other signs of under-eating.
- Your sense of self-worth depends on eating "perfectly".

**In your situation:** cutting three things at once because you read they "wreck mental health" is a common way into this pattern, especially if anxiety is part of why you're interested in nutrition. Naidoo's own framing helps here. She stresses **adding** brain-supporting foods (vegetables, fibre, fermented foods, omega-3s, herbs and spices, berries, nuts and olive oil) far more than strict elimination, and says flexibility matters.

## 5. A more balanced way to do this

1. **Add before you subtract.** For 2–4 weeks, focus on extra vegetables, legumes, fatty fish, fermented foods, nuts, seeds and berries.
2. **Change one thing at a time.** If you still want to test something, try one change (for example, cutting caffeine to one morning cup) for 2–4 weeks and track mood, sleep and anxiety.
3. **Test for coeliac disease before going gluten-free.** Then, if you want to, do a structured gluten trial and reintroduction.
4. **Don't single out MSG.** Reduce ultra-processed food overall.
5. **Use the 80/20 rule.** Naidoo-style eating is a pattern, not a purity test. Social meals, treats and imperfect days are fine.
6. **Get help if food rules are causing distress.** A registered dietitian, ideally one experienced in eating disorders, or a therapist can help. Food is one tool for mental health alongside sleep, exercise, therapy and medication where needed, not a substitute for them.

**Bottom line:** Naidoo would likely support cutting back on caffeine if it makes you anxious, and a gluten trial if you have symptoms or coeliac risk. She wouldn't support a blanket ban on all three. MSG barely features in her advice. The bigger risk in your plan may be the restriction itself.
