# Gluten, MSG and Caffeine: What Naidoo Actually Says, and Where You Might Be Overdoing It
*Based on Dr. Uma Naidoo's* This Is Your Brain on Food *(2020)*

> This is educational, not medical advice. If you have a diagnosed condition or take medication, talk to your doctor before making big diet changes.

**Short answer:** Naidoo doesn't tell everyone to cut gluten, MSG or caffeine. Each one is a **conditional** recommendation in her book:
- **Gluten:** only for people with celiac disease or gluten sensitivity.
- **MSG and glutamates:** only for PTSD and OCD.
- **Caffeine:** a limit, not a ban.

Cutting all three at once, without a reason that fits you, goes further than she does. And she explicitly warns about the mindset that can drive that kind of change.

---

## 1. Gluten

**What she says.** Gluten is on six of her cheat sheets: anxiety, ADHD, memory, OCD, bipolar disorder and schizophrenia. Every time, it comes with the same condition: avoid it **"if you have celiac disease or non-celiac gluten sensitivity."** For that group, she says to avoid all wheat products, such as bread, pizza, pasta and many alcoholic drinks (e.g. anxiety, p. 79–80; ADHD, p. 112–113).

**What she doesn't say.** Nowhere does she say gluten harms the mental health of people without those conditions. Whole grains are actually on her lists of foods to embrace:
- The **MIND diet** includes whole grains (p. 136).
- Her **myoinositol** list for OCD includes whole-wheat bread (p. 157).
- Her depression list includes whole-grain bread in moderation (p. 54–56).

**Where you might be overdoing it.**
- If you haven't been tested for celiac disease, do that **before** you cut gluten. Testing usually needs gluten in your diet to be accurate *(general knowledge, not from the book)*.
- Going gluten-free without a reason often means losing fiber and B vitamins. You also tend to swap in refined gluten-free products, which are often high-GI. That's the kind of carbohydrate Naidoo wants you to limit.

## 2. MSG and glutamates

**What she says.** "MSG, other glutamates and glutamic acid" appear on **only two** cheat sheets: **PTSD (p. 98)** and **OCD (p. 157–158)**. They aren't on the lists for depression, anxiety, ADHD, sleep or memory.

**The list is much broader than MSG.** It includes many whole, healthy foods:
- fish sauce, oyster sauce, soy sauce, tomato sauce and fermented beans;
- **miso, Parmesan and other cheeses**;
- **mushrooms, spinach, seaweed and tomatoes**;
- high-protein foods such as **meat and seafood**;
- savory snack chips and ready-to-eat meals.

**Her caveat.** Right on that list, Naidoo notes that some of these foods help with other conditions, and that what you need is a **personalized plan**. For example:
- Miso is a probiotic she recommends for depression and anxiety.
- Spinach is a glycine source on her OCD embrace list.
- Seafood provides the omega-3s she recommends for almost everything.

**Where you might be overdoing it.**
- If you don't have PTSD or OCD, a strict glutamate-free diet isn't something she recommends for you.
- Taken literally, a full glutamate cut would remove tomatoes, spinach, mushrooms, fish, meat, cheese and fermented foods. That's most of the **Mediterranean eating pattern** she uses as her default.
- If you do have PTSD or OCD and want to try it, start with the processed sources: chips, ready meals, bottled sauces and added MSG. Those carry little nutritional cost. Work with a clinician before cutting fish, vegetables and fermented foods.

## 3. Caffeine

**What she says.** She never says to cut out all caffeine. Her guidance is to **keep it under 400 mg a day**, and that number comes up again and again:

| Cheat sheet | What she says about caffeine |
|---|---|
| Anxiety (p. 79–80) | Under 400 mg a day |
| ADHD (p. 112–113) | It **can help** ADHD; under 400 mg a day |
| Memory (p. 136–137) | Coffee is **beneficial**; under 400 mg a day |
| Insomnia (p. 178–179) | "You don't have to eliminate it completely." Under 400 mg, and **none after 3 p.m.** |
| Bipolar (p. 205–206) | Under 400 mg a day |
| Libido (p. 228–229) | Coffee is fine; under 400 mg a day |

Coffee and green tea are also on her **polyphenol** list for ADHD.

**Where you might be overdoing it.**
- Cutting caffeine completely removes a source of polyphenols that she counts as helpful.
- For most people, a sensible goal is to stay **under about 400 mg a day** (roughly 3–4 regular cups of coffee) and stop by 3 p.m. *(cup estimate is general knowledge)*.
- If you're a heavy user and want to cut back, taper rather than stopping abruptly. Sudden withdrawal often causes headaches, fatigue and irritability *(general knowledge)*. That fits her advice to make gradual changes, and her alcohol line, "By cutting back slowly, you will help lower anxiety."

## 4. The orthorexia question

You were right to ask about this. Naidoo covers it in her **OCD chapter (ch. 7)**. **Orthorexia nervosa** is an extreme fixation on eating "healthily" or "correctly". She admits the irony of warning about it in a nutrition book. She also describes muscle dysmorphia. Her example is a bodybuilder whose extreme protein and supplement intake took him close to kidney failure. He recovered with balanced eating and therapy. Her advice is to **avoid radical diet changes**, unvetted supplements and extreme intake, and to work with a doctor or nutritionist (p. 157–158).

**Questions to ask yourself.** These are commonly used signs, not a diagnostic test *(general knowledge)*:
- Is the reason for cutting these foods something you've read, rather than symptoms you've noticed or a diagnosis?
- Do you feel anxious, guilty or "contaminated" after eating something off your list?
- Is your list of "safe" foods getting shorter over time?
- Is food planning taking a lot of your time, or getting in the way of eating with friends and family?
- Do you feel morally better or worse depending on how "clean" you ate that day?

If several of these sound familiar, that matters more than gluten or MSG. Consider talking to a doctor, therapist or a dietitian experienced with eating disorders. In the US, the ANAD helpline is one place to start *(general knowledge)*. If you have a history of disordered eating, avoid elimination diets unless you're working with a professional.

## 5. What Naidoo would emphasize instead: add before you subtract

Her cheat sheets are mostly about foods to **embrace**:
- **The Mediterranean pattern:** vegetables, beans, whole grains, olive oil, nuts and fish.
- **Omega-3s:** salmon, sardines, mackerel, herring.
- **Fiber and prebiotics:** beans, oats, berries, onions, garlic, leeks, asparagus.
- **Fermented foods:** yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut, tempeh, miso (unless you have PTSD or OCD and are personalizing your glutamate intake).
- **Polyphenols and spices:** berries, green tea, colorful vegetables, and turmeric with black pepper.

The things she most consistently says to avoid aren't gluten or MSG. They are:
- the **Western diet**: fried and fatty red meat, and refined high-GI carbs;
- **added sugar** and high-fructose corn syrup;
- **fried foods**;
- **trans fats**, and too much omega-6 oil;
- **artificial sweeteners** (aspartame especially).

If you want to change one thing, cutting sugar and ultra-processed food is closer to her core message than a triple elimination.

---

## 6. A balanced way forward

1. **Name your goal.** Is it mood, anxiety, focus, sleep or something else? Start from that chapter's cheat sheet rather than a general "avoid" list.
2. **Gluten:** keep eating whole grains unless you've been tested for celiac disease or have clear symptoms of gluten sensitivity. If you suspect either, see your doctor before cutting gluten.
3. **MSG:** if you don't have PTSD or OCD, there's no need to restrict glutamates. Cutting down on processed snacks and ready meals is reasonable for everyone anyway.
4. **Caffeine:** set a cap (under 400 mg a day, none after 3 p.m.) rather than a ban, and taper slowly if you're cutting down.
5. **Change one thing at a time** and track how you feel over several weeks. That's how Naidoo works with her patients, and it shows you what actually makes a difference.
6. **Remember that food supports treatment; it doesn't replace it.** And treatment is individual. As Naidoo says of the glutamate list, it's about a **personalized plan**.
