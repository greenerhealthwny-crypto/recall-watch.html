# Naval on Luck and Decision-Making (per *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant*)

*Answered from general knowledge of the book (compiled by Eric Jorgenson, 2020). The quotes are close paraphrases unless they are well-known lines.*

## The four kinds of luck

Naval takes this framework from Marc Andreessen, who drew on neuroscientist James Austin's book *Chase, Chance, and Creativity*. Each kind of luck depends more on you than the one before it:

1. **Blind luck.** Pure chance. Something good happens that you had nothing to do with. You can't plan for it.
2. **Luck from hustling (persistence).** You "stir the pot," move around, try many things, and meet many people. More activity creates more collisions with chance. Energy and motion make you luckier.
3. **Luck from preparation (being prepared to notice).** You know your field well enough to spot an opportunity that others would miss. "Chance favors the prepared mind."
4. **Luck from your unique character (luck that finds you).** This is the kind Naval cares about most. You build a reputation, skills, and a way of thinking so distinctive that opportunities come looking for you. His example: if you're the best free-diver in the world, a treasure hunter who needs someone to recover sunken gold will call *you*. That "luck" exists only because of who you became.

Key points around this:
- **"Build your character so luck finds you."** The goal is to become the person whom the right opportunity is drawn to.
- Specific knowledge, accountability (putting your name on things), and reputation are what turn the fourth kind of luck on.
- **Getting rich without getting lucky.** Naval wants wealth to come from a repeatable process, not a lottery ticket: "If you gave me a thousand lives, I'd want to get rich in a thousand of them." Leverage, specific knowledge, and long-term games with long-term people make luck close to deterministic.
- **Compound interest in relationships and reputation.** Play long-term games with honest, high-integrity people, and over time trust compounds into opportunities that look like luck.

## Making decisions

### Judgment is the key skill
- In an age of leverage, **judgment matters most**. One good decision applied through code, media, capital, or labor is multiplied hugely. Leverage magnifies the effect of your judgment, so being slightly more right is worth enormously more.
- Naval defines judgment roughly as **wisdom applied to external problems**: knowing the long-term consequences of your actions.
- Clear thinking comes before good judgment. "Clear thinker" is high praise for him.

### Clear thinking and seeing reality
- **Get rid of ego and identity** to see the world clearly. The more you hold on to fixed opinions and an identity, the less clearly you see. Desires and attachments distort perception.
- **Understand things from first principles and the basics.** Smart people can explain things in simple terms. If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it.
- Read widely and absorb **mental models** (evolution, game theory, compounding, economics, basic math, inversion, Munger's latticework), because they make you think better.
- Beware of **status games and social signaling**. Don't adopt beliefs as part of a "package" to fit in a tribe.

### Practical heuristics
- **"If you can't decide, the answer is no."** When you're unsure about a big decision (a job, a relationship, a place to live, an investment), the default should be no. Modern life offers endless options, so only say yes to clear yeses.
- **Simple heuristic for hard choices:** when two options are close to 50/50, **take the path that is more painful in the short term** (the harder conversation, the harder road). The brain overweights short-term pain, so the more painful option is usually better in the long run.
- **Make big decisions slowly and small decisions fast.** Who you work with, where you live, and who you marry are the most important choices. Spend real time on them.
- **Choose partners, colleagues, and a spouse with care.** Look for intelligence, energy, and above all **integrity**. Someone who has the first two but lacks integrity is dangerous.
- **Long-term games with long-term people.** Choose to play repeated, positive-sum games where compounding works for you.
- **Desire is a contract you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want.** Choose your desires carefully and keep only one at a time, so they don't cloud your decisions.
- **Accept, change, or leave.** In any situation you have three options: change it, accept it, or leave it. Wanting it to change while not acting causes suffering.
- **Avoid decisions driven by envy, anger, or social pressure.** Calm, rational, peaceful minds make better decisions. Meditation and self-awareness help.
- **Think for yourself.** Don't outsource judgment to the crowd. It's often better to be right in a way nobody agrees with than wrong with consensus.
- **Spend more time making the big decisions.** There are roughly three big ones early in life: where you live, who you're with, and what you do.
- **Value your time.** Set a high aspirational hourly rate and don't let small tasks eat up hours better spent on high-leverage decisions.

### Summary
Naval's view of luck and decisions fits together. You can't control blind luck, but you can **move a lot (luck 2), prepare deeply (luck 3), and build a unique character and reputation (luck 4)**. Then, with leverage in hand, the main thing that matters is **clear, ego-free judgment**. That means slow, careful big decisions, defaulting to "no" when unsure, picking the harder short-term path, and playing long-term games with high-integrity people.
