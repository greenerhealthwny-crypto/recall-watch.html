# Naval Ravikant on Happiness (from *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant*)

The Almanack, compiled by Eric Jorgenson, splits Naval's thinking into two halves: **Wealth** and **Happiness**. The happiness half treats happiness as something you can learn and practice, not a reward that shows up once you've achieved things.

## 1. Happiness is a skill and a choice
- Naval says happiness can be **learned, like fitness or nutrition**. You can train it on purpose.
- "Happiness is a choice you make and a skill you develop."
- He calls himself someone who used to be anxious and unhappy and became much more peaceful by working at it. He says people have a happiness "set point" that can be raised over time.

## 2. Happiness is the absence of desire
- His core definition: happiness is **what's there when you remove the sense that something is missing**. It's peace and contentment, not euphoria.
- "Desire is a contract you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want."
- So choose your desires carefully. He suggests keeping **one big desire at a time** and letting the rest go.

## 3. Happiness lives in the present
- Unhappiness mostly comes from the mind running into the past (regret) or the future (anxiety). Happiness comes from being **present**.
- He compares the mind to a tool. It's useful when you need it, but most people let it run nonstop, and that constant internal chatter is a big source of suffering.

## 4. Peace and acceptance
- "Happiness is being satisfied with what you have. Success comes from dissatisfaction." He sees a real tension between the two and says you have to choose and balance them.
- **Peace is happiness at rest**, and happiness is peace in motion.
- Acceptance: for any situation you can change it, accept it, or leave it. Choosing to stay unhappy about it while doing nothing is the worst option.
- Getting what you want doesn't bring lasting happiness. It just switches off one desire until the next one shows up, so **you can't buy happiness with external success**.

## 5. Practical habits and tools
- **Meditation**: he uses "choiceless awareness," where you just watch your thoughts without judging them, sometimes for an hour each morning. He thinks of it as cleaning out the mind's backlog of unresolved thoughts.
- **Physical health first**: exercise every day, sleep, sunlight, good food. "A fit body, a calm mind, a house full of love" can't be bought, only earned.
- **Less ego, less identity**: the less tightly you hold your identity, labels and opinions, the less there is to defend or feel hurt about.
- **Avoid status games**: status competition is zero-sum and endless. Wealth creation is positive-sum.
- **Envy**: you can't trade for just one part of someone's life. You'd have to take all of it, and once you see that, envy fades.
- **Choose people carefully**: spend time with happy, high-integrity people and cut down contact with angry or cynical ones.
- **Cut back on news, social media and too much stimulation.**
- **Gratitude and impermanence**: keeping in mind that life is short and you'll die one day makes things matter less and helps you appreciate them more.
- **Habits over goals**: happiness comes from many small daily choices, like not complaining, being honest and judging less.

## 6. Saving yourself
- "Happiness, love, and passion aren't things you find — they're choices you make."
- Reading, especially philosophy (Stoicism, Buddhism, writers like Krishnamurti and Osho), is how he says he rebuilt his own mental models.
- Meaning is personal, and you create it yourself. He takes a mostly secular, materialist view: the universe has no built-in purpose, so you're free to decide how to live, and that freedom is an argument for peace and not for striving.
- In the end he puts **freedom and inner peace above outside achievement**.

## In one sentence
For Naval, happiness is a calm, present state of mind you reach mainly by wanting less, accepting reality, staying healthy and practicing habits like meditation. You don't get it by chasing achievements or status.
